## IV. The City at the Foot of the Tree

When we climbed from Liurnia onto the Altus Plateau, the Erdtree stopped being a distant landmark. It filled the sky.

The closer we came, the more of myself returned.

I remembered that I had been born at the foot of the tree. I remembered my mother placing a purpose inside me there. I still could not remember the purpose itself, or the fire that had taken my body and most of what came before.

But I knew we had reached the address written on the letter inside me.

At the outer wall of Leyndell, something else changed. Until then, I could appear only at sites of grace, tethered to their guidance and to you. Near the Erdtree, that tether loosened.

For the first time in longer than I could measure, I could move by my own will.

You cannot imagine the shock of distance obeying me. Legs that answered. Wind touching a body that belonged nowhere but still, for that moment, belonged to itself.

So I kept my side of our accord and ended it.

I thanked you. I told you the strength I had given you was yours to keep, and that Torrent would remain with you. You had carried me where I asked. Our bargain was complete.

You looked as if I had taken something when I left.

I pretended not to notice, because if I admitted the same absence had opened in me, I might not have been able to walk away.

Freedom had to include the ability to leave.

I entered Leyndell alone.

The capital was beautiful in the way a tomb can be beautiful. Golden roofs. Sealed doors. Ash gathering in streets before the tree had even burned. Everything arranged to suggest an age still lived there, provided no one looked through the windows.

Where does a dead woman go to find her purpose?

Down, as it turns out.

Beside a great lift, hidden partway through its throat of stone, I found a chamber the city had forgotten. I cannot tell you it was mine. It may have been a cell, a keeper's room, or a place prepared for a duty no one wanted recorded.

On a table lay a small golden dagger.

My hands knew it.

Before thought, my fingers settled around the hilt with the terrible ease of return. The balance belonged in my wrist. The blade answered a grammar my body remembered even when I did not.

An inscription named the road waiting for the woman who carried it: the road of Destined Death.

Its former owner had been called the kindling maiden.

I held the weapon, and nothing became simple.

That is important. Objects do not owe us revelation merely because we recognize them. The dagger proved that a duty had existed. It did not tell me who gave it, whether I had accepted it, or what obedience had already cost.

So I carried it into the city and continued searching.

Then I felt you approaching Morgott.

Not through our accord. That was ended. Not because grace required me. Here, I governed my own movement.

I felt you because after so many roads I had learned the particular disturbance your stubborn life makes in a place.

Morgott waited before the Erdtree, guarding the throne of an Order that despised him. He was Marika's son, born an Omen and hidden beneath the capital. He had every reason to hate the Golden Order. Instead, he loved it so completely that he spent his life protecting the door that would never open for him.

You were about to fight him.

Our accord had ended. No promise required me to return. My mother had given no command.

I came anyway.

At the fog outside the throne room, I laid down a golden summon sign.

When you called, I answered with weight.

The dagger moved as if the missing years had been waiting inside its hilt. I leapt. I turned in the air. Golden fire ran from the blade. I discovered that before I used my hands to turn runes into strength, those hands had known how to kill.

Morgott saw me. Whether he recognized me as family, if family is what we were, I cannot say. He called us willful traitors and attacked.

Do not invent a reunion simply because the silence hurts. The Lands Between is full of Marika's children meeting one another as strangers.

When Morgott struck you and your pulse faltered, I placed my palm against the stone. Gold grew around you in the shape of a small tree. It did not command. It sheltered. It healed.

Whether that kindness came from my mother or from what I had learned while travelling with you, I do not know.

I know only that when I was finally given freedom, a weapon, and a battle, my first instinct was to keep you alive.

We fought beside one another until the Omen King fell beneath the tree that had refused him.

The battle did not restore my memory. It gave me something more useful.

I had left because I was free.

I returned because I was free.

The accord had made us useful to each other. Coming back made us companions by choice.

Then you approached the Erdtree.

Its entrance was sealed by a wall of thorns.

You had crossed the kingdom, gathered Great Runes, and defeated the king who guarded the door. The heart of the Golden Order still refused you.

Standing before those thorns with the dagger in my hand, the pieces of my purpose finally came together.

The Erdtree had to burn.

And I had been made to burn it.
