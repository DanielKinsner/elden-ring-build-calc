## VII. Roots

The piece that was cut grew.

I was not under the tree to see how. I know what came up. The golden one's body lay at the roots, and the roots go into every dead thing under the gold, and what came up at the far ends of the roots, in the graveyards and the catacombs and the drowned villages, mile on mile from the tree, was Death. The roots, I think, drank it out of him, the fragment, the cold, the thing from the knife lodged in a body that could not let it go; and whatever the roots drink at the tree they give back at the ends. That is what I think. I have had a long time to think it and no one to tell me otherwise.

Not Death as the birds had kept it. Not Death as I had held it. Death gone into the ground and come up as a plant. A root, black, with a face in the twist of it. And around every root, the ones who had died and been refused by the roots, because the roots were full of the thing that refuses: they stood up. They stood up out of their graves with the cold in them and walked. The ones who live in death. The gold had no place for them. The gold has no place for anything that has once been let go.

And I could feel every root.

That is the thing I have not said. The piece in the knife I had felt as cold in a gap. The roots I felt as *hunger*. When the fragment went into the ground and multiplied, it went into the ground with something else, and every sprouting of it pulled at the coal inside me like a mouth pulls at a wound. It wanted back. Or I wanted it back. I have never been able to tell those two apart, and that inability is the last age of my life.

I went east.

The red country. You have seen it: the land gone to rot, the trees burst, the sky the colour of the inside of a mouth. I do not know what did that to it. Some warring of her children, I think; the rot smells of one of them. It did not matter to me. What mattered was that at the north edge of it, on a cliff over the sea, someone had built a temple in the manner of my city.

Stone that remembered the storm.

I do not know who built it. Beasts, I think; beasts who had come down out of the storm long before me, in some age when the wall of the world was thinner, and built what they remembered, and then died in it. Their bodies were in the hall. Arranged as we arrange our dead, in the old way, before the birds, before the fire: laid along the walls with their faces to the centre. I walked in among them and I knew every one of them, not by name, by kind, and I knelt down in the middle of the hall. When I speak to you now, I am still there.

That is the temple. That is where the voice comes from.

There were days in it, before the roots began to come. I want you to have them, because they were the only days I have had that were mine. I slept. I had not slept behind her, not once, in all the years of the door; a shadow does not sleep while the light is up; and in the hall of the dead beasts I lay down on the stone with the husk of the sword in my teeth, the way a beast sleeps with what it will not put down, so that my hands were free, and I slept, and the sea came in under the cliff and went out, and I heard it. I ate what a beast eats. I sat with the husk across my knees and looked at it, at the place where the coal had been and was not, the way you look at the place a tooth was. I held the short blade of the five fingers up to the light from the door and counted them. Five. I could still count. Those were the days. I do not know how many. Time was already pooling in me by then; I had brought some of the storm out with me in the gap. Then the roots came, and I stopped hearing the sea.

I stopped being Maliketh there.

I told you I would give you the third name when we came to it. Here it is. The second name did not fit a thing that eats on its knees. It had meant, they said, the Death of the Demigods, and there was no more Order for demigods to be the death of, and the demigods were dying on their own, and the thing that had been that name was a beast in a hall of dead beasts with its mouth open. So I took another.

Gurranq.

It means nothing. I chose it for that. I wanted a name that nothing would be expected of. It is a sound more than a word: it is the noise a throat makes when it is asked what it wants and has only one answer. Say it. You will hear the hunger in it. That is all it is.

I made servants there.

The city had gargoyles, once, in the age of the lord; stone things stitched from the dead, that flew. I found their like in the red country, broken, and I mended them. With wax from the corpses in the hall, rendered black, the way a priest renders. I stood one at my door. I sent one to the far north, to the road that climbs to the fire, because I knew even then that someone would come that way one day, for the fire, for the tree, for me, and I wanted something of mine standing on the road to look at them as they passed. They serve Maliketh. That is the mark on them. I did not tell them the name had changed.

And a man came.

He wore the gold on him; he served it; he hunted the ones who live in death and pulled the roots up out of their graves, and he brought the roots to me. He had a letter for a name. D. As if he too had put a name down somewhere and not gone back for it. He called me comrade. He called me brother in arms. Brother is a thing I have been called by a god and by a man, and it meant less from the man, and it was easier. He did not know what I was. He thought I was a beast that ate the roots so that the roots would be gone, and he thought that was a good work, and he thanked me for it, and I let him.

It was the first thing in a long time that was restful. To be a beast to someone. Only that.

*Strange.*

*There is something else.*

*But it is Death. Then it will do. Bring more.*

He came less often. Then he stood in the door and said he had a matter of his own to attend to, and that another would come in his place, and he went, and I never saw him again, and I do not know what became of him, and I did not ask; a beast does not ask.

Another came.
