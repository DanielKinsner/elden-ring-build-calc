## II. The Water Above

Somewhere in the sanctuary a drip has started. Thou wouldst not hear it. I hear it because I was raised by that sound, and a man does not forget the voice that raised him even when the voice is water.

Everything came from above. That is the whole geography of my childhood and I will not pretend it had more than one direction. Water came from above; it ran down the walls and gathered in the channels and went on somewhere lower, and we drank from it before it went. Bread came from above, when it came; there were hatches, and hands I never saw, and I have thought since that someone in the capital had the duty of feeding the things under the capital and did it the way one feeds a dog one is ashamed of owning: regularly, without looking. Light came from above, at one hour, through shafts too high to climb. And the sound of the city came from above, through every grate and drain and crack in the stone, so that I knew the capital as a deaf man knows a room by the vibrations in the floor: feet, bells, carts, a great many voices saying the same words at the same time, which I learned later was called prayer.

We were not alone down there. The dark had other Omen in it, and understand what that meant before I say a word about my brother, because it is easy to picture two royal children in a cell and it was not that. It was a city under the city. The channels went for miles. There were halls down there that had been something else once, before the water took them, and in those halls there were dozens of us, of every age, horned and hornless, and most of them had never had a word said to them that was not a curse. They bellowed. It is the word the nobles used, later, when they sent to me to complain about the noise from below, and I let them use it because it was accurate. A horned man who has never been spoken to does not speak. He makes the sound the throat makes when the throat has never been taught anything, and it goes up the drains, and the city above puts its hands over its ears and calls it an affront. I grew up inside that sound. It was the only choir I had.

Two of us did not bellow. Two of us had a shackle each and a line we could not cross and, on the far side of the line, a stair, and above the stair a mother. I do not know what that does to a child; I know what it did to two.

He listened down.

I say it that way because it is the truest way I have to say it and because I promised thee order. Where the channels went lower, into the parts of the dark that even the other Omen avoided, where the water stopped moving and began to stand, and stink, and hold things in it, my brother went. Not at once. He was an infant, as I was, and then he was a child, as I was, and we were the same child for longer than I can now believe; we had one face between us and one set of horns coming in at the same rate and one shackle each with the same measure, and for years I could not have told thee which of us was which except that one of us was always looking up the shaft when the light came and the other was always looking at the water on the floor to see what the light did to it. That was the whole difference, at first. It was enough.

I listened up.

I could not help it and I have never claimed it as a virtue. The grates were above me, and the gold came through them, and the words came through them, and a child listens to where the words are. I learned the city before I learned my own name. I learned that it had a Queen and that the Queen had a lord and that the lord's name was shouted by soldiers in a way that made the stone shiver, and that there was a tree, and that the tree was the reason for everything, because every prayer I could make out through the drains ended in it. I did not know what a tree was. There is nothing under the capital that grows, except the things that grow on the dead. I built the tree out of the word for it and the light that came down the shafts, and what I built was a thing made of gold that stood over the city and was the reason the city was allowed to exist. I was not far wrong. I was only wrong about which of us it was allowing.

The first gold I ever saw was on water.

I will be exact, because thou hast seen the tree from every side and I saw it from one, and I will not have thee think I saw less. There was a shaft, wider than the rest, that came down somewhere near the middle of that dark, and at one hour the light that came through it reached the floor, and the floor there was wet, and the light lay on the water and moved. I was small enough that I could sit inside the shape of it. My brother would not. He said, when he had words, that it was cold, and it was; the light from that tree is not warm, whatever the city says about it, it is only bright. I sat in it anyway. I sat in it every day it came, for years, and I did not know what I was doing, and I know now, and I am going to tell thee, because it is the first thing on the scale.

I was practicing.

Not for anything. There was nothing to practice for; a shackled child in a drain has no prospects. I was practicing the posture of a thing that is looked at by the light. I did not have the word loved. I did not have the word seen. I had a warmth that was not warmth falling on the top of my head where the horns were coming in, and I had the whole city above me saying the name of the thing that sent it, and I sat there and let it fall on me and I did not ask it for anything, because I had already understood, at whatever age one understands such things, that it was not going to answer. Thou hast heard what the Order says of me. That I was not loved and loved regardless. The Order is not wrong; it is only late, as it is with everything about me. I did not decide, later, when I was a man with a staff, to love a thing that would not love me. I began in a shaft of borrowed light with my brother in the dark behind me telling me it was cold, and I have never once, from that day to this floor, been anywhere else.

Let me give thee the rest of the geography, since I have started, and since thou hast walked it now and will know whether I lie.

The channels ran under the whole city. There were pipes a man could stand in and pipes a rat could not, and I have since learned which streets they ran beneath and I could tell thee, if it would help thee, that the sound of the bells from the great cathedral came down loudest near a certain junction and the songs from the taverns near another. There was a chapel down there. I did not know the word; I knew a hall with a shape at one end that the other Omen would not go near, and I know now that it was a cathedral built for the forsaken, by someone who thought the forsaken should have one, and that beneath it the water went lower still into a place that no one, not the Omen, not the rats, not my brother in the first years, ever came back from. There was a sound from down there. Not a bellow. A kind of laughing that was not laughing. The merchants who had been sealed in, when they still had voices, said their lord was at the bottom, and that he was frenzied, and they said it the way the city said tree. I kept away from it. I have kept away from it all my life. I sealed it, at the end, and I will tell thee with what.

And there was the stair. Always. A child who cannot go somewhere knows exactly where it is. I could have walked to the bottom of that stair blind, with the water at my knees, and did, and stood at the line where the gold on my wrist went tight, and listened up. My brother did not come to the stair. He said there was nothing up there for either of us, and he said it the way one says a thing one has decided rather than a thing one has found out, and he was right, and being right about it did not make him stand there with me.

I will tell thee one more thing about the water and then I will go up a floor, to the word.

The water came from above and it carried what the city was done with, and one of the things the city was done with was speech. Not only the sounds through the drains; things. Scraps of things. A page torn from a ledger of grain. A soldier's tally-stick with the notches still on it. Once, a whole prayer-sheet, water-soft, from the cathedral, with the gold worn off it in the shape of the hands that had held it. I fished these out. My brother thought it was foolish and said so, and I have never had an answer for him except that the light came down the shaft and the words came down the drain and I wanted, with a want I did not have a name for, to know what the light was saying. I taught myself. There was no one to teach me. The Omen who bellowed could not, and my brother would not, and the hands that fed us never once said a word. I learned the shapes of the letters from what the capital threw away and I learned the sounds of them from what the capital said over my head, and I put the two together on my own, on wet stone, over years, and that is why I speak as I do. I speak like a city's refuse. I speak the way a man speaks who learned every word from the outside of a grate. The Erdbishops, when they came to know me, thought my speech a little grand for a soldier and could not have said why. It was because I had never heard a word said kindly, or casually, or to me. I had only ever heard them declared.

My brother learned the dark. I learned the drain.
