## XIII. The Undercapital

The smell comes up through the floor. It always has. The Order builds its throne room on the roots of the tree and the roots go down into the same wet dark as everything else, and there is no incense in the world that will keep the Undercapital out of the room above it. I ruled in that smell for an age. I was born in it. I want to tell thee now what I did about it, which is the heaviest thing on the scale, and I want to tell it without ornament, because it does not deserve any.

I did nothing.

Hear the whole of it before thou weighest it. The city under the city did not stop when I came up. The halls were still there and the channels and the standing water, and in them were still the horned ones, the fiendlings the nobles called them, who had never lost a horn, and the hornless ones who had, and they bellowed, and the sound went up the drains into the capital exactly as it had when I was the thing making it. And the capital complained. It complained to the bishops and the bishops complained to the lord, and I was the lord, veiled, in this room, and the complaints came to me in the mouths of men in fine cloth who could not have borne to be in the same street as what they were complaining of. I remember one. A viscount of an old house, who wished it known that the noise from the repository of waste below was a vile and ceaseless affront to the sanctity of the tree, and that his family had long thought the fiendlings should be expunged, and that he hoped to be received by the Lord of Grace so that he might explain that the idea had been his. I received him. I stood behind the veil and heard him, and when he was done I said the two words a veiled lord says to such men, which are see to it, and he bowed and went away happy to have been seen by me.

See to it. I said that. I want thee to have the exact words, because I have judged men on fewer.

The hunters went on. The perfumers' guild had done its work under the city since before I was born and it did not need a lord's order to go on doing it; it needed only a lord who did not say stop. I did not say stop. I did not fund them, and I did not send them, and I did not once in all the years put my name to a warrant that sent a cleaver made of children's horns down a stair to cut more; and that is true, and it is nothing. A keeper of walls who does not stop a thing inside the walls has done the thing. I knew that. I knew it in the language of the Order, which says that all things converge and that a lord is answerable for what converges under him, and I knew it in the older language, the one from the drains, which says only that the hands that do not come are also hands.

Why. Thou wilt want the why, and I will give thee the two I have, and thou wilt see that they do not add up to one.

The first is the veil. To have gone down that stair as the lord and come up with the fiendlings behind me would have been to lift the hood in the square. The city's peace was a tall old man in gold who did not show his face; the moment the face was a horned one the peace was over, and the walls were still under assault, and the tree still needed keeping, and I told myself that I could not spend the city on the sewer. I told myself that for an age. It is true. A thing can be true and be an excuse; I have been a judge and I know the taste.

The second is the one I have never said. I did not want them near me.

I had put my blood in a stick. I had recanted the thing under my horns and shut it in wood and walked on it, and every day of my reign I stood in this room in the hood with the old shape under it and told the shape it was not mine. And below me, through the floor, the old shape was bellowing. Dozens of it. Hundreds; I never knew. It was my own blood in the throats of things that had never been taught a word, and it came up through the roots of the tree into the room where I was pretending, and I could not have them near me because they were the thing I had sealed, loose. Thou hast heard me say my brother went down and I went up. Hear now what going up meant. It meant that everything I had come from was under my feet, and I stood on it, and I did not look down, because I had learned from a man on a horse that one does not.

There was a defiler in the gaol.

I will tell thee about him because thou hast met him, or will, and because he is the mirror the Order put under me without knowing it had. A man, not an Omen, who had wished to be one. Who had made himself armor in the shape of a horned thing with its horns cut off, because that was how he wished to see himself, and who went among the dead of the city and put into their corpses a curse that grew horns on them, a pox, tender, with omen horns, so that they could not go back to the tree, so that they and their children and their children's children would be born outside the circle. He wanted to make the whole world Omen. He wanted to defile the Order until defilement was all there was and therefore nothing. His body lay in the sewer gaol under the capital for the whole of my reign, chained. I did not put him there. I want that exact. Whoever did, did it in my name, because everything under the city was done in my name once the city had a name for the thing that ruled it, and I let the name be used. I did not order it. I did not countermand it. I never went down to look at him. I did not need to. I knew what a man who wanted to be an Omen looked like; I had been one, in a shaft of light, wanting the opposite thing just as hard.

And under him, the bottom.

I have told thee there was a sound down there when I was a child, a laughing that was not laughing, and that the sealed merchants said their lord was at the bottom and that he was frenzied. I did not go down to it as king either. I did a thing instead that I will tell thee about when I tell thee about the barbs, because it belongs with them; I will say here only that there was a fire under the city, of a kind the Order has no word for except madness, and I knew it was there, and I stood on the floor above it in the hood and kept the tree between it and the sky.

So. Hear the sum, since I have never let anyone else say it.

There was a city above and a city below, and I had come out of the one and ruled the other, and for the whole of my reign the one went on making the other. Horned infants in the hovels of the capital were still taken to the knife. The ones who lived were still put down the stair. The ones down the stair still bellowed, and the ones above still complained, and the perfumers still went down with their lanterns and their cleavers, and the images with the prayer under them were still carved, and still came down the drain eventually, like everything else. I could have stopped it. Not all of it; the law was the Queen's and the city was hers and I was a thing standing in front of a chair. But some of it. A warrant. A word to the bishops. A night with the hood off. I did not, and the reason I have given thee is the veil, and the reason under the reason is that I could not bear to be near the thing I had shut in a stick, and under that there is no reason at all, only a man standing on a floor with the bellowing coming up through it, saying to a viscount: see to it.

There is a bell in this city that rings for the tree's hours. I had it rung, as king; it was one of the few things I ordered outright, because the tree's hours were the hours I had kept in the dark, and I wanted the whole city to keep them. It hangs above the great cathedral. And under the cathedral, some way down, there is a hall with a shape at one end that the horned ones would not go near, and the bell's sound goes down there too, as everything does. I have thought, since, on this floor, that the whole of my reign can be put in the picture of a bell rung above a cellar so that the things in the cellar will know the hour, by a man who was raised in the cellar and could not go down.

I did not order it. I did not countermand it. Set those two sentences on a scale and tell me which is heavier; I never could.
