## VI. The Tree From Beneath

There are roots under this floor. I can feel them the way one feels a sleeper's breathing through a shared wall. I have never been able to tell whether they are aware of me, and I have stopped asking, and I am going to tell thee now how I came to stop asking, because it is the hinge of everything after, and because thou hast the right to know what a man was made of before thou killedst him.

The Order has one teaching. It has a great many doctrines, and I have read them all, and the fundamentalists have made a scholarship of them with laws and causes and regressions, and under all of it there is one teaching, and I had it whole before I could read. I had it from the drains. It is this: all things return to the gold. Everything the city said, every prayer, every bell, every shouted name, every sentence of scorn about the noise from below, came down to me carrying the same shape, and the shape was a circle, and the circle was the tree, and the teaching was that whatever was not in the circle was not. Not wrong. Not cursed, even, though they used the word. Simply not. Outside the circle there was no ground to stand on. That is what forsaken means in the Order's mouth, and I will come to the word; for now, see a child working out, from the outside of a grate, that the entire world above him was built on the proposition that he did not exist.

The Queen said it once in words. I heard the words later, as king, from a church on a high place where they are kept, and when I heard them I knew I had heard their shape before.

The Erdtree governs all. The choice is thine. Become one with the Order. Or divest thyself of it. To wallow at the fringes; a powerless upstart.

A choice. She called it a choice. And thou wilt say, as I said, lying under the grate with the shape of those words coming through the stone before I had the words themselves: what choice. A thing with horns cannot become one with an Order whose first act was to put the horned thing under the ground. The circle does not have a door on the underside. And I lay there with my brother's breathing beside me in the dark and the light on the water at the far end of the channel and I thought about it for what I believe was years, because that is how long things take when one has nothing else, and I came to the only answer that was ever going to let me stand up.

If all things yearn to converge on the gold, then a horned thing that yearns is not a contradiction of the Order. It is the Order's proof.

Hear it again; I have said it to myself ten thousand times, and I would have thee hear it as I had it, not as the Order would have it. The teaching says that everything wants to come back to the tree. Everything. If that is so, then the Omen under the city, the thing the tree cannot look at, must also want it, must want it most, and if such a thing could be made to want it and keep wanting it and never once be turned away by the wanting going unanswered, then the teaching would be true in the hardest case it had, and the tree would have, at its root, in the dark, the one believer whose belief could not be explained by anything the tree had given him. I did not think of it as loving the tree. I thought of it as being the case that the Order needed and did not know it needed. I was going to be the sentence at the bottom of the page that made the rest of the page true.

Thou seest what that is. I see it now, on the floor. It is a child in a drain deciding to be indispensable to the thing that threw him away. Say it. I have said it. It does not change what I did with it.

I had no grace. I want that exact. The Order's word for the light's guidance is grace, and it is a thing the tree gives, a small gold that dwells in the chosen and leads them, and my father had it and lost it, and my brother on the horse had it, and every soldier who shouted in the square had a little of it, and I had none. I sat in the shaft of light every day and the light fell on me and nothing in it moved toward me. Nothing pointed. The gold that led men to the tree lay on the top of my head like water and ran off. I was born without it; the Order has a word for that and it put the word on the memory of me, and it is the truest word it ever used: graceless. Not cursed. Graceless. A thing the light falls on and does not enter.

And so the love, when it came, could not have come from the light. There was no channel for it to come by. Hold that beside the Order's account of me, which thou hast read on the stone above this room, and which says that I was not loved and loved regardless. The Order thinks that is the wonder. It is not. Any dog will love what feeds it, and the tree did not feed me; the hands did that, and I did not love the hands. The wonder, if there is one, is smaller and harder. It is that a thing can begin to love in a place where there is nothing to love it back and no light to lead it, from nothing, out of the plain fact of having been thrown into the same dark as the roots. I did not choose the tree because it was good to me. I did not choose it because it was beautiful; I had never seen it. I chose it because it was there, above me, the only thing above me that was not a hand, and because the alternative was my brother's face turned toward the standing water, and I had seen what the water did to a face.

Here is what the choosing looked like. It did not look like anything. That is the whole difficulty of telling it.

I sat in the light. I had done that since I was an infant. But I began, in those years, to do a thing that I have never had a name for and that the Order's records would call by a word that is too big for it. I began to keep the light's hours. I knew when it came and I was there before it. I knew when it went and I stayed after. I learned the sounds of the prayers well enough to say them, silently, with my mouth shut, in the dark, in the shape of the mouths above, and I said them not to be heard, because I had understood by then that I would not be, but in order to have said them. I found the scraps of the Order's writing in the water and I dried them and read them and put them in a crack in the stone where the water would not come, and that was the first archive I ever kept, and the last one I ever kept for myself. I took the two floors of the word they had for me and I set the lower floor aside and kept the upper, and told myself that a warning is a kind of service. And when my brother said, as he said more and more, that I was building a church in a sewer to a god who had built the sewer, I did not answer, because he was right, and I had already told thee what I do when he is right.

My father took a lion on his back to hold down the thing in him that would not stop, and I loved the lion before the man. Here is where that comes due.

There was a thing in me that would not stop. It was in my brother too, and in him it was beginning to show, and I will tell thee about that in its place, because it deserves a place of its own. In me it showed as heat. Not fire yet. Heat, in the blood, at the base of the horns and along the tail, that rose when I was angry and rose when I was afraid and rose, worst of all, when I sat in the light too long and it did not answer, so that I would come out of the shaft with my skin hot and my brother would look at me from the dark with my own face and say nothing, which was worse than anything he could have said. I knew what it was. I had read the Order's word for the blood of things like me on a prayer-sheet: accursed. And I had read what the Order did with what it called accursed, which was to put it where it could not be seen. And I decided, at whatever age it was, with the light going out of the shaft and my brother's silence at my back, that if the Order would not put a lion on me then I would be my own lion.

I did not do it that night. There was a night, later, and I will give it to thee when I have given thee what happened to my brother first, because the two are one thing and I promised thee order. But the decision was made in the shaft. Something must sit on us. Something must hold the thing down. My father had a beast to do it, given him by the age. I had nothing given me by any age. So I would do it myself, and it would cost what it cost, and I would call the cost devotion, because devotion was the only word on the prayer-sheets that fit a thing done to oneself for a god who had not asked.

Thou wilt want to know if it was faith. I have been asked that, in the manner of the Order, which asks by inscribing, and I have never answered it, and I will answer it now, once, on this floor. I do not know. I know that it was not hope. Hope wants something back. I had already worked out, in the light, that nothing was coming back, and I had decided to go on anyway, and I do not have a word for that, and the Order does not, and I doubt thou dost. Call it what the stone calls it, if thou must. But hear the stone's sentence the way I hear it.

It was not because I was loved. Write that plainly. I will not have it softened by any hand, including mine.
