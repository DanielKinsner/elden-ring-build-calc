## III. The Word They Had for Us

Omen. Thou hast said it to my face and I did not flinch, and thou shouldst know that the not flinching took me most of a life to learn.

I did not have the word until I was old enough to have wanted one. Down there we were not called anything. The hands that fed us did not call; the other horned ones had no calling in them; my brother and I called each other nothing, because when there are two of a kind and no third to tell them apart, names are a luxury. The word came down the drain like everything else. It came in the mouths of the nobles, first, in the years when I had learned enough of their speech to follow a sentence, and the sentence was always the same shape: a complaint. The Omen below. The noise of the Omen. A vile affront to the sanctity of the tree. I remember lying under a certain grate with the water in my hair, working out that the thing they were affronted by was me, and feeling, for the first and last time in my life, something close to pride. I had reached them. Nothing else of mine ever would.

Then the hunters came, and I learned what the word cost.

I will describe one, because thou hast met them, and I met them first. He came down a way I had not known was a way, with a lantern, and I saw the lantern before I saw him and thought, for a moment, that the gold had come down to the floor at last. Then the mask. Long horns on it, twisted, longer and more twisted than any horns on any Omen I had ever seen, and under the horns the face of an old man smiling. Not a mask of an Omen. A mask of what an Omen dreams of when it dreams badly. They carve it so, on purpose; I learned that later from a robe-maker's ledger. They carve the face of the thing that frightens us and put it on the men who come to kill us, and the men are perfumers, of all things, apothecaries whose founder drank a draught to rid himself of feeling so that he could do the work, and the work is done with a cleaver whose blade is a row of horns cut from the last children they found. I have held one of those cleavers. It is heavy. It is heavy with exactly what thou wouldst think.

He did not come near us. That is the thing I have never been able to settle. He went into the halls where the others were, and there was the sound that there is, and he came back the way he had come with the lantern lower and the cleaver darker, and he passed the mouth of the channel where my brother and I lay against the stone with our shackles cold on our wrists, and he did not turn his head. I have thought he did not see us. I have thought he saw us and had been told, by someone above, that there were two in that dark he must not touch, because the blood under their horns belonged to the house. I do not know. Set it beside the other things I do not know; the pile is high and I am not ashamed of it. What I know is that the law had a door in it, somewhere, with our measure on it, the way the shackle had, and the hunters did not come through that door, and the children they found on the far side of it did not have one.

Understand what the word does, because it did it to me and I have watched it do it to others.

Omen. A sign. A thing that means something else is coming. That is what the city's word says, on its face; the Order chose a word that made us into a warning. And I lay under the grate and asked the water what we were a warning of, and the water did not say, and the years went by, and nothing came. No plague that we had foretold. No dragon. No breaking of the sky. Only more children with horns, and the cleaver, and the images with the prayer under them. We were a sign of nothing but ourselves. But there is an older word under the word, the way there is older stone under the streets, and I felt it before I could have said what it was. It is not sign. It is shunned. It is the word for a thing that must not be touched or looked at or named, the word a priest uses for the unclean days, the word one uses for a corpse before the rites. The city said Omen and meant warning and felt unclean, and I learned to hear both floors of it at once, and I have never heard the word since, not in thy mouth, not in mine, without hearing the lower floor.

Now the other thing the water taught me, which took longer, and which I would not have believed if I had not read it in the Order's own hand.

There were, in the refuse that came down, the leavings of the old wars. Not many; the capital keeps its glory and throws away its shame, and the old wars were glory. But glory frays. A strap from a knight's pauldron came down once, with the stamping still on it, a horn; a whole horn, curled, worked in red gold, the mark of an order of knights that had served my father in the first age, before I was born, and wore the horn on their shoulders as a boast. I learned their name from a prayer-sheet that came down later: knights of the crucible. The crucible was what the tree had been before it was the tree. A vessel in which all life had once been blended, when the world was young, so that men had horns and tails and scales and feathers, and these were not shames; they were signs of the divine, and the knights who bore them were the first lord's own, and the Order, in those years, called their strength holy. And then, the sheet said, in time, the strength shown by these knights, and even their appearance, was seen as chaotic and deserving of scorn.

In time.

I read that sheet by the light of the shaft until the water took the ink. Thou wilt say I am making too much of a strap and a prayer, and thou art right that a child in a drain will make a scripture out of anything. But I was not wrong, and I have had the run of the Order's records since, as king, and I have read the rest. The horn on my head and the tail at my back are not a curse that fell on the Queen's house from outside. They are the oldest things in it. They are what the first lord's own knights wore on their shoulders for an honor, and what the misbegotten in the sheds are whipped for, and what my mother's tree had been before it was gold, and the only thing that changed between the honor and the whip was the Order deciding that it had changed. I was not deformed. I was not punished. I was born with the old shape in an age that had decided the old shape was over, and there is no word in the Golden Order for that. It has heresy and it has impurity and it has Omen. It does not have late.

I told my brother.

I remember it exactly, because it is one of the few times I ever told him a thing before he told me one. I said that we were not wrong, only old. That the horn had been holy once. He listened, and he looked at the standing water, and he said that if the horn had been holy once then the ones who cut it were the heretics, and that a heretic's tree deserved whatever came up out of the dark to meet it. I said that was not what I meant. He said he knew what I meant. He said I meant that if I stood in the shaft long enough the tree would remember what horns were for. And I did not answer, because he was right, and because I have never in my life been able to answer him when he was right, only to go on doing the thing he was right about.

So. The word. Thou hast it now from both floors, as I had it. It meant that I was a warning of nothing, and it meant that I must not be touched, and under both of those it meant that I was a thing the tree had once made on purpose and had since decided not to have made. I have carried it since as one carries a name: because it was given, and because putting it down would have meant admitting that someone else got to decide what I was called, and I had already admitted that once, on the stair, and I found I could not do it twice.

I was not wrong. I was late. There is no word for late in the Golden Order.
