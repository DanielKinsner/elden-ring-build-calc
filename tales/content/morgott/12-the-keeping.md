## XII. The Keeping

The light in this room is at the hour of the second bell, or would be, if there were bells. I know it without looking. I kept the tree's hours in a drain before I could speak and I kept them in this room for an age and I am keeping them now on my back with the roof going grey, and I want to give thee one of them whole, a day of the reign, because I have given thee the reign as a set of judgments and it was not judgments. It was days. It was the same day, a great many times, and the sameness was the keeping.

Before the light, the bell.

I had it rung at the hour the light first reached the floor of the wide shaft under the city, which no one above knew was the reason, and which I never told the bishops, who thought I had chosen the hour for the tree. I had. The tree and the shaft kept the same time; I was the only man in Leyndell who knew there were two clocks. I woke before it. I had not slept, most nights; a thing with horns does not lie easily on its back, and the tail must go somewhere, and I had a bed in the old lord's rooms that I used as one uses a chair one has been given and cannot refuse. I put on the hood in the dark. I put on the robes over the tail and the gloves over the hands and I took the staff, and by the time the bell was struck I was on the rampart.

The rampart at the first bell is the best thing I had. I will say that plainly; there is not much in this account that I will call the best of anything, and this was. The city below, still dark, going gold from the top down as the light came off the tree and found the roofs. The great dragon on the wall with the frost on it. The sentinels on their horses at the gate below, gold, not moving, the standard to which every guardian of the tree aspires, as their own shields say; I had read the shields. And the soldiers coming on to the wall for the day watch, in their ranks, saying the prayer they said at every changing of the guard, which I heard every morning of my reign and never once let my face answer, because my face was under a hood.

Erdtree, O Golden Circle. Grant us the droplets of life. Structure us. Reveal our path. We are your guardians. We swat at flies, snuff out flame, repel the Omen, and undo Tarnish.

Repel the Omen. I stood ten paces from them in the gold cloth and heard my own garrison pray to be strong enough to keep me out, and then I inspected them. Have the whole of that and not the half. It was not bitter. I have been bitter, and I know the taste, and this was not it. It was the taste of a thing that fits. They prayed to repel the Omen and the Omen walked the line of them and corrected a strap here and a spear there and they were the better for it, and the tree was the better kept for it, and if there is a picture of my reign truer than the bell over the cellar it is this one: the guardians of the tree praying against the thing that was keeping them.

Then the day.

The bishops at the third bell, in the hall below this room, with the rites. I stood behind the veil for those and said the responses, which I had by heart from a grate, and no one in the hall ever remarked that the Lord of Grace said the liturgy as if it had been taught him by the stone itself, which it had. Audiences after. I have given thee one; there were hundreds, and they were all the one. Men in fine cloth who wished to be seen by the veiled lord and to go away having been seen. I saw them. It was the only thing they asked of me that I could give without lying.

And the record. I kept it. I learned to read from what the city threw away; here is what I did with it once I had the run of the city's shelves. I read everything the Order had written about itself, and then I began to write it. Not in my own hand; a lord does not. I had the monuments cut. Thou hast read them on the fields, the two lines each, on the sword-stones: the defenses of the city, the wars of my brothers and sisters, my father's last campaign. The masons cut them in my years. I read every line before it was struck and let it stand, and thou wilt have noticed, if thou hast an ear for such things, that the lines about my own wars call the thing that held the wall by the other name. The Fell Omen stacks high the corpses of heroes. I let them cut that. I could have had them cut the King. I did not. I have thought since that it was the one honest thing the record says about me, and that I put it there myself, under a name the city would not connect to the hand that approved it.

The burials. I have told thee of the first. There were many after, and they were the part of the day I did not let anyone take from me. A knight of the city who had died well was brought to the roots, and the bishops said the rite, and the lord spoke the words that gave him the tree, and I spoke them, every time, in the hood, standing at the place where the roots come up through the floor of the hall, an arm's length from the barbs. I gave the tree to men whose names I did not know. I gave it to the banished knight who had been my hand. I gave it to the honorable one who had taken Godefroy in chains. I stood at the door I could not go through and opened it, and watched them carried in, and shut it, and went back up to the seats. I never once resented one of them. Believe that if thou canst; it is the thing about me I find hardest to believe myself. I resented the tree. I resented the Order. I did not resent a single dead man who was given what I could not have. They had earned it by the Order's own measure and I was the Order's own keeper, and a keeper who begrudges the door what it admits is not a keeper. He is a thief who has not yet stolen.

Then the nights, which thou hast had. The fur on its hook; the riders at the gate; the road. I will not give them again. I will only tell thee that a man who rides all night and inspects the wall at dawn and stands through the rites and the audiences and the burials and stands, after all of it, before the seats until the last bell, does not do so for years without the body sending in its account, and I want to give thee the body, because thou hast seen it and will have wondered.

I came up the stair young. I do not know in years; I have told thee I count in defenses and heaps. But I came up it with a back that was straight under the horns and a face that was my brother's, and thou hast seen what the city gave back to me. The horns are heavy. Thou wouldst not think it, but they are; they go on growing, and a thing that carries a crown of bone on the right side of its head for an age carries it with the whole spine, and the spine bends to meet it. The staff stopped being a pretense somewhere in the second war. The hair went white. I am old, and I was made old by the city the way the city makes everything old, by needing it every day, and I do not complain of it. I had an age. Most of the things under this floor had a cleaver. But when the songs say the Omen King was a bent old thing at the end, know that he was not born bent. He was bent by keeping. There is no other way for a thing to be bent that is honest.

Now the thing that came in the middle of one of those days and that I have not told thee, because it is the last thing I know of my brother above the ground, and I have been putting it off.

A man was taken at the outer wall. Not a champion; a different kind. He came in the night without a lamp, which was the first thing the sentries noticed, and he was not seeking the tree; he was seeking blood, in the plain sense, cutting throats in the outer ward as a man does who has been sent to. They brought him to the hall in chains and I heard him from behind the veil. He would not say who had sent him. He did not need to. He had on him a medal, and the bishops' scholar read it to me, and it said that it was granted by a new dynasty and that it had the power to grant audience with its lord, and the lord's name was on it.

Luminary Mohg.

I had not heard the name said above the ground. I had said it to a shape at an altar; I have told thee that; but no one above had ever said it in my hearing, because no one above knew it existed. And here it was, on a medal, with a title in front of it that the Order gives to its saints. Luminary. A light. My brother, who had gone down into the dark with his veins lit, had made a dynasty out of it. A new one, the medal said. With knights sworn in blood, and men sent up into the world to cut throats for it, and an audience to be granted, as I granted audiences, to those who had earned it. He had found not only a mother. He had found men.

Understand what that was to hear. I had made a hand out of banished knights and called it a hand. He had made a court and called it a dynasty. I had stood in front of a chair for an age and made a law that no one might sit. He had built a chair in the deep and sat in it. I had made the future a thing that must not come. He had put his name on one and sent it up the road with a knife. And the men over the fog, I learned later, did not know who he was; they called him the one known only as the Lord of Blood, and searched for him, and I stood behind a veil in the hall with the name on a medal in front of me and knew exactly who he was and told no one, and did not send the hand, and did not go.

There is one more piece of it and then I will put the day down.

The bishops' scholars, who had the run of the towers, told me a thing about my shard that I had not asked. The great towers where the ring's pieces are made whole again each answer to certain pieces and not others, and mine answers to the tower across the eastern bridge, beyond the city's east ward; and so, they said, does one other, and the two are alike, so alike that the tower cannot be said to prefer either. They did not know whose. I did. The ring's pieces knew us both. The same tower would take his shard and mine and find them the same, as the smith had found our wrists the same, as the light on the water had found our faces the same. Whatever he had made of himself in the deep and whatever I had made of myself in the hood, the thing my mother broke had gone out into the world and recognized two of the same, and called them both hers, and neither of us has ever heard her say so.

That was a day. The bell, the wall, the prayer against me, the rites, the audiences, the record, the door I opened for others, the road, the seats. And once, in the middle of one of them, a medal with my brother's name on it and a light in front of the name, and a lord who read it and said, to the scholar and the guards and the man in chains, the two words a veiled lord says when he means to be done with a thing: see to it. I will tell thee, when we go down under the city, of another man I judged on the same two words. I judged my brother's messenger on them, and did not say the name, and went back up to the seats, and stood before them until the last bell, and did not sleep.

I kept the hours of a thing that did not keep mine. That was the reign. Thou hast walked through what it kept.
