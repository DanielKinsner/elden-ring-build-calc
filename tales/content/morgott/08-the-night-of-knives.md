## VIII. The Night of Knives

A city can wail. I did not know that until the night it did, and I have not been able to hear a crowd since, not cheering, not praying, not screaming on a wall while I cut it down, without hearing under it the sound Leyndell made the night my golden brother died.

I was still below. Understand the geography of it: my twin gone down into the dark with his new light, my blood shut in a stick, the stair still barred to me by a shackle that had not yet forgotten its work. I had the shaft and the drains and the archive in the crack, and the city's hours, which I kept, and I had kept them so long that I knew the capital's night the way a physician knows a pulse. And the pulse stopped. That is the only way I can say it. There was an hour in the deep of a night when every sound from above ceased at once, as if the city had been struck dumb, and then, after a silence I could not measure, a single voice, far off, high, and then the rest.

They came veiled. I learned that afterward, from the record and from the men who had been on the walls; I am giving it to thee here because I want it beside the sound. Assassins, women, with knives that had been made to kill a god's child by a rite I will not pretend to understand, wearing veils that no sentry could see through, and they went into the palace of the Queen on a night of fog and put those knives into the one son the house had ever shown. He was the first of the demigods to die. The stone says so, and the stone is proud of the phrase, and I have never been able to read it without wanting to add: he was the first because he was the only one they could find. The rest of us were under the ground or over the sea or in the moon's country, and the knives went where the light was.

I did not know, that night, what the wailing was for. I lay under the grate where I had once put my face to the iron and I listened to a city grieve, and I understood only that the word I had put on my list, the word the city said like tree, was being said now in a way I had never heard, over and over, in the voice one uses for a thing that has already gone. And I knew. I had seen him for the length of a breath and I knew. A thing that is loved as he was loved does not get wailed for while it is alive.

Then the Queen, whom I never met awake. I met her that night in the only way I ever did, which was as a change in the stone. I do not know what she did. The record says she was driven to the brink, and I believe the record, because I felt the brink. There was a night, not the same night, or perhaps it was, I have never been sure, when the light in my shackle went wrong. Not out. Wrong. The gold in the iron, that had lain against my wrist all my life with the steady warmth of a thing that knows its purpose, flared, and guttered, and flared, like a lamp in a draft. And far above me, through every drain at once, a sound came that was not a sound. It was the feeling of the whole city being a bell, and the bell being struck once, from inside.

She had broken the ring. Thou knowest that. The whole of thy road has been walking across the pieces. What thou canst not know, because thou wert over the fog and dead, is what it was like to be a thing held by the Order's gold at the moment the Order's gold lost its center. I will tell thee, and I will tell thee that this is the part I believe rather than the part I know, because the record has nothing to say about how a shackled child under a city came to be standing in its throne room, and I have never asked the record to.

The gold went faint.

Not in a night. Over days, weeks; I do not know. The light down the shaft came at its hour and was thinner. The prayers from above changed their shape, and then stopped, and then started again in a different shape, and I did not know the new shape and could not follow it. And the shackle on my wrist, that had made a line at the bottom of the stair that I could not cross, went slack. Not open. It never opened; I wore it up the stair and I wore it for years after, under my sleeve, and I have it still, somewhere, I suppose, on whatever is left of the wrist thou hast just seen. But the gold in it no longer knew where the line was. I walked to the bottom of the stair as I had a thousand times and stood at the place where it always caught, and it did not catch. I put my foot on the first step. Nothing. I put my foot on the second.

See what that is. I did not, for a long time, and when I did it unmade me for a while. The Order had put me under the ground and held me there with its own gold. And the Queen, in her grief, broke the thing the gold came from, and the gold forgot me. I was let out by the breaking of the Order. The Order's ruin was my door. And I went up that stair, with my blood in a stick in my hand and my horns scraping the ceiling of the stairwell and the shackle loose on my wrist, in order to hold together the thing whose breaking had let me go.

If thou findest that a contradiction, thou art not the first, and thou wert never going to be the last. My brother would have laughed. He had gone down. I have wondered, since, whether his shackle went slack the same hour, and whether he felt it, in whatever dark he had chosen, and did not turn around. I have decided that he felt it. I have decided that he did not turn around. Neither of those is in the record either.

The stair was longer than I had made it in my mind. Everything that one has only imagined is shorter than the thing. It went up through the bones of the city, and I will give thee the going, because I gave thee the stair as a line I could not cross and it deserves to be given as a place. It began as the stair I knew, wet, with the water running down it the way the water ran down everything. Then it dried. That was the first thing: a step that was dry, and then another, and I stopped on it and put my hand flat on the stone, because I had never in my life stood on a dry step, and the stone was warm from something, a kitchen, a forge, the city's own heat, coming through the wall. Then the rooms. The stair went through rooms that had been sealed and rooms that had been forgotten: storerooms with the seals broken from the inside by damp; a chapel the size of a cell with a shape at one end I did not stop to name; a gallery of stone faces with the names chiselled off them; a chamber hung with cloth gone to rags that had had, once, gold in it, and had had the gold picked out by hands that came before mine. I passed things on it that I did not stop for; I have gone back for some of them since. And the air changed. I had breathed one air for the whole of my life, the drain's, wet and close and full of us, and somewhere on the stair it thinned and went cold and carried a thing I had never smelled and knew at once from the prayer-sheets: the smell of the tree, which is not a smell of leaves. It is a smell of gold, if gold could be smelled. I stood in it and could not go on for a while, and then I went on.

There was a door at the top with no handle on my side, as I had always known there would be, and it was open. I had made that door out of the dark for an age, a door with no handle, the most closed thing in the world, and when I came to it, it was standing open, because whoever had shut it had gone with everyone else, and there had been no one to close it after them.

And the city was empty of what mattered.

Not of people; there were people, and I will come to them, and to what they saw when they saw me, and to what I did about it. But the house was empty. Thou hast walked this city; thou knowest how the streets go up toward the tree and the great stair goes up from the streets to this room. I walked it that first time in a hood I had made from a sack, at night, with my tail wrapped up under it and the staff in my hand, and I met no one. I went up through the streets I had known as sounds. The tavern above the grate I had lain beneath, dark, its shutters closed. The square where the name had been shouted, empty, with the wind in it, larger than any room I had imagined and smaller than the sound had been. The cathedral with its bell not ringing. I stood in the middle of the square in my sack with the tree over me, whole, the whole height of it going up out of the city into a sky I had never seen, and I did not look at it long. I looked at the ground, which was gold, and walked on it, and it held. Every gate I came to was open, and I understood, before I reached this room, what had happened above while I was below. The Queen was gone. Not dead; gone. The record says she was nowhere to be found and that is a phrase written by someone who looked. The golden brother was dead. My father was a chieftain in some badland under his old name. The second lord, the one with red hair whom the city had taken after my father, was gone too, into wherever the Queen had gone; I did not know then and I will not pretend to now. And the pieces of the ring, that the Queen had broken, had gone out across the world to her children, every one of them, the record says, and the children had taken them and gone mad with them and begun a war.

Every one of them. I have read that sentence as king a hundred times and I have never once been able to decide whether it includes me. The shard came to me; I will tell thee how when we reach the seats, because it happened in the seats. But I did not go out into the world with it. I did not take a country. I did not raise a host. I came up a stair into a house with every door open and no one home, and I stood in it, and the thing I felt was not the thing the record says my brothers and sisters felt. It was not the mad taint. I did what a dog does when the family has left in the night and the door is open. I went from room to room. Then I lay down across the threshold. I will not pretend it was nobler than that. I have had a long time on this floor to stop pretending.

I came up out of the drain into a house with every door open and no one home.
