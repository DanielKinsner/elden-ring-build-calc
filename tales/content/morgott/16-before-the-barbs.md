## XVI. Before the Barbs

I have not turned to it. Thou wilt have noticed; I promised thee order and I have kept the tree at my back for the whole of this telling, as I kept it at my back for the whole of my reign, standing between it and the door. I am going to turn to it now. Not with the body; the body is past turning. With the telling. This is the first time. And the light that comes down the trunk into this room has changed while I have been lying under it; it is paler than the hour it should be, the way water pales when something is being poured into it. I have noted that too.

The base of the trunk comes up through the floor of this room. Thou hast stood at it. It is not bark, there, at the root; it is something between bark and gold, the way the light on the water was between light and water, and it goes up out of sight into the thing the whole world has spent its prayers on, and around it, where a man might put his hand, there are the barbs. A wall of them. Grown from the trunk, black-gold, each as long as a spear, woven so that nothing living could pass between two of them without being opened. I do not know when they grew. That is on the pile of things I do not know and I am not ashamed of it; I came up the stair into a room with the barbs already on the tree, or they came soon after, and I have never been able to say which, because a thing that has spent its life looking at a tree from underneath does not, when it first sees the trunk, know what the trunk looked like before.

The tree wards off all who deign approach.

I said that to thee, lying here, and I chose the word. Deign. Thou wilt have taken it for scorn, and it was, and under the scorn it was this. To deign is to lower oneself. It is what a lord does when he stoops to a thing beneath him. And I said that the tree wards off all who deign approach, and what I meant, under the scorn, was this: to walk up to that trunk and put a hand to the barbs is to presume that one is the kind of thing the tree would open for. It is to stoop to the tree as if the tree were a gate. I never did. In all the years, in the hood, in the fur, at every hour of the tree's day and night, I never once put my hand to the barbs, and I am going to tell thee why, and it is the third of my three gates, and I will show it to thee and not open it.

I stood before them. That much I will give. I stood with the staff in my hand and the blood in the staff; I had put my hand to one thing already, once, in a channel, and put it under, and I knew the weight of a hand that has done that.

There were nights, more than I can count, when the room was empty and the city was asleep and the bell had rung the tree's last hour, and I would take off the hood, here, alone, because there was no one to see, and stand at the place on the floor where the shadow of the barbs ended, with the trunk going up in front of me and the black-gold wall between, and my horns bare. I did not pray. I had done my praying in a drain with my mouth shut. I stood. I looked at the barbs the way I had looked at the light on the water, and I let the tree look at me, if it looked, with the whole of the old shape uncovered, and I did not raise my hand. Every night I did not raise it. Hear that as an act, because it was one; it is harder to stand before a gate for an age and not try it than to try it once and be refused. I know. I have done the one. I have watched thee do the other.

Why. Here is the gate. I will put my hand on it so thou canst see it is mine.

I hunted ambition. I called the men on the road candles carried into a house that was not theirs. I put them out because they had presumed to come to the tree and ask it for the chair. And I stood here at night, before the barbs, with the chair behind me and the trunk in front, and I understood, the first night and every night after, that if I raised my hand I would be one of them. I would be a thing come to the tree with a claim. And the whole of what I was, the hood and the seal and the stick and the years, was built on not being that. I could not permit myself the one thing I killed men for. Not because the tree would have refused me; I do not know that it would have, and I do not know that it would not have, and I am not going to tell thee which I believed, because what I believed is not in the record and I have decided that it will not be. What I wanted is not in the record. That is the gate. It is sealed. Thou canst see my hand on it.

Now the fires. I promised thee these, twice, and they belong here, with the barbs, because they are the other half of what I did at night.

There are two fires in the world that could burn this tree. I knew of both. The first is under the city, at the bottom of the dark I was raised in, below the hall with the shape at the end of it that the horned ones would not go near. The merchants who had been sealed down there called it their lord and said it was frenzied, and I have told thee I heard it as a child, a laughing that was not laughing, and I have felt it since, on this floor, as a kind of heat with no light in it that comes up through the roots when the city is at its lowest. The Order has no word for it but madness. I knew, without anyone telling me, that if that thing ever came up the stair the way I had come up the stair, the tree would burn from the roots.

The second is on the mountain. The giants' fire. My father put the giants to the sword and the Queen shut their flame up on the top of the mount and called it the beginning of the epoch, and the churches keep her saying so, and the fire is still there, tended, the churches say, by one giant she cursed at the end of that war to tend it for eternity; a trifling giant, she called him, and I have stood before that echo too, and heard my mother make a keeper of a thing she despised; and it is the fire the tree was built over, the fire the whole Order is a lid on. Everyone in the capital knows it, the way everyone knows the tree's hours. Nobody goes north. The road to the north out of this city goes through lands the Order calls forbidden and up a great lift to the snow, and at the end of it is the forge, and the forge is the one fire in the world that could be carried to the trunk. I knew that too.

I sealed them both.

With my name. Not the king's; the other one, the one the bishops gave me. I went down the stair once as king, in the hood, past the halls, and set a seal at the bottom of the hall with the shape in it, behind the altar, of the Order's gold in the words the Order used for me: Sealed by Morgott the Grace-Given. I will tell thee what I found in that hall in the next part of this, because it deserves its own. And I went to the way north. I went at night, in the hood, alone, with the shard against my ribs, out through the east of the city where the road leaves the last ward for the lands the Order calls forbidden, and I stood at the last threshold the city owns and looked up the road, which goes into dark and then into cold and then, they say, into snow, and which no one from the capital walks. I have never walked it. I did not want to. I set the same seal there in the same words, with my hands, in the Order's gold, and I stood a while with my back to the city and the road going away from me, the only road out of Leyndell I ever closed with my own name, and then I went back in. The city read the seals and thought them holy. A door sealed by the Lord of Grace. And I stood between the tree and both fires, for an age, with a name that was not true on both locks, and I have thought since that this is the truest picture of my reign there is. A graceless thing keeping the fire off a tree in the name of grace.

And the creed. Thou hast heard it from me in pieces, in this room and on the road, and I want to give it thee whole, once, so thou canst see its shape and decide what it is.

The god had gone. Not the tree; the thing behind the tree, the will the Order says sent the ring and the grace and the fingers that read it. The record says the war led to abandonment by that will, and I did not need the record; I could feel it, in this room, the way one feels a house that has been left. The guidance had stopped. The bishops went on with the rites and the fingers over the fog went on, I am told, saying things, but the thing that had been at the top of the world was not answering, and the reason, as the record has it and as I have it, was ambition. The children had wanted the chair, and the wanting had broken the ring, and the breaking had driven the god away, and what was left was a tree with barbs on it in a house with no one home. And so my creed, if thou must call it that, was this: the god has left because of ambition, and so the world must stay as it is, with no one in the chair, forever, because the only thing worse than a tree with no god behind it is a tree with a man in front of it who thinks he is one.

Thou seest what that is. I see it, on the floor. It is a man who was never allowed to want a thing making a law that no one may want it. I have had time to see it. I did not see it on the wall. On the wall I saw a tree that no one could enter, and two fires that could burn it, and a road full of men who wanted it, and I put my name on the fires and my hands on the men and my back to the tree, and I called the whole arrangement loyalty, and it was, and the thing it was loyal to had no door.

I sealed the fire below and the road to the fire above, and I never touched the thing between them.
