## XXII. Ash

Time passes in a room. I did not know that until I had nothing to do in one but lie in it.

Thou didst go. I told thee in the prologue that I felt the seal on the north road draw when my heart did, like a bolt on the far side of a gate, and I did; and I felt the other one too, the one at the bottom of the dark, behind the altar, in front of the shape with my face. Both of my seals went when I went. I had set them in the name the bishops gave me, and the name was a hood, and the hood came off with the rest, and the Order's gold on both locks did not know what to hold to any longer and let go. The fire below and the road to the fire above, open, in the same breath. I had guarded the tree with my life. I had not understood that my life was the lock.

And the door behind the seats opened.

I have told thee I never went through it. It opened by itself when I died, or was opened by thee; I could not turn to see, and I have not tried. But I heard what was in it. I have told thee the Queen's words stay where she said them, on fields, in churches, and there were words in that room. Her voice, from behind the chairs, in the dark, close, closer than I had ever been to it in my life, saying a thing to someone who was not there.

O Radagon, leal hound of the Golden Order. Let us be shattered, both. Mine other self.

I do not know what it means. That goes with the rest of what I do not know, near the top. My mother's voice was behind a door I kept shut for an age, saying a thing to the second lord about being shattered together, and calling him her other self, and I lay on the step of her chair with my blood drying on it and heard it and did not understand it and will not guess, because I have already told thee what a life of guessing at her made of me. It was her voice. It was the first time I had heard it in a room instead of on a wind. It was not speaking to me. That is the whole of what I know, and I have put it with the rest.

Then the light went wrong.

I told thee at the start that the gold was going the wrong way; I have been telling thee this whole account inside it, and marking it as it went, and now it has gone. Here is what it was. The tree's light comes down into this room from above, always, at every hour, gold; it is the one thing about the room that does not change, the thing I kept the hours of in a drain. It thinned first. I noticed that while I was telling thee of the other name, the hunting name: the gold on the floor beside my hand not less bright but less deep, the way the light on the water had been thinner in the days after the ring broke. Then the edges went white. I told thee that too, at the fight. It was as if the light had been heated. And then, while I was telling thee of the step and the words, it went white through, all of it, the way iron goes white before it runs, so that the seats behind me threw no shadow and the room had no hour in it at all, and I lay in a light with no time in it and understood that the thing above had stopped being a tree and had begun to be a fire. Then the red. At the edges first, the way the fire had come into my brother's veins at the roots of the horns; then everywhere; the red I had seen in a channel and on a sword's edge and nowhere else in an age, the red the Order has no prayer for, coming down the shafts of this room where the gold had always come. And the room began to fill with a sound I had never heard and knew at once, because I had spent my life keeping two fires away from it: the sound of a tree the size of the world beginning to burn.

It came by the road I had sealed.

Thou wilt know better than I how. I know only what the room told me: that the fire did not come up from below, from the thing that laughs, but down, from above, from the direction of the mountain where my father put the giants to the sword and my mother shut their flame, and that it was carried, and that the only road by which it could have been carried was the one I had closed with my name and opened with my death. Thou didst not deign approach. I told thee the tree wards off all who do, and I was right, and it did not matter. Thou didst not walk up to the barbs and ask. Thou didst go around, north, by the way I had kept shut, to the one fire that could reach the trunk, and thou didst bring it, and the barbs that refused every claimant in an age did not refuse the flame. I had spent my life keeping the fire off the tree so that the tree would stay shut. Thou didst put the fire to the tree so that it would open. We had the same tree and the same fire and the same barbs, and we did the opposite thing with them, and thine worked.

The ash came down. Not at once. First a grey in the red light, the way dust hangs in the light of a shaft, so that I thought for a while it was only the light going bad. Then it was on the floor. A little; a film on the stone beside my hand that had not been there. Then it was on the step where my blood had dried, and the blood went under it, and I watched that with more attention than I have watched most things. Into this room, through the roof of the sanctuary, through the roots; it came down the way the light had always come, from above, and it lay on the floor and on the seats and on what was left of me, and it went on coming, and the seats went grey in my sight one at a time, the small ones first because they were nearer the door, and then the great one, the seat, the one I had kept an age from every hand; the ash did not know which it was. It lay on all of them evenly, without preference, on the great chair and the small ones alike, and I watched it, and I thought of a thing I had not thought of since I was a child in a shaft. That everything comes from above. That I had built a whole god out of that fact, and a whole reign, and here was the last thing to come from above, grey, on every seat at once, and it was the first thing that had ever come down to this room that did not care which chair it landed on.

The city went grey with it. I could hear that too, through the floor, the way I have heard everything. The bells stopped. The song did not; something went on singing it in the streets under the ash, thin, one voice, the way a thing is sung by someone who has forgotten there is anyone to hear, and it had lines I had not given thee, and I will give them now, because they were written for this hour by someone who had not seen it. Our land's grown old, now ashen, and cold. But look up, the Erdtree glows. It went round again, and again; whoever it was had only the one verse and sang it into the ash as if the ash were the audience it had been waiting for. I listened to it the way I had listened to the drains. It did glow. That was the thing. Through the ash, above the city, the tree was lit as it had never been lit, gold and red together, the light and the blood, the two things I had spent an age keeping apart, one fire.

One thing about the fire, and then I will wait for what I am waiting for.

I called it ambition. All of it, for an age: the lamps on the road, the claim on the chair, the thing in a man that walks him up to a tree and makes him put his hand to it. I said in the prologue that a flame which will not stay out is not ambition, that it is something else, and that I would spend what was left of me looking for the name. I have looked. I do not have it. But I have the shape of it, lying here with the ash on me, and the shape is this: it is the thing in thee that did not need the tree to open in order to go on. I needed it. I told myself I did not; I told myself I was the case that made the teaching true, the believer with nothing given; and I stood before the barbs every night for an age and did not raise my hand, and I have told thee that was an act, and it was, and it was also this: I could not bear to find out. Thou couldst. Thou didst not go to the barbs to find out. Thou wentest north for the fire, as if the answer did not matter, as if the road were the thing and not the door. That is not ambition. I had no word for it because I had never once in my life done a thing without first needing to know whether the tree would allow it.

The ash is on the seats. The tree is burning. The room is the color of the drain I was raised in. And there is a sound now on the stair from the sanctuary that I have never heard in this house and have heard every day of my life, through a grate, in a square, shouted by a thousand men until the stone shook.

The fire came anyway. It came by the road I had sealed. My seal broke when I did. I had guarded the tree with my life, and my life was the lock.
