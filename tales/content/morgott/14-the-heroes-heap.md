## XIV. The Heroes' Heap

Through the door of this room, if I could rise, I could see the direction of the outer wall. I have looked that way from the ramparts more nights than I have looked at the tree. There is a field outside the wall, and thou hast crossed it, and thou wilt have noticed what it is made of. I made it. This is the account of that.

They came back. The Queen had promised, in the church on the road, that after their death she would give back what she had claimed, and that they should return and wage war and brandish the ring; and the Order, whatever else may be said of it, keeps its promises to the dead. They came back over the fog. Not all at once. In ones, at first, then in bands, then in a tide; the grace that had been taken from my father's warriors was given again to their bones and they stood up wherever they had fallen abroad and turned toward the tree like plants. Tarnished. That was the word the city had for them, and it was said with the same contempt as the word for me, and I noticed that, and did nothing with it for a long time.

Understand what they were to the city. The record over the fog says the round table was once full of venerated warriors; I did not know that then and it would not have mattered. To the capital they were a rumor and then a road-fever and then an army. Men with grace in them who had died and come back, whom the tree itself had called, who therefore believed, every one, that the chair was meant for them. They had a claim I did not. I have never once denied it. The Queen had promised them, by name, as a host, and she had promised me nothing, and they came up the road toward the promise with their lamps lit and found a thing in the road that she had forgotten to mention.

They laid siege. The record of the city says it plainly, in the mouth of a noble I have heard say it to a Tarnished's face: the Shattering caused a great many fools to overstep their bounds, and their impudence led to insurgency against Morgott, Lord of Grace; they raised an army and sought to lay siege to this sacred ground. That is the city's version and it is correct in every particular, and the noble who says it goes on, in the next breath, to tell how the traitors were eyed by the grotesque Fell Omen and their corpses piled high outside the wall, and does not hear himself. He praises the lord and curses the monster in one sentence and does not know they were the same sentence. I have listened to him say it. I have stood behind the veil and heard my city describe me to my face as two men, and I have never once corrected it, because the second man was the one who had actually held the wall, and the first was the one they would have let hold it.

The Second Defense of Leyndell. Two lines again, on a monument, and this time they are what happened. The Fell Omen stacks high the corpses of heroes. Yet the Erdtree remains unshaken.

Heroes. The monument uses the word, and I have let it stand, and I will tell thee why. They were. Not because they were right; I have told thee I do not know if they were, and I have had time on this floor to stop pretending I ever did. Because they had come back from death to walk up a road toward a thing that had been promised them, and I met them on it, and they did not turn. A hero is not a man who wins. It is a man who does not turn on the road. I know that because I was the road, and I saw a great many of them come, and none of them turned, and I put them down in a heap outside the wall of a city that has called the heap a lesson ever since. The lesson, the noble says, is that the capital is simply not where they belong. I have heard him say that too. I was born under the capital. I know a great deal about where things belong.

The field. Thou hast crossed it; cross it again knowing.

It lies between the outer wall and the road. The engines of the first alliance are still in it, sunk; on top of them, the second war. I fought on that field in the fur, at night, at the head of the riders, and by day from the wall in the hood, and the heap grew. Be exact about that: it grew because I made it grow. I did not let the dead be carried off. I had the corpses left where they fell, in sight of the road, so that the next band coming up it would see what the last had come to, and I told myself this was mercy, that a man who turns back at the sight of a heap is a man I have not had to kill. Some turned. Most did not. The heap is a lesson only to those who were never going to climb it.

And on that field, among the dead, I heard my mother's voice.

The Queen's words are kept by the churches and carried by the wind, and I stood before them as king. This was not a church. It was the field, at night, after a fight, with the riders gone back to the gate and the lamps going out in the hands of the heaped, and I was standing in the fur with the staff in my hand and my breath going out white in the cold, and the words were there. Not spoken. There, the way the light is there on water. Lingering. They must have been said once, on that ground, by her, to someone; the churches are not the only places she spoke, and where she spoke, the words stay. I did not know that. I stood in them before I knew what they were.

Hear me, Demigods. My children beloved.

I have never in my life been called beloved. Not by the hands, not by the bishops, not by my brother, who pitied me, not by anyone. And I stood on a heap of the dead I had made and heard my mother's voice say my children beloved into the dark, and for the length of a breath I did not know who she meant, and I let myself, for that breath, be inside the word. I will not describe what that was. I have no words for it that are not the Order's, and the Order's words for it are the words it put on my memorial, and I have told thee what I think of those.

Then she said the rest.

Make of thyselves that which ye desire. Be it a Lord. Be it a God. But should ye fail to become aught at all, ye will be forsaken. Amounting only to sacrifices.

Forsaken.

I knew the word. I had known it from the drains; it was the Order's word for what is outside the circle, the word for the ground that is not ground. I had not known it was hers. I had not known she had said it to her children, to the ones she had shown, on a field, as a condition. Become something, or be nothing. Be a lord, be a god, or be a sacrifice, and understand that I will not keep the ones who are nothing. I stood there in the fur and understood three things in the time it takes to understand nothing at all. That she had meant it. That she had meant it for all of them, the general and the praetor and the witch and the golden one and the grafted one, every child on every seat, and that the war they had made was the war of children who had heard their mother say or be forsaken and had believed her. And that she had meant it, most of all, first of all, before any of them, for the two she had never shown. We had failed to become aught. We had been made nothing before we could fail at anything else. We were the sacrifices the sentence was for. She had said it over our heads, to the ones with pages, and the ones with pages had gone mad, and I was standing on the result.

And I went on.

That is the thing to have from this above all the rest. I did not go down. I did not take off the fur and walk into the dark like my brother and find something that wanted my wound. I heard my mother say that the nothing would be forsaken, and I looked at the heap of heroes, and I understood that I was the nothing and the heap was made of the somethings she had promised, and I went back to the gate, and the next night I went out again. I killed more. I stacked them higher. I have asked myself on this floor what I thought I was doing and I have an answer and it is not a good one. I thought that if I could keep the tree unshaken long enough, by my own hands, out of nothing, with no grace in me and no promise and no page, then the sentence would have to be wrong. Not forgiven. Wrong. A thing that had been made nothing had kept the tree, and the tree stood, and the sentence on the field would be a lie, and I would have made it one.

The tree stood. I stacked the heroes. The sentence is still there in the field. Thou hast walked through it. There is a voice in the streets now, under the floor, singing something I know. I will give it thee in its place.

I put the word away with the blood. I had a place for such things by then: a stick, a hood, a door behind the seats I did not open. I put forsaken in with the rest and sealed it and went on being the Lord of Grace above a heap. And it came back; thou wert there, on this floor, when it did. At the end, with my blood loose and the seats stained and thee standing over me, it came up out of the place I had put it, in her voice, in my mouth, and I said it to thee. I said we. I will tell thee what I meant by we when we come to it. I did not mean the demigods.

Forsaken. I put the word away with the blood. It came back with it.
