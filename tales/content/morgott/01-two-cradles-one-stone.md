## I. Two Cradles, One Stone

The floor is cold. I have lain on colder. I begin with the cold because it is the first thing I knew, and because a man who begins his account with his mother is lying about the order in which things arrived.

I was born above. I did not see it, and no one has ever told me so, and I know it as surely as I know the weight of my own horns. Hear the reasoning; it is the first piece of reasoning I ever did, and I did it in the dark, and it has held. The Order has a law for children like me, and the law has two floors. A horned child born in a hovel, or a shop, or a soldier's quarters outside the wall, is taken to the knife. Every horn is cut from it, and most of them die of the cutting, and the ones who live are left to be what they are, unhorned and unforgiven. A horned child born in the palace is not cut. It is carried down. It is put in the dark under the city with a shackle made to its measure and a small image of it made in secret and hidden somewhere above, so that it can be mourned without being mentioned. I was not cut. I was carried. Therefore I was born above. I have never needed anyone to tell me which floor of the law I fell through.

There were two of us. I will say his name later; I have found I cannot say it early in anything, not even this. For now: two horned infants, in the house of the Queen and her first lord, in an age when that house was the brightest thing under the sky. That is what the Order would say if it said anything. It has said nothing. There is a page in its records for my brother above, the golden one, and there is no page for us, and so I cannot tell thee whether we came before him or after, and I have stopped pretending it matters. What I can tell thee is what was done, because the doing left marks.

They did not cut us.

Set that down before anything else and keep it where thou canst see it, because everything I did with my life was done in the shadow of that one withheld knife. Somewhere above me, in a room with light in it, a decision was made that the two horned things that had come out of the Queen would not be put under the blade like the horned things that came out of tanners' wives. Not from kindness. Do not mistake it for kindness; I did, for a while, when I was young, and it cost me more than the truth would have. From rank. A royal Omen cannot be buried in a pauper's pit with its horns in a bucket. It must be buried alive, with them on. Its blood is holy. That is the whole of the reasoning. My horns were spared the knife because the blood under them was the Queen's, and the same blood was why I could not be allowed to be seen. Royalty kept me alive and royalty put me under the ground, and they were the same act, performed by the same hands, and I have spent an age trying to separate them and cannot.

I do not know whose hands.

That is the first of the three gates, and I will show it to thee now so thou wilt know its shape when it comes again. I do not know who carried us down. I know the law was the Queen's, because every law was the Queen's; the Order did not have laws that were not hers, any more than the tree had branches that were not its own. I do not know whether she gave the word herself, or whether she was in the field with my father and came back to find it done, or whether she never asked and was never told, and I have decided, lying here, that I will not guess. I guessed for a long time. I guessed her face bent over the two of us, and I guessed her turning away, and I guessed her never once coming to the top of the stair, and every one of those guesses was a way of having a mother, and I have had enough of having a mother by invention. She made the law. Whoever carried it out carried me. Leave it there.

What I can give thee is the shackle.

There is a thing in this world made for me and for no one else, and it is not a crown. It is a fetish of gold-worked iron, small enough to close on a wrist that had not yet grown, and its magic was the Order's gold, the same gold that lights the streets and gilds the knights, put to the work of keeping one particular child from climbing a stair. Thou hast held it. I know thou hast, because I felt it, on the bridge and again in this room: the old gold catching at me like a hand at a sleeve, for a breath, then failing. It has almost no strength left in it. It has enough to remember what it was for. Think on that. Somewhere in the capital, before I had a word for the capital, a smith was given the measure of an infant's wrist and told to make it a prison, and he made it well, and it lasted an age, and what is left of it can still find me across a battlefield. There was never a thing made for me with that much care again.

And the image. I have never seen it. The law says one is made, in secret, for a royal Omen, and hidden, so that the house can grieve a child it has decided was never born. I looked for mine, once, when I was king and had the run of every room above the ground. I did not find it. I stopped looking, and I will tell thee why when we come to the seats: a man cannot look for his own memorial and keep a city at the same time, and I chose the city. But it exists. It was made by someone who knew my face when it was new, and put it somewhere dark, and went back upstairs. There are two of me in the world that I have never seen. One is that image. The other is my brother, and I have seen him, and that was worse.

I have no memory of the stair, and I have walked it since, both ways, more times than there are seats in that room, and I have never once walked it without the body remembering what the mind does not. It goes down from the streets in a way that is not meant to be found: through a well, through a floor, through the kind of door that has no handle on the inside. Thou hast found it. Thou findest everything. The city calls what is under it a repository of waste, and it is not wrong; everything the capital cannot bear to look at is put down that stair, and it all arrives in the same water. Refuse. The dead who are not wanted in the tree. Men whose crimes the Order preferred to forget rather than to judge. Merchants, once, an entire caravan of them, sealed in. And children with horns, of which there were, in my time down there, a great many, and of which two had a shackle each with their measure on it, and the rest had nothing, because the rest had been born in hovels and had lived through the knife and had nowhere else on the face of the earth to go.

See the two of us set down. Not by whom. Set down, on stone, in a place where the light from above came in at one hour in the day and only one, through gratings so far up that the gold arrived as a rumor of gold. Two infants with the Queen's blood in them and horns already, small ones, the buds of them, coming through the skin of the skull the way a tooth comes through a gum. Shackled. I do not mean chained to a wall. I mean the gold on the iron knew us, and would not let us past a certain line, and the line was the bottom of the stair. The other Omen could go where they liked in that darkness. The two of us could not go up. We were the only ones down there who had anywhere to go up to, and we were the only ones who could not.

I said I would not begin with my mother, and I have not; I have begun with the law, which is what she left in the place where a mother stands. But I will end this first part of the telling with a thing I did not understand until I was old, and had killed a great many people, and had time on a rampart at night to think about knives.

The knife they withheld from us was not withheld from everyone. In the city above, while I was learning to stand on stone, the perfumers' hunters were at their work with a cleaver made of the horns of the children they had already cut, and the children who lived through it were memorialized, when they died, with a small image and a prayer carved under it. I have read the prayer. The images come down the stair eventually, like everything else. It says: please, do not hate me, or curse me. Please. A child's words put in the mouth of a child by whoever made the image, so that the living could be forgiven by the dead for what the living did.

Nobody carved a prayer under mine. There was no need. I was not dead. I was only under the city, with my horns on, alive, and the whole point of the second floor of the law is that a royal Omen never gives anyone the occasion to ask forgiveness.

They did not cut us. Remember that when thou weighest what they did instead.
