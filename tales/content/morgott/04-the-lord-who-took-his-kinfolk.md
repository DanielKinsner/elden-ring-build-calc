## IV. The Lord Who Took His Kinfolk

Above me, if I could turn my head, there is a statue. Thou hast passed it. A man the height of a rampart, with a lion on his back and an axe in his hands and his eyes on something past the door, cut in the gold-veined stone the Order uses for the few things it wants to last. I have stood under that statue more times than I have stood under anything, and I have never once been able to tell whether the face is looking down or away. I learned my father's face from it. Other sons forget their fathers' faces; I had to build mine from a sculptor's guess, at an age when I had already killed men.

But I knew his name first. Before the tree's, almost. It was the name the soldiers shouted.

Thou must understand how sound comes down through a city. Not evenly. The prayers came down as a hum; the bells as a weight; but a name shouted by a thousand men at once in a square comes down a drain like a fist, and the first time I heard it I thought the stone was breaking. Godfrey. Then the feet, marching, and the stone shivering under the marching, and then the name again, and it went on for most of a day, and when it stopped the city was quiet in a way I had never heard it, and my brother said, from the dark, that something had left. He was right. He was always right about leaving.

Here is what I learned of him, by ear first and by stone after, and I will tell thee which is which where it matters.

He was the Queen's lord and he was the lord of the battlefield, and the second was the reason for the first. The age of the tree began in war, and he was the war. He led the host against the giants and put them to the sword and shut their fire up on the top of the mountain, and the Queen said over the army when it was done that a new epoch had begun, one glistening with life, and told them to brandish the ring for the age of the tree. He faced the lord of the storm alone. And he had been, before any of that, a thing so ferocious that when he swore to be a lord he took a beast upon his back, an old lion with a mind, to sit at his shoulders and hold down the lust for killing that would not otherwise stop. All of that is stone. The Order cut it into crowns and axes and the shields of the men who guard this room, and I read it there later and found that it matched, nearly to the word, what the drains had told me. The city never lied about my father. It was the one subject on which it had no need to.

See me learning it. A child in a drain with a strap from a knight's pauldron in his hands and his father's name coming down through the grate like weather. I made him out of that, the way I made the tree out of a word and a shaft. I made a man who could not stop killing and had put a lion on his back so that he would, and I loved the lion before I loved the man, because the lion was the part of the story I understood. Something must sit on us. Something must hold the thing down. I was not yet old enough to have a thing that needed holding, but I had watched my brother look at the water, and I had a sense, even then, of what was coming up out of it.

Then the day the sound changed.

I did not know what it meant when it happened. I have put it together since from the Queen's own words, which the churches kept, and which I have stood in front of as king and heard carried by the wind the way they were carried by the drains. There came a moment, the stone says, when his last worthy enemy fell. And when that moment came, the hue of his eyes faded. That is the whole of the Order's account of the end of my father's lordship, and I have turned it over for an age and I do not think it is a metaphor. Something went out of him when there was no one left to fight, and the Queen saw it go, and she said the words.

My lord, and thy warriors. I divest each of thee of thy grace. With thine eyes dimmed, ye will be driven from the Lands Between.

I heard the going. I did not hear the words; I have told thee I learned those later, from stone and wind. But I heard the going. Feet again, but not marching now; a long, slow leaving, a host going out through the gates of the city and away, and no shouting, no name, only the feet, for a day and a night, and under the feet the sound of a city that has decided not to watch. My brother lay on his back in the dark and laughed. Not cruelly. I have thought about that laugh for longer than I have thought about most things, and I do not believe it was cruel. He laughed because a thing he had always said had turned out to be true. He had always said there was nothing up there for us. And now the one man in the world who might have come down the stair and looked at the two horned things at the bottom of it and known them for his own was walking out of the gate with his eyes gone grey, and would never come down any stair again.

He took with him his kinfolk.

That is the stone's phrase; thou hast read it, cut into the memory of him that the tree keeps. When he was robbed of his grace he took with him his kinfolk and left the Lands Between, and they marched a long march, and at the end of it he set his kingship down and was a plain warrior again, a chieftain of some far badland under a name that was his before the Queen gave him hers. Kinfolk. I have read the word until it stopped meaning anything and then read it until it meant something again. His warriors; his clan; the men who had shouted his name in the square. I do not know whether I was ever meant to be inside that word. I do not know whether he knew there were two of us in the dark to be inside it or not. That is not one of my three gates, because it is not mine to seal; it is his, and he is not here, and I have decided that a man who is not in the room does not get to have his silence spoken for. But I have counted, many times, the way one counts a garrison, and the count comes out the same. He took his kinfolk. We were under his feet when he went. We were not kin.

There is one more thing, and then I will go to the brother who was.

After he had gone, the Queen spoke again, in another church, softer, and the churches kept that too. She said that after their death she would give back what she had claimed. That they should grow strong in the face of death, her lord's warriors. That she would call them home. I have stood before that echo as king and heard it and understood, as any man would, that it was not addressed to me; that the grace she promised to return was grace she had first given, and one cannot return a thing to a place it has never been. And I have stood there and understood, as perhaps not any man would, that she had made a promise to my father's exiles that she had never made to my father's sons. They were to come back. That was the arrangement. Be driven out, die abroad, and come back for the ring. I was never driven out. I was never abroad. And so when they came, at the end, in their thousands, with their little lights, the ones she had promised, I was the thing standing in the road.

I did not choose that. Let it be clear, because there will be a great deal in this account that I did choose and I would not have thee confuse the two. The Queen made a promise to a host of men leaving by the gate, and left two of her own under the gate, and never spoke to either, and then went away herself; and what was left, when the promise came due, was the two of us. One of us went down. And one of us stood in the road, in a hood, and put out the lights of the men our father had taken with him when he went.

He took his kin with him. I have counted, and we were not kin.
