## IX. The Empty Seats

They are behind me now. I cannot turn to them and I would not if I could; I have looked at them from every side for an age and I know what they look like empty better than any man alive or dead. But thou hast seen them, so let me tell thee what I saw the first time, when I came through that door in a sack with my tail under it, and thou canst set the two against each other.

A room at the foot of the tree. Not large; the Order builds its grandeur outside and keeps its rooms small, so that a man standing in them feels the wall of the world at his back. A platform. On the platform, seats, of the old wood, more than one, and one greater than the rest. Behind them, a door, closed, that I did not open then and have never opened; I will tell thee why in a moment. And in front of them, where I stood, the floor thou hast just laid me on, with the roots under it. No one. I had known there would be no one. I had not known what it would be like to be the only living thing in a room built for the presence of a god.

I will not tell thee whose seats. I said in the prologue that I would name none, and I have had reasons for that for longer than thou hast been alive, and the reasons are these: the Order never told me, and I never asked, and a man who has been given a room and not the names of the chairs in it does well not to guess. There was one seat that was the seat. Thou knowest which. And there were the others, and when the war came and the shardbearers each took a country and a title, I looked at the others and understood whose they might have been, and I did not sit in any, and I did not let anyone else sit in any, and I have kept them empty for an age, and I want thee to understand that keeping a thing empty is work. It is harder than filling it. Anyone can fill a chair.

The door behind the seats was the Queen's. I knew it the way one knows which room in a house is the one the children are not allowed in: by the silence around it. I have not gone through it. I could have; there was no one to stop me for the whole of my reign and I had the run of every other room. I did not, and the reason is not reverence, or not only. It is that a thing which has spent its life imagining a mother does not, when the mother's door is at last unguarded, go in and look. It stands outside. It keeps the door. That is the whole of my kingship, if thou wantest it in one sentence, and I have just given it to thee, and I will spend the rest of the telling giving thee the sentence's parts.

The shard came next, and I will tell thee how. The record says a thing about it that thou hast read, on the piece itself, when thou didst take it from what was left of me. It says that it is the anchor ring that houses the base, and that it proves two things: that the Omen King was born of the golden lineage, and that he was indeed the Lord of Leyndell. Hear what that sentence is. It is a proof offered to people who could not look at my face. My blood needed proving. The Order could not see it in the horns, so it required a document, and the document was a piece of the ring my mother broke, and here is how it came to me.

It was in the room. Thou wilt say the shards went out to the Queen's children, and they did, and the record says they all claimed them, and I have never argued with the record on that point because I do not know how the others came by theirs. I know how I came by mine. I stood in this room in my sack, and at the foot of the tree, at the place where the roots come up through the floor and the base of the great trunk is, there was a piece of gold lying on the stone the way a fallen thing lies, and it was not lying still. It was moving toward me. Not fast. The way the light on the water moved. I did not go to it. I have told thee I learned the posture of a thing that does not ask. It came the length of the floor and stopped at my foot and I picked it up and it was warm, and I knew, with the whole of my blood, that the ring had recognized in me the one thing about me the Order had never wanted to see. It knew whose I was. It came to the blood. It was the first thing above the ground that ever had.

I have carried it since. Thou hast it now. It will tell thee, if thou takest it to the tower across the eastern bridge, exactly what it told the Erdbishops when I showed it to them: that the thing in the hood is the Queen's. I hope it is some use to thee. It was the only thing I was ever given that did not come with a measure on it.

The Erdbishops. I owe thee an account of them and I will give it, though I will name none, because there were several and they were one thing, the way a wall is one thing. They were what the city had left of its priesthood when the Queen went into wherever she went; men in gold who kept the rites and did not know, any longer, for whom. I went to them. Not at once. I spent a season in the city first, at night, learning its streets from above as I had learned them from below, and I found that I knew them, every one, by the sound they made in the drains, and that walking them was like walking through a song one has known all one's life and never seen written. Then I went to the bishops, at night, in the hood, with the shard in my hand and the staff in the other, and I spoke to them.

I spoke the Order's speech. That is what undid them, more than the shard. I had learned every word I owned from the outside of a grate, from prayers and proclamations and the sermons of the very men in front of me, and when I opened my mouth what came out was their own liturgy in the voice of a thing the height of a door with horns under its hood. They did not see the horns. I had made sure of it. They saw the shard, and heard the tree's own words in a voice that could have shaken their cathedral, and they were men whose god had gone away in the night, and they did what such men do. They asked my name.

I had one. I do not know who gave it to me. It was given below, by the hands, or above, by whoever made the small image of me and hid it, and I have it as one has a scar, without any memory of the cut. Morgott. I said it. And one of them, I will not say which, said the other word.

Grace-Given.

Thou must understand what the word is before thou canst understand what I did with it. The Order's word for the tree's guidance is grace: the gold that dwells in the chosen and leads them. My father had it and lost it. The warriors who left with him had it and lost it, and the Queen promised to give it back to them dead. I never had it. I was born without it, and the Order says so on the stone above this room, in the one word it ever chose for me that was exact. Graceless. And the bishops, seeing a hooded thing come out of the night with the ring's own gold in its hand and the tree's own words in its mouth, in the hour when the tree had gone silent, named the thing what they needed it to be. A gift of grace. A lord the tree had sent. They said it and looked at each other and I watched them decide, in the space of a breath, that it was true, because the alternative was that the tree had sent nothing and they were alone.

I did not choose the name. That is the second of my three gates, and I will show it to thee now and not open it. I do not know who first said it; I have told thee that. I will tell thee one thing more and then seal it. I did not refuse it. I stood in a room of priests with my horns an inch under a sack and heard myself called the one thing the stone says I was not, and I let it stand. I have told myself, since, that a veiled lord needs a name that answers the question no one may ask. That is true. It is also true that no one had ever called me anything that I wanted to be, and this was near enough, and I was tired, and I let it stand.

The rest followed as such things follow. There is a shape that a city takes around a man who stands in a certain place long enough, and I stood in it. The bishops brought the knights. The knights brought the sentinels on the walls, the great gold ones on their horses that are the standard to which every guardian of the tree aspires, and the sentinels brought the soldiers, and the soldiers were the ramparts, and by the time the first banner came up the road toward the outer wall there was a thing in Leyndell that the banner could be raised against, and the thing was me. They called me lord. Later they called me king. I let the second stand as I had let the first; I have never known which of the two is worse. Understand the verb, though. I did not take the seat. I stood between it and the door, and a thing that stands between a seat and a door long enough is called a king by people who have stopped asking questions.

And the veil. They called me the Veiled Monarch, the ones who came from outside and wanted to know what the capital had at the top of it; I have heard that the men at the round table over the fog said the phrase as if it were a riddle. It was not a riddle. It was a hood. First a sack; then, when the bishops could not bear a lord in a sack, a hood of the Order's cloth, gold-worked, deep, that hid the horns and threw the face into shadow; and robes that hid the tail, and gloves, and a staff that any old lord might lean on. The city saw a tall old man in gold who did not show his face and spoke the tree's words and had the ring's shard, and it decided that a lord who hides his face is a mystery, and a mystery is a kind of majesty, and it called me by the mystery's name. Veiled. Nobody asked what was under it. That is the thing about a veil the city has decided is majestic: it is the one thing in the city no one wishes to lift.

Under it, every day, for the whole of the age, was the thing they had put under the ground.

I was called Grace-Given. I did not correct the spelling.
