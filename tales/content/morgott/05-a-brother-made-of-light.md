## V. A Brother Made of Light

I have kept a memory. I am going to give it to thee. I have never given it to anyone, and I am giving it now because I have lain here long enough to have decided that a thing kept by a dead man is not kept, only lost more slowly.

There was a golden brother. Thou knowest the name; the whole of the Lands Between knows it, and it is the name the Order chose to remember when it chose to forget the rest of us. Godwyn. He was the son the house showed. He was the one with a page. When the soldiers were not shouting my father's name they were shouting his, and the shouting had a different weight, lighter, the way a thing sounds that is loved instead of feared. The city said his name the way it said tree. I had noticed by then that the city had a small number of words it said that way, and I had begun to make a list of them, and he was on it.

He fought the dragons. That came down the drains as thunder.

I do not mean I understood it as dragons at the time. I mean that there was a season, when I was young enough that my horns had not yet crossed each other, when the sky above the city fell in. There is no other way to say it. The stone shook for days. Things came down the shafts that had never come down them before, dust and broken slate and once a long piece of iron that I think now was a rain-gutter torn from a roof. The bellowing in the halls stopped; even the Omen who had never learned a word understood that something above was larger than the thing that fed them. And under it all, at intervals, a sound like the whole city being struck with a hammer the size of the city. I know now what it was. It was an ancient dragon called Gransax, come down on the capital, and it was the only time in all the record that the walls of Leyndell have fallen, and it was the beginning of my brother's war.

He won it. Not alone; there was a host, and there were the knights of the capital, who afterward took the dragons for their own faith and put lightning in their prayers. But it was his. He fought one of them to the last, a dread thing called Fortissax, and when it was down he did not kill it. He made it his friend. I heard that come down the drains as a change in the prayers, a new word in the hum, and I did not know what the word meant until I read it on a spear-shaft years later. He made his enemy his friend, and the city built a cult out of it, and there is a dead dragon draped on the rampart of this city to this day, one of the ones that fell, that I have looked at from a window every night of my reign and thought: this one they killed, and the other he did not, and the one he did not kill was the thing they loved him for.

I will tell thee now the thing I have kept.

There was a grate. Not the wide shaft where I sat in the light; a smaller one, at the level of a street, in a channel I had found by going where the water was loudest, and it opened on the underside of a road so close to the surface that I could put my fingers through and feel wind. I went there often. I went there because it was the nearest I could come to the sound of feet. And one day the feet came differently: a great many of them, slow, in step, and a music over them, and the city's word for my brother said by so many mouths at once that the grate itself hummed with it.

I put my face to the iron.

I saw legs first. Soldiers' greaves, gold, in rows. Then a gap in the rows, and in the gap, a man on a horse.

I saw him for the length of a breath. I will not make it longer than it was; I have hated, all my life, the way the city made things longer than they were. He was gold, in the way the light on the water was gold: not the color, the fact. The city's love was on him like weather. He looked ahead. He did not look down, and there was no reason on earth for him to look down, and I did not want him to. I did not want to be seen. I had already learned, in the shaft, the posture of a thing that is looked at by the light and does not ask, and I held that posture with my face against the iron and my horns scraping the stone of the road above, and the breath ended, and the rows closed, and he was gone.

I never saw him again.

I have thought, since, about what it was I loved, because I have been asked, in the manner of the Order, which does not ask so much as inscribe: he loved the tree. And that is true, and I will not have it taken from me. But a child does not love a tree. A child loves a thing that looks back, or seems as though it might, and my brother, going past that grate with the light on him and his eyes ahead, was the first thing in the world that I had seen the light on that was not water. The tree's light with a face. I did not love him for himself; I had no self of his to love. I loved him as the first proof that the thing I sat under every day could rest on a man and make him what the city said he was. It could be done. Someone had done it. He was walking past me with it on him.

I went back to the shaft the next day and sat in the light and it was the same light and I was the same thing under it, and I understood that the difference between him and me was not the light. It was that the light had been allowed to reach him. Nothing had been withheld in his case except the knife, and the knife had never been near him.

My brother, the other one, the one in the dark with my face, asked me where I had been. I told him. He said, and I give thee his words as I have them, that I had seen a man on a horse, and that the horse had more to do with me than the man did, because the horse at least was allowed to have its hair. I laughed. It is one of the very few times in my life I have laughed at something he said instead of at something he was, and he heard the difference, and did not say anything cruel for the rest of that day. We were still the same child, then. Very nearly. The seam had begun but the cloth had not yet parted.

Here is why I have told thee this before the rest, out of the order I promised.

The first thing I did as this room's keeper was to have a torch made. I had come up at last; the Queen was gone and the seats were empty and the city had no one, and I stood here for the first time with my hood on and my horns under it, and before I had a name the city would use, before I had put one man in the ground, I sent to the smiths and the priests. A sentry's torch, with an incantation worked into its flame, so that any man who carried it on the walls of this city could see through a veil. I had it furnished, the record says, on behalf of the tree and the Grace-Given Lord, so that a Night of Black Knives would never come again. I will tell thee about that night when we reach it; thou knowest what it was. I will only say here what I have never said, which is who the torches were for. The city thought they were for the tree. The Erdbishops thought they were for the throne. They were for a man on a horse who did not look down, and who was killed by things that came veiled, and I could not undo that, so I did the only thing a keeper of walls can do for a brother he saw once. I made sure no one would ever see him killed twice.

That is the memory. Thou hast it now. Put it somewhere it will not get wet.

I never saw him again. I built a city's torches so no one would see him killed twice.
