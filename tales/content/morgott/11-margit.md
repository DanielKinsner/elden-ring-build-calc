## XI. Margit

The gold on the floor beside my hand is thinner than it was when I began. I have noted it; I know what it means, and I will not say so yet. Now the other name. Thou hast met it first, on a bridge, in the rain of my hammer, and it is the name most of the world has for me, and it was never a lie. It was another gate in the hood.

Hear the difficulty. A veiled lord cannot be seen on a road. The whole of the city's peace rested on a tall old man in gold who did not show his face and did not leave this room except for the wall, and the war did not stay at the wall. It came up the roads. It came in ones and twos, after the alliance rotted; not hosts now but champions, men with a name and a sword and a claim, who had heard that there was a chair at the top of the world with no one in it and had decided that the tree would want them in it. They had ambitions. The record's word, and mine. Ambitions for the tree, or for lordship. And they came up the roads to the capital with those ambitions lit in them like lamps, and the sentinels could kill some, and the knights could kill some, and there is a kind of man who cannot be killed by a wall, who must be met on the road by a thing worse than the road. I was that thing. I could not be it in the hood.

So I went out as the other thing.

The body first, exactly, because the city's account of the Fell Omen is a monster and it is not wrong and it leaves out the only part that matters. I took off the gold. I took off the hood. I wrapped myself in a cloak of fur, ragged, the kind of thing a beast might have left on a fence, and under it I was bare. Horns. Tail. The whole old shape. Everything the Order had put under the ground, walking up a road in the dark with a staff in its hand. The cloak did not hide me; it is worn about the exposed body, the record says, and that is exact. It marked me. It said: this is not a man in a hood. This is the thing itself. And the thing itself came up the road toward the champions with their little lamps and did what a thing does.

The name. I will give thee the reason I have given myself, and thou mayest weigh it. I needed a name that was not the king's, so that no one who met the thing on the road could carry the name back to the room. And I needed a name that I could answer to in the dark without thinking, because a name one has to think about is a name one forgets in the moment the sword comes. Margit. Near enough to mine that my body turned when it was called. Far enough that no one else's would. That is all. I have heard the Order's scholars make more of it, and the men over the fog, and I let them; a thing that hunts on roads is glad of any mystery it can get. But it was a working name. It was the name of a tool. I chose it the way one chooses a handle.

And the other word, the one they put after it. Fell.

They meant it as the city means things. Savage. Deadly. A fell beast, a fell blow. The Fell Omen: the horned thing on the road that stacks the corpses of heroes. I heard it first in a tavern under a grate I had once lain beneath, said by a soldier who had seen me from the wall, and I let it stand as I have let everything stand, and then I found that I could not stop hearing the other floor of it. To fell. To bring down. I felled them. I said as much to thee, in this room, in the words I had chosen for thy grave, and thou wilt have thought it a boast, and it was, but hear it now on its lower floor: I was the Fell Omen because I was a thing that felled, and I was a thing that felled because I had been born felled, put down, laid flat under a city, and the only way I ever found to stand was to put others down in the road. I do not offer that as an excuse. I offer it as the shape of the word.

Let me tell thee how it went, because thou hast been on the other side of it and I would have thee know the near side.

They came up the road. I have never been able to see ambition as anything but a candle carried into a house that is not yours. Thou wilt say that is a hard way to see a man who has walked a hundred leagues to stand before a tree, and it is, and I saw them so anyway, because I had spent my life watching the light come down a shaft and never once carrying it, and here were men who had picked it up and were bringing it, uninvited, to the door. One puts it out. One does not hate the candle. I did not hate them. That is true, and it does not help. I came up the road out of the dark in the fur with my horns bare and my staff in my hand, and I said to them what I said to thee, or near it: that they were emboldened by the flame of ambition, and that someone must extinguish it, and that it would be Margit the Fell. And then the gold.

I have not told thee about the gold and I must, because it is the other half of what I was.

I could make weapons out of it. Not forge; make. I would put out my hand in the dark of a road and the Order's own light would come into it and take a shape, a hammer, a spear, a great blade, a fistful of daggers, and I would strike with it and it would go back to being light. I do not know how. I have thought about it for an age and the nearest I have come is this: the gold that would not enter me would still answer me. It would not lead me anywhere, the way it led the chosen; it would not be grace. But it would come when called, the way a dog comes to a stranger who says the right word in the right voice, and I had the right voice, because I had learned it from the outside of a grate, and it did not know, or did not care, that the mouth saying the word had horns over it. Thou hast been struck with it. Thou knowest it is real. It was the only thing the tree ever gave me and it gave it to me without meaning to, and I used it to kill the men who came to sit in its chair, and I never once in all those years asked myself whether the tree would have wanted them dead. I did not need to ask. I had decided.

And I had riders.

The Night's Cavalry. Thou hast met them on the roads at night, on their black horses with the black feathers, riding the dim ways with their great blades, and thou wilt have thought them a plague of the land, and they are, now, wandering, with no one to ride to. They were mine. The Fell Omen led them. They were deliverers of death for great warriors, knights, and champions, and I rode at the head of them in the fur with my horns bare and they never once, in all the years, asked me what I was. I have wondered, on the wall, whether they knew. Men who ride with a thing at night, for years, in the dark where the eye is not trusted and the shape of a thing is what one rides beside, come to know it. I never asked them. I did not want the answer, and I have thought, since, that not wanting the answer was the nearest I ever came to being loved by anyone I could see.

Now the thing I promised thee in the prologue I would not pass over. What I felt.

The city says the Fell Omen was a horror. I was. I stacked the corpses of heroes, the monument says, and it is not a figure; there were nights I stood on a heap of what had come up the road and looked down at the lamps going out one by one in the hands of the dead. And I felt a thing, standing there, that I will not call pleasure, because it was not warm, and I will not call righteousness, because I had read enough of the Order's scholars to know what that word is for. I will tell thee what it was, plainly, once. In the hood, in this room, I was a thing hiding. On the road, in the fur, I was a thing seen. The champions saw me. They saw the horns and the tail and the old shape and they did not look away, because a man does not look away from the thing that is going to kill him; and for the length of a fight, on a road at night, I was looked at, by someone, as what I was. It was the only time. I killed them for it and I did not know that was what I was doing and I have known it since the bridge, since thee, since thou didst look at me in the rain with the lamp still lit in thee and did not look away and did not die.

I will not call that freedom. It was not; a thing that must go down a road in the dark to be seen is not free. But I will not call it a mask either, whatever the scholars say. The hood was the mask. The fur was the face.

In the hood I had horns and no one to hide them from. I will not call that freedom. I will not call it anything.
