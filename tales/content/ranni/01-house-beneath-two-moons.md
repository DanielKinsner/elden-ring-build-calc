## I. A House Beneath Two Moons

My mother walked at night. That is the first thing I knew about her, and it remains the truest.

Before she was a queen — before Caria was a word said carefully in golden courts — she was an astrologer. Thou must understand what that meant in the old reckoning. The astrologers came before the sorcerers, as the spring comes before the river. They lived on mountaintops that nearly touched the sky, and they read fate in the wheeling of the stars, and they counted the Fire Giants their neighbors — which tells thee the altitude of their ambitions, that they kept house beside beings the rest of the world called the end of it. My mother's people looked up as a discipline. Down was where one kept one's feet; up was where one kept one's attention.

She walked at night, a young woman with her eyes on the sky, and the sky looked back. She met the full moon. I phrase it so because she phrased it so — not *saw*, never *saw*. One sees a lamp. One meets a power. What passed between my mother and that pale attention I never asked, and she never told, and I have since learned in my own person that such meetings do not translate. But she came down from it changed, with the moon's favor about her like a signature, and the Academy of Raya Lucaria — which had spent an age perfecting the art of being impressed by nothing — was impressed. Charmed, say the kinder chronicles. Conquered, say the honest ones. She mastered their glintstone halls without burning one of them. She led their knights. And the house of Caria, astrologers' stock, mountain people, became royalty between one generation and the next, because a girl went walking after dark.

I was born into the argument that followed.

For a house raised by the moon is a heresy waiting to be named. The Order of gold had one sky and one tree and one appointed way of dying, and here stood a court in the lake-lands that curtsied to something else entirely — that took its law from a cold light the Erdtree did not own. They sent no armies at first. They sent a husband.

Radagon. I will give my father this: he was magnificent, in the way weather is magnificent. Red-haired, giant-souled, a champion out of the gold with a scholar's hunger and a penitent's spine. He came against Caria, the histories agree, and then he was of Caria; my mother met him as she met the moon, and for a time the two skies shared one bed. Radahn came of it, and Rykard, and last and least in stature, I.

And then he left her. I will spend the sentence plainly, since the plainness is the wound: my father completed his war, and his courtship, and his three children, and then the gold that had lent him to us called in its loan, and Radagon went away — away to the capital, away to the queen of everything, away into a marriage and a divinity that had, it appeared, merely been interrupted by us. He did not die to us, which would have been grief. He *resumed*, elsewhere, which is a thing there is no ceremony for. My mother did not rage; I would have found rage easier to inherit. She went quiet the way a great house goes quiet when its purpose is removed — room by room, over years — and she took to holding things. Instruments. Books. Later, and lastly, an egg of amber, of which this testament will say little because I have never once been able to say much. The Academy she had charmed began, in her quiet, to charm itself into other loyalties. The knights kept their oaths; oaths were what remained. I was young, and I watched the whole architecture of my parents' impossible marriage — two skies, one bed — resolve into its governing truth: that the gold had never given us anything. It had *stationed* something with us, and recalled it when the station ended.

File that beside the vanished aunt, love, in the drawer where my earliest lessons lie. Twice before I was grown, the golden order taught me what its gifts were: instruments on loan, with the ownership retained. I would meet the doctrine a third time, wearing a wolf's shape, and by then I knew the drawer to file it in on sight.

Do not mistake what I felt for my brothers. The chronicles make us strangers because our wars made us strangers, but the wars came later, and childhood came first, and in childhood Radahn was simply the largest fact in any room he entered. He loved smallness — that is the thing no ballad will tell thee. The greatest general the age would ever produce gave his heart early to a horse too scrawny to carry him, and rather than give the beast up, he went away to Sellia and learned the magic of gravity — bent the pull of the very earth around his own body — so that he need never abandon his beloved, spindle-legged, entirely unsuitable steed. Men study gravity to move stars. My brother studied it to stay light in the saddle of a friend. Keep that in thy pocket. Thou wilt need it when I come to what the world made of him, and what his falling cost me, and bought me.

Rykard watched. That was his gift, even then — a patience that resembled languor the way a serpent's stillness resembles sleep. He read everything, credited nothing, and asked, of any scheme presented to him, one question only: what does it require? Not — mark this — what does it mean. I would have use for that question, in time. I am not proud of every use I found for the people who loved me. This telling will not pretend otherwise.

And there was a fourth royal child, in a manner of speaking — a story, told in my house the way one tells of a room that has been sealed. A sister of my mother's line, so the servants had it, from the generation before mine: a princess who set aside the moon for the sake of a burning man, and walked out of every book that had been prepared to hold her name. I never learned the truth of her. When I asked, my mother's face would close like a door in snow, and I — being young, being a coward in the particular way clever children are cowards — stopped asking. I note her here for one reason only: it was the first proof set before me that a person could be *written out*. That a house, a law, an entire sky, could simply cease to say a name. I filed the lesson. The heavens would later wish I had not.

Now. The night I am circling.

I was young — I will not sharpen the number; the moon does not care for birthdays and neither, any longer, do I — and my mother woke me with a hand on my shoulder and led me out of the manor by the servants' stair, down through the frost-stiff gardens, her fingers wrapped around mine. I remember being proud. Understand: she had other children, taller, louder; she had an academy, a queendom, a husband made of weather. She had chosen *me* to wake.

We walked past the last lamp. She did not conjure light. That, first, told me something was different — my mother, who could have written her name across the dark in glintstone, chose to let the dark stand. We stopped where the gardens gave out into open cold, and she said — and here I must be exact, for I have kept these words the way other women keep jewels — she said: *Look up, and be still, and if it should regard thee, do not look away.*

And I looked up, and it was there.

Not her moon. I knew her moon by then as children know their mother's perfume — that full silver graciousness that had charmed an academy and lit our halls on festival nights. What stood in the sky above me was its opposite number. A moon like a held breath. Cold, and dark, and veiled — I could see it and not see it, the way thou canst see a figure through frosted glass, certain of the presence, denied every particular. It hung there and it attended. I do not know a better word. Lamps shine on everyone. This regarded *me*.

I did not look away. I want that recorded, not as courage — it was not courage, I was a child with a cold hand in her mother's warm one — but as fact, because everything that follows in this account descends from that one obedience. I stood still beneath a dark attention, and I did not look away, and somewhere in the length of that regard the thing I had been meant to be went quietly out of the world, like a candle relieved of duty.

What did I behold? Thou wouldst ask; thou hast a collector's greed for other people's revelations. Here is the whole of my inheritance on the subject, and I give it to thee entire: it was cold, it was dark, and it was veiled in a mystery I have spent a life declining to profane with guesses. My mother met her moon and it made her a queen. I met mine and it made me — patient. There are attentions one waits decades to deserve.

We walked back in silence. At the manor door my mother knelt — queens do not kneel; mothers do — and looked at me a long moment, and what was in her face was not pride and not fear but *recognition*, the look of a woman finding a familiar handwriting in an unexpected place. She said nothing. She never spoke of it after, and her silence was not forgetting. It was the discretion of one initiate before another. Of all the things my mother gave me — blood, rank, a talent for being underestimated — I hold that silence dearest. She saw what had regarded me, and she did not try to own it, and she did not warn me from it.

She let it be mine.

A house beneath two moons, then. Hers full, and warm as silver ever gets; mine dark, and patient as a debt. The Order of gold would eventually learn there were two of us. It would mistake the second for a resource, as it mistook everything for a resource — a girl, a bloodline, a light in the sky; all of it, to the golden accounting, so much unclaimed inheritance.

The heavens kept books. I would live to correct the arithmetic.
