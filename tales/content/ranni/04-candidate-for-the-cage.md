## IV. A Candidate for the Cage

Thou hast won a throne and declined to sit it. Of all the qualifications thou broughtest to this marriage, that is the one I dwell on. It means that when I tell thee what was done to me — and it was done *to* me; the honors of the great are always done to one, like surgery — I am speaking to the single soul alive who will not ask why I was ungrateful.

They chose me a throne I had not asked to sit. Here is how the choosing goes.

There are, above the thrones of this world, appointments to a higher one. The god of an age does not merely die and leave a vacancy, as honest kings do; the Order provisions its succession the way a careful house lays down wine. From among the demigods, certain few are raised to candidacy — Empyrean, the word is, and the word is beautiful, and I have come to hear it as one hears the beautiful names of diseases. An Empyrean may succeed the god-queen Marika. May become the new vessel of the Order; the next voice of the golden law; the throne behind all thrones.

Of my generation there were three. Miquella, the abiding child, who unmade his own curse daily and was loved by everything that met him. Malenia, his twin, the sword that rot could not stop swinging. And the moon-marked daughter of Caria — myself. Three cages built beside one another, and each of us fitted for ours by a different tailor.

Mark that I said *fitted*. No Empyrean is asked.

Of the other two I will speak carefully, for I knew them mostly as one knows the weather of distant provinces, and this testament does not retail what it did not witness. But the parallel is owed its paragraph. Miquella I met seldom and remember wholly: a mind like deep water under a child's face that would not age, cursed to eternal youth as his twin was cursed to rot — the two of them born of the golden queen's own strange doubling, and both, from the cradle, at war with what their bodies had been dealt. He unmade his afflictions daily and was loved by everything that met him, which I distrusted then and distrust in memory now, love — not the sweetness; the *reach* of it. And Malenia: the blade that rot could not stop swinging, valor of a kind that makes ballads and unmakes provinces. Each of them chosen, as I was chosen, by their own Two Fingers; each fitted for a cage cut to their particular shape — his gilded with adoration, hers forged of duty, mine lined with frost. Three candidates, three cages, one tailor's guild.

I say it not for symmetry but for the lesson I drew even then, watching my fellow-fitted from across the realm: that the Order did not select for obedience. It selected for *magnitude* — for souls large enough to carry a god — and then spent all its craft on the containing, as though magnitude could be indefinitely held. Every Empyrean of my generation strained the cage in the end, each in the grammar of their own nature. I merely strained mine first, and with cutlery.

The tailors are called the Two Fingers.

Thou hast stood before such a thing, so I need not persuade thee of what beggars the persuading: a hand — only a hand — vast, blind, severed from any arm the eye can find, upright in its chamber like a monument to interrupted intention. Two fingers of it stand, and by turnings and tremblings it speaks, and reader-crones in its service render the twitching into counsel. It is the Greater Will's instrument in the Lands Between — so runs the doctrine — the living relay of the outer power that raised the Erdtree and wrote the law of gold. Every Empyrean is assigned her own. Hers, the doctrine says, as a bride has a chaplain.

Mine waited beneath a cathedral on a plateau of my mother's own country, which the Order no doubt intended as a courtesy and I received as a survey-stake driven into Caria's heart: *this ground, too, is spoken for.*

I went to it. I was young; the word Empyrean still had its shine on; and I will confess to thee — because this testament does not flatter its author — that I went *hoping*. Yes. Write it. The girl who had stood beneath a dark attention and not looked away went down those stairs hoping to be seen like that again — hoping the heavens' second glance would be as grave and as clean as the first. I had met one power that regarded me and asked nothing. I assumed its officials would resemble it.

Thou knowest already the shape of my disappointment. Thou hast heard the Fingers speak.

An old voice, repeating itself.

I say *voice* by courtesy. What I found was ritual without a celebrant: the enormous hand, the trembling, the attendant crones translating tremor into scripture with the serene assurance of clerks who have never once been permitted to see the ledger. And the counsel, when rendered — child, the counsel. Fragments. Formulas. The same three assurances in rotating order, like a catechism worn down to the grooves. I put questions to it — real ones, the kind my crone had taught me to sharpen: *what is the law for; who holds the far end of thee; what became of the ones who refused* — and the hand trembled, and the crones smiled, and out came again the first assurance, word for word, as though the asking had never happened.

I have interrogated corpses — thou hast seen my craft — and gotten fresher conversation.

Understand me with precision, for this is testimony, not philosophy: I do not tell thee what the Fingers *were*. I tell thee what I found. Perhaps behind that hand the Greater Will burned vast and attentive and the failure was all in the relay. Perhaps the relay was faithful and the far voice itself was… old. I could not know. I cannot know now. What I knew, and know, is this: I listened for the thing that had regarded me in my mother's garden — some equal gravity, some cold clean attention worth the price of a life — and I heard machinery. Machinery that had, by every sign I could read, been running unattended for a very long time, and had not noticed.

And to that, they proposed to marry my eternity.

Now the terms. Attend to the terms; the terms are where they keep the cage.

To be Empyrean is to be eligible for godhood — so the shine on the word. What eligibility meant, when I paced its edges, was this. A god of the golden Order is its vessel, and a vessel does not choose its wine. The candidate is watched from her marking to her crowning: her shadow — thou rememberest, *given, that the given thing may be watched through* — walks at her heel her whole ascent. Her fate is written into the sky's own ledger, kept by stars the doctrine says are the very handwriting of destiny. Her body, upon election, ceases in any grammar that matters to be hers: it becomes the law's address, the throne's upholstery, housing for a tenant that does not pay but *is paid*, in worship, forever. I looked for the clause where the woman survives the honor. I am a very good reader. It is not in the document.

And the alternative? There is a word for an Empyrean who declines, and the word is kept in no lexicon, because the Order does not name what it does not intend to permit. The candidacy is a gown fitted while thou sleepest. Wake, object to the cut of it, and thou discoverest the seams are sewn through the skin.

I did not decide at once. Legend requires that I woke one midnight incandescent with refusal; legend, as usual, prefers its women sudden. The truth is slower and colder and more like me. I *studied*. Years, my dear one. I attended the readings and let the crones believe me pious. I mapped what was watched and what was not. I learned my shadow's hours — and hated the learning, and did it. I reread my crone's whole doctrine by the new light: what is the least a body must be, for a will to keep house in it — and its corollary, which I now understood had been the true lesson all along: *what is the most a body can be made to hold against its will?* Answer: everything. A body is where they keep thee. A body is the leash's collar-end. Flesh is the Order's jurisdiction — grace flows through it, fate is written on it, gods are made *of* it.

I had been shown, three winters running, that the vessel is habit.

Somewhere in those years of studying — I will not date it even for thee; some hours are mine — the question turned over, quietly, like ice re-setting on a sill. I ceased asking how to endure the cage. I began asking what, exactly, of me the cage could actually hold.

The first thing I did with my new question was commission a helmet.

Thou wilt laugh. Do; it is the movement's one joke, and it is not a joke. My war counselor — thou hast known him; the great slow troll-smith with the courteous voice, Iji, of whom much more and much worse and much better hereafter — was even then the finest craftsman sworn to Caria. I went to his forge with a commission I could not safely speak aloud, so I spoke it in the Carian way: as a problem of materials. *Master smith,* said I, *suppose a mind that must not be read by the readers of minds. Suppose eyes upon it always — old eyes, high eyes, eyes that take their seeing straight to my enemies. Build me what a treason wears.*

He was quiet a long time. He was always quiet a long time; it is the great slowness of his people, and it shames the quickness of mine. Then he said — and here began a fidelity I did not deserve and did not refuse — he said: *Mirrors, my lady. What watches from above sees surfaces. We will give them surfaces.*

He made himself a helm of crystal looking-glass, and thereafter never took it off — not at the forge, not at counsel, not, I am told, in sleep. The world took it for a smith's eccentricity. It was armor against the intervention of the Greater Will and its vassal Fingers, worn by a person committed — the word is exact, and he chose it, and I honor it — committed to high treason. And I knew, the first time I saw his face gone silver behind it, two things with equal certainty: that I was no longer studying, and that the cost of what I had begun would not be billed to me alone.

He was afraid. I knew it; the helm said it for him, every day, in the plain speech of mirrors: *I am afraid, and I am staying.* Terrified of his own treachery, and treacherous anyway, for my sake, at his own slow considered word.

Remember his fear. It was the most honest thing in my service, and this account will come back to collect it.

So stood the board at this movement's end. A candidate, outwardly dutiful, inwardly resolved — not yet upon a deed, but upon a *direction*: that whatever the heavens had fitted, I would not wear. A smith in a mirror, terrified and true. A shadow at my heel, loved and unlistened-to, for I dared not test where his seams were sewn. And above us all, an old voice repeating itself beneath a cathedral, dreaming — if such machinery dreams — of the day its candidate would come willingly to her coronation.

I would come to that cathedral again, at the very end, with a blade out of a buried city. But between me and that hour stood the small impediment upon which every design of mine had, at this point, quietly run aground.

An Empyrean who merely dies is not free. Death, too, was theirs. The Order held the very act of ending — held it locked in a black blade, in the keeping of a beast, behind the walls of the capital of the world.

To refuse a god's inheritance, one needs a knife no god forged.

I went looking for the forging.
