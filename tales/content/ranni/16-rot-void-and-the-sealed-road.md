## XVI. Rot, Void, and the Sealed Road

Read me the itinerary I set thee, as a stranger would read it. Item: enter the Grand Library of Raya Lucaria, sanctum of a broken queen, and open, with the key her daughter discarded in dying, a chest the royal line has passed down its princesses. Item: take from it a ring. Item: carry the ring down beneath the world, across a lake that is one standing wound, past the spawn of a fallen star, up a sealed mountain road, to a cathedral small as a chapel and old as the plateau, and there — item the last — place it on the hand of the woman who sent thee.

A stranger, reading so, would say: this is not an errand. This is an ordeal — the old kind, the kind faiths set their saints. And the stranger would be nearer right than the ballads, but wrong in the one place that matters. An ordeal tests whether the candidate is worthy of the prize. I knew thy worth by then past testing. My road's horrors tested something else, and I will name it before this movement ends, because I have never named it and the naming frightens me still.

The library first. I have kept my mother in this testament like a lamp in a further room, and here thy road and her stillness touched — the only time. Thou wentest in where I could not and cannot; thou hast told me of it once, entire, and I asked thee never again, and thou hast honored that as thou honorest everything. The chest stood where the household's oldest memory said: beside her. Under the amber light. Within the circle of her rocking. Princess after princess of Caria has taken up her ring from that chest, each in her season, always — this is the tradition's marrow — always from beside the throne of the mother. My house does not post its inheritances by courier. The daughter walks in and takes up her future in the mother's sight, that the line may witness itself continuing.

She could not know the hour, or the visitor, or perhaps anything at all — I state sequence, thou rememberest, not mechanism; her state is a wound I do not narrate. But the chest was kept, and the key still answered it, and the ring lay ready. However broken the keeper, the keeping had held. Take that, thou who art now of this house, as the truest thing I can teach thee of Caria: our order fails at the crown and holds at the chest. Thrones we lose. Trusts we keep.

And so the Dark Moon Ring came into thy hands — with my words already on it. Thou knowest which. I wrote them long before thee, when the design's last mile still had one walker: *whoever thou mayest be, take not the ring from this place. The solitude beyond the night is better mine alone.*

A warning label. Thou didst wear it to the wedding.

Then down. I will walk thy road's stations briefly — they are thine, and I had them secondhand, and this testament does not colonize thy story — but each one owes the record a sentence.

The lake. There is a place under the world where the ground is an illness. Scarlet, still, patient: rot not as decay but as *doctrine* — the same red sisterhood that unmade my brother's mind, pooled and waiting, a sea of the world's oldest fever. I sent thee across it. Mark the tense and the pronoun; this ledger does not hide behind itineraries. I sent thee, and thou wentest, and the crossing wrote itself into thy flesh in a script I have watched thee flex on cold mornings since.

And between the lake and the thing that waited beyond it, the road did the strangest of all its offices. Thou hast told me of it in the tone thou reservest for what thou canst not classify: at the rot-sea's far reach, past the drowned cloister, the way forward was no way at all — only a coffin of grey stone, lying at the lip of a great falls of scarlet water, open, waiting, as though the road itself had died at that spot and been decently boxed. And the only passage onward was to lie down in it.

Thou didst lie down in a coffin at the edge of a falls, and let the waters of the world's illness take thee over the brink, blind, to whatever received thee below.

I did not design that station — enter it in the record; that one the deep supplied of its own grim courtesy. But when thou toldest me of it I laughed, and thou wast patient with the laughing, and here at last is its translation. Every threshold of my history, the same fee: the Night asked me into a rite of my own dying; the river under the world took me boxed and committed; and now my consort-to-be, at the very door of my wedding-road, must lie down in a coffin and be carried by dark water past the point of turning back. The old world had one truth it told over and over to anyone who approached anything that mattered, and it is this: *the way forward is through thy own funeral.* I no longer argue with it. I have merely arranged, on the far side of the last funeral, for there to be stars.

The star-spawn. At the lake's far shore, in the great cloister, a thing was waiting that I owe a stranger accounting than hatred. Thou knowest the family: the malformed star, the void's own natural-born, kin to the very calamity that ate the Eternal City's sky an age before. One of that line lay athwart my sealed road — between my buried coffin-path and my moon's own altar, of all the thresholds in the world. Why there? I have my cold guess: that the powers which punish with falling stars had long memories and one standing sentence for skyward-reaching witches, and had posted, at the door of my ambition, the same breed of bailiff they once sent to the Nox. If so, the message was received. I read it. I sent thee against it anyway. Thou brakest the bailiff of the void across thy knee, and I have wondered since whether some clerk of the outer dark is still waiting for the eviction's return receipt.

And then the road stood open — the Moonlight Altar, the high hidden plateau of my own tradition, sealed the age long behind rot and void and mountain. Grass silvered under an old sky. The rises of the moonfolk. And at the plateau's edge, one thing more I must enter into the record with its true weight: a dragon, waiting. Adula. My sworn knight, thou wilt say — I told thee her oath two towers ago. Aye. And when thou camest up the sealed road, my sworn knight met thee at the cathedral of my own wedding with her glintstone teeth bared, and thou hadst to break her.

I have turned this over more than I let thee see at the time. Was her oath false, then? No — hear a colder possibility, the one I credit. Adula swore to my Dark Moon: not to my convenience, not to my guests, to the *Moon* and its coming order. And a knight of that oath, finding a stranger on the sealed road to the sleeping lady's own altar, does exactly what Adula did: stands, and bars, and tests. She could not know what thou wast. Her ferocity was her fidelity. It is the recurring cruelty of my whole design, thou wilt have counted by now, that it kept setting my faithful against my beloved — shadow against lady, smith against wolf, knight against consort — as though some law of the old world, dying, had decreed that everything sworn to me must be spent proving it. Whether she lives, I have not asked. If the night finds her somewhere beyond our sight, perhaps she will forgive my house that we married the intruder.

Let me not hurry past the plateau itself, for thou didst earn its description and it is the one landscape in this testament I may describe as *mine*. The Moonlight Altar: the high hidden shelf of Liurnia, above the mists, behind the seals, where my tradition kept its holiest quiet. Grass silvered and long, moving under a sky that always seemed one hour past dusk whatever the world below was doing. The ruins of the moonfolk stand there — older shrines of the moon-attending line my mother rose from, worn to doorframes and floorplans, their congregations long since folded into legend — and the rises of the old scholars, where the turtle-marked puzzles of my house still guard what small wisdoms we thought worth locking behind wit rather than walls. My family, love: we lock things behind *riddles*. It is the tenderest thing about us. A wall says: thou mayest not. A riddle says: prove thou art one of ours, and everything we have is thine.

Thou walkedst that grass to reach me. No pilgrim had walked it in an age; the seals had seen to that, and before the seals, the simple forgetting of the world below, which had golden business and no appetite for high cold places. I have wondered since whether the plateau knew what was coming up the sealed road — whether ground so long attended by one tradition acquires a kind of hearing. If it did, then it heard the strangest procession in its history: one Tarnished, rot-scripted and void-ashed, carrying a leaden ring past the doorframes of the moon's oldest houses, to wake the last of the line from her seat on a dead god's hand. The moonfolk built for centuries against precisely such an hour, and never guessed its shape: that when the great cold visitation finally came up their holy mountain, it would come on foot, alone, and knock.

Now the naming I promised — what all that horror actually tested.

Not thee. Me.

Every station of that road, love, was of my own architecture. The ring's warning: my words. The lake: my chosen moat. The sealed road: my seal. I had built the approach to my own hand the way a woman builds who has decided — decided in a coffin, in the dark, with fear and doubt and loneliness for witnesses — that no one is coming, and that no one *should* come, and that the little cold arithmetic of her heart will balance forever provided nobody ever again brings a warm number near it. The horrors were not tests. They were *arguments*. Each one said, in my voice: turn back, the solitude past this point is spoken for.

And station by station, thou didst decline the argument. Uncomplaining. That was the terror, love — I can name it now, here, under our own stars: not that thou wouldst fail the road. That thou wouldst finish it. Because a woman who has furnished her whole eternity for one can survive anything except the sound, far down the sealed road, of a second pair of feet, coming on, steady, past every warning she ever posted — and not stopping.

Thou didst not stop. The cathedral was small, and old, and under it something enormous lay dead, and the doll sat upon the corpse with her four hands folded, listening to thee come.

The next movement is my wedding. I have been avoiding it for sixteen. Come, then.
