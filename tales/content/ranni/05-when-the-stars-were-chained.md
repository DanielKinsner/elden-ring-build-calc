## V. When the Stars Were Chained

I have taken, aboard this voyage, to watching the sky for whole watches together. Thou hast noticed; thou art too courteous to say that thy wife stares. Let me give the habit its history. For most of my life, looking at the sky was not contemplation. It was checking a lock.

Understand first what the stars are, to my mother's people. Not ornament, and not oracle merely — *ledger*. The astrologers read fate in the wheeling of the heavens because fate, in their doctrine, is written there: each life a line of light, each destiny a course among courses. And a Carian is raised knowing the corollary that the golden Order would prefer unthought: what is written in moving script can change. The stars wheel, therefore fate flows. Amend the sky, and thou amendest what may be.

Now hear the sentence I was slow to feel the teeth of.

If fate is written in the wheeling of the stars, then a power that could *stop the stars* could stop fate. Could hold every unwritten possibility exactly where it hung. Could nail the future to the sky's rafters, and leave the whole cold ledger of what-might-be arrested mid-line — mine among the rest.

Such a power existed. I had watched it learn to walk. It called me sister.

Radahn.

I told thee to keep the scrawny horse in thy pocket. Take it out now, and set it beside what the chronicles sing: the Red Lion, the Starscourge, mightiest of the demigods, who in his youth went to the star-tumbled wastes of Sellia and mastered gravity itself — and thou wilt see what I saw from the towers of my girlhood, which no ballad has the patience for. The same appetite drove both. The boy who bent the pull of the earth so he need never outgrow a beloved and unsuitable steed, and the general who challenged the very heavens — one hunger, my dear, in two sizes: *that nothing he loved should be taken by mere bigness*. Not the horse by his own weight. Not — I am guessing now, and I will mark my guesses in this movement like snags in ice — not the world he knew by whatever he saw coming for it out of the deep sky.

At some hour of his ascent — the chronicles blur it, and I was in my towers counting other costs; a sister's attention is finite and mine was ill spent — my brother made war on the stars themselves. And won. Hear it as the plain madness it is. He met the wheeling constellations in some contest no record I trust has ever adequately described, and he *arrested* them. The general halted the sky. Fate's ledger stopped mid-entry. They do not sing that verse often, because no singer knows what to do with it: it is too large for praise. It is the single greatest deed of arms in the history of this world, and my family produced it, and I am going to tell thee now what it did to me, and thou must hold both truths at once again — the awe, and the arithmetic. This house holds both or nothing.

For consider what I was, when the news of the halted heavens settled over the Lands Between like a sheet over furniture. A marked candidate, resolved on refusal. A woman whose whole design — thou hast heard its first draft — ran through two needles' eyes: a death no god forged, to slip the leash; and a certain buried treasure, of which more hereafter, to finish what slipping began. And the second needle lay in a city under the earth, in Nokron, whose gate is not a gate at all. The Eternal City opens to nothing but calamity from above: its door is a falling star. Its key hung in the sky.

Hung. Mark the tense. My brother had nailed it there.

I do not know — snag in the ice, mark it — whether he knew. Whether, among all the futures he pinned when he pinned the sky, he saw one line of cold light that was his sister's road and stopped it with the rest, knowingly, the way a general seals a pass; or whether my fate was simply *in the drawer he locked*, one paper among ten thousand, unread. I have turned this question for the better part of an age. There is a version of him that stopped the stars against some horror only he had measured, and caught me in the net as accident. There is a version that knew precisely which niece of the heavens would find the lock inconvenient. And there is a version — this is the one I permit myself least — that did it for no reason a sister could audit at all, out of the simple totality of him: that having warred on everything of a size to fight, only the sky was left. He never said. I never asked. We were royalty; we had people for our conversations, and then we had wars instead.

The Order, mark thee, did not stop visiting merely because the sky had stopped moving. Through the stalled years the machinery of my candidacy ground on by habit: reader- crones on their circuits, emissaries with letters sealed in gold, the occasional delegation come to assess the ripening of the moon-princess toward her throne. I received them all. I want this entered in the record with its full chill, because it is the performance I am least proud of and best at: for year upon year, the woman who had already commissioned a treason's helm sat in her tower and *performed candidacy* — poured the tea, murmured the formulas, let the crones read the stillness of her parked fate and cluck over the general's obstinate sky, and sent them home believing the delay was astronomical, not doctrinal. They pitied me, love. The Fingers' own clerks pitied the poor arrested candidate, waiting so patiently for her fate to resume. I accepted the pity with lowered eyes and used it for cover the way winter uses snow.

If thou wouldst know what a century of that does to a soul, I can report only this: one learns that patience and deceit, practiced together long enough, braid into a third thing that has no name and does not fray. My crone would have called it cold. The Order, had it ever once looked closely, would have called it treason at room temperature. It never looked closely. Institutions read force and read defiance; stillness they file under *safe* — and that filing error, my dear one, repeated across every desk of a golden age, is the reason this testament exists.

Shall I tell thee what it is to have thy fate arrested? Thou, who hast never once stood still since the fog gave thee back?

It is not imprisonment. Prisons are honest; they have walls one can hate. It is *suspension*. The readers could no longer read me — the crones' art gave back only stillness, a line of light parked in the dark, going nowhere, meaning nothing. The Fingers' own scripture of my future — that gilded itinerary, candidacy to coronation — faltered in the same hour; thou canst not guide a fate that does not flow. I will confess the first feeling was black amusement: my brother, of all the heavens' instruments, had blundered into granting me a stay of execution. The Order's clock had stopped. No one could crown me while the sky held its breath.

But a stay is not a pardon. Attend, for this is the movement's freight: a stopped fate stops *all* its branches. The coronation could not come — nor could the escape. My road to Nokron hung nailed among the rest. I was neither the Order's yet, nor mine. I was a word the world had paused in the saying, and there is a horror in that no wall ever taught me: the horror of the indefinite, which does not press upon thee like a cell but *withholds*, like a sentence never finished, like a—

Thou seest. Even now the grammar of it infects me. I lived years inside an unfinished clause.

And here, my dear one, is where this movement earns its place in the account — for I must tell thee what the chained stars taught me, and it is not a lovely lesson.

They taught me that waiting is a weapon the strong may simply confiscate. My brother would not live forever; nothing so large does. But he could outlast *me* — not my years, an Empyrean's years are long, but my nerve, my design's secrecy, my smith's terrified fidelity, the seams of my shadow's loyalty. Every year the sky hung shut, the watchers had another year to find the treason under my stillness. I could not wait him out. I could not fight him — hear the sizes: the man had beaten the *sky*; I was a scholar-princess whose best work was done in whispers. And I could not recruit him. Do not think I never weighed it — he was no friend to the golden leash himself; there was a version of this history where the Starscourge and the moon-child stood together, and I have wept for it, once, briefly, in a body since discarded. But to recruit him I must tell him, and what I had begun could survive anything but being known.

So I did with Radahn what I did with every love this design of mine ever touched. I set him in the ledger. Asset: the stars are stopped; no one crowns me; the plot has time to ripen in the dark. Liability: the stars are stopped; the second needle waits behind a nailed sky; my road ends at a locked door with my own blood standing guard, unknowing, on the far side of it.

And I turned to the first needle. The one that needed no stars. The one that needed only theft, and rite, and a single unwatched night.

The sky would keep. Death, I had learned, was nearer to hand.

One more thing, before I let this movement close, because thou art married to a woman who corrects records and I will not have this one stand crooked even in my favor.

Whatever he intended — against me, or ignorant of me, or for reasons sized beyond audit — my brother's chained sky *sheltered* the years in which I built the worst thing I ever built. Thou wilt hear, in the next movement, what I did while the heavens stood still. Know that it was done in the calm his impossible arms held open. The historians, when they notice this at all, will call it irony. I ask thee to file it where I file it, under a colder heading: *debts*.

I have paid him since, in the one coin left to me. Thou wast there. Thou wast, in fact, the payment. But that beat belongs to a later movement, at a festival, under a released and falling sky — and I have sworn to tell thee this history in the order I paid for it.

The stars would wait. They had been ordered to.
