## VII. The Rampart

I'll tell you about the tree first, because it's the only thing in this that I'll never have the words for, and I'd rather fail at it early.

The city's under it. She was right; a city, walls on walls, gold roofs gone dull, and a wall round the outside so high I went up it in the dark on the inside of a stair that had a crack in it, and came out at the top onto a rampart, and there was a gold light, and I knelt, and I looked up.

M'lord. I've been a tree.

I know what a tree is from the inside. I know the wet coming up and the wind in the top and the not being looked at. And I looked up at that, that thing, that's not a tree, that's the sky with roots, that's gold poured up instead of down, with the light coming off it so thick you could have cut it and hemmed it, and I thought: that's what I was a bad copy of. That's what the clod was thinking of, when he laughed. He made me into a little wrong drawing of that.

And I wasn't angry. That's what I can't get across. I knelt on the rampart with my face in the gold and I felt something go in my chest, in my breast, right in the middle where the pot comes up of a night, but it wasn't the pot, it was the other way, it was something cold coming off the boil, and I've no word. I've tried. I said to you when you came: "M'lord, did you see it? Th-the Erdtree?" And I stammered on it, on the, which I don't, and I said, "I don't really have the words for this," and I didn't, and I don't, and I said the one thing I had, which was the nearest: "I was so — so dazzled. I felt something stir. In my breast."

And then I said the thing I was sure of. "The Erdtree's waiting for you, m'lord. I know it, I do. I feel it in my bones." And I did. I feel it now. "O please. Take the throne."

That's the tree. I've failed at it, as promised. Let me get to the rest.

You came and went from the rampart a good deal, the way you do, and I'd kneel and take a cape off and read the city off it, which is a sad city, m'lord, it smells of old incense and things that were locked up too long. And every time you came I'd have the question further up my throat, and every time you went I'd swallow it, and one day it wouldn't go down.

I asked it about you. That's the first thing to understand. I couldn't ask it about me, so I asked it about you.

"M'lord. Have you ever wished you might be born again."

I said it easy. I said it like a thing you say about the weather. And then I said the rest, quick, before you could answer, which was safe, because you never did: "Well, they say that Rennala of Raya Lucaria has the power to help people be reborn." I'd had it off the book-folk at the gate. The queen in the school with the egg, who could take a person and put them back like a coat you'd cut wrong and start again with the same cloth.

And then, because I'd got the words out and had to do something with your face, I made the joke. "Oh? Me? Reborn?" I laughed. I laughed at the idea of it. "Oh, look at me." I did the thing with my hands, the look at this. "When you're this ugly, well. Being reborn? It'd hardly make a difference, I'm afraid."

That was the joke. I made it so you wouldn't have to. I made it so that if you laughed, you'd be laughing with me, and if you didn't, I could say I'd not been serious.

You didn't laugh. You stood there in the gold and looked at me, and I thought: you've heard the question inside the joke. I don't know how. I thought: somebody's told you.

I'll tell you now, m'lord. I said I'd come back to it and I've been putting it off and there's no more road to put it off along.

'Ansum. My 'ansum. The word she had that nobody else had, the one I put on my face and carried up the cliffs and held in front of me in the dark.

I know what it means.

I've known since the lake. There was a woman on the road below the cliff, one of the grey ones, the wanderers, in the rags of a good dress, with a small one at her skirts that had gone the same way she had, and the small one had a face on it like the heel of a boot, and she stopped in the road and looked down at it out of whatever's left in them and said, "Come here, my 'ansum," in a voice like a door that hadn't been opened in years. And it went to her. And they walked on.

It's what they say. Down along the coast, where she must have got her words from, off the drowned, off the pockets. It's what you say to anybody you're fond of. To a small thing with a face like a boot. To a dog, I shouldn't wonder. It doesn't mean what I'd put it to mean. It means mine, near enough. It means come here.

I sat on the cliff a long time after that with the needle in my hand.

And here's what I decided, and you can call it what you like: I decided I didn't care. She said it to me and to nobody else, whatever it meant. She looked at me with that face like mine and held her head up and said it. And if what it meant was come here, mine, then that's a thing worth having been told, and if what it meant was the other, the thing I'd made it mean, then I'm the only one alive who knows, and I'll keep it. Both. I'll keep both. Forgive me my convenient delusion, m'lord. I know exactly what it is. It's mine and I've earned it.

So when I asked you about being born again, that's what was under it. Not make me beautiful. I'd given up on beautiful, on the cliff, with the grey woman and her small one. It was: somebody once looked at me and said a word, and there's nobody left who will, and the one person in the world I'd want to hear it from is a person who doesn't speak.

I couldn't ask for that. You can't ask for a word. It's not a thing that can be handed over. So I asked for the other. I asked for the thing that comes in a pouch.

You went away. And I knelt there, and it wouldn't go back down, and when you came again I did the thing I'd sworn I'd not do, which was ask you straight.

"M'lord." I looked up. "In all honesty. What do you think of me."

You didn't say. I knew you wouldn't. I said it anyway, all of it, the whole of what was in the throat. "Am I fit to serve a lord such as you. In all my ugliness." And then the thing under that, which frightened me to hear: "Must I be reborn? No matter what ill fate awaits me?"

Because I'd heard about the egg. I'd had that off the book-folk too. That what comes out of it is not always what went in. That the little ones round her feet are the ones who came out, and went back, and came out, and that they sing because it's what's left when the rest has gone. I knew. I want that clear, m'lord, so that whatever comes of this you'll not think it was done to me. I knew there was an ill fate in it. I asked you anyway, and you looked at me, and said nothing, and I said, "Oh, I'm such a fool. I don't even have answers to the simplest of questions."

And I didn't. Whether I was fit. Whether I should. Whether the wanting was a reason. Simple questions. I'd have given the golden needle for one of them answered, by anyone.

You brought it back in your hand.

I knew it before I saw it. There's a thing they carry, the silver things in the deep places, a drop of something that's not quite alive and not quite not, and the book-folk had told me it was what she used, the queen, what the egg ate. They're not common. You don't come across them. Somebody has to go down into the dark under the world and take one off something that doesn't want to give it up.

"M'lord," I said. "What's this."

And then: "Oh. Oh."

I said, "It can't be. For my rebirth?" I said, "But these are precious. Are you certain that it's for me." I said, "Oh," again, and I said the thing I'd been going to say to whoever it was that ever answered the question, and it turned out to be you: "M'lord. How did you know?"

Because you had. You'd known. Somebody had told you, or you'd heard the question inside the joke, or you're a person who hears what small things say when they say something else; but you'd gone down into the dark and come back with the exact thing, the thing that comes in a pouch, and put it in my hand.

And I told you the truth, then, or a version of it, the version that fit in a sentence. "It was my only wish. That I might honour you with a decent appearance."

That's what I said. And I'll tell you what I meant, in here, since you can't hear the difference. I meant: I wanted to look like something that could belong to you. I meant: I've knelt by your road in a dead man's hat with a face like this, and every time you stopped I've thought, he's got to stand there and be seen stopping for that. I wanted to be a thing you could be seen with. That's the whole of it. That's what a decent appearance is. It's not for me. It was never for me.

Here's the part I'd not say to your face.

If you'd said it. Not with a needle, not with a tear off a silver thing from the dark. If you'd stood on the rampart in the gold and looked at me and said, *Boc, you're — * anything. Any word. Fine. Alright. Not that ugly. If you'd said, stay.

I'd have stayed. I'd have stayed till the tree came down. I'd have knelt at that light till the city went to ash round me and you never came back, and I'd have been happy, m'lord, that's the thing, I'd have been happy, with your hem in my hands and nothing else.

But you don't speak. And I'd asked for the other thing. And you gave me what I'd asked for, exactly and at cost, which is what a lord does, and it's on me, all of it, that I asked for a thing in a pouch when what I wanted was a word.

I'm not sorry. I want that with the rest. I'm not sorry you gave it me.

"M'lord," I said. "It shouldn't take me long."

I got up off my knees. I put the needle through the mantle where it goes. I put the tear in the hat, in the band, under the feather, where nothing would get at it. I said, "I'm off to see Rennala of Raya Lucaria."

And I said — I've gone over this, I've gone over it all the way down the hill and across the lake, and it's not much of a thing to have said last, to the person you've said everything to — I said:

"I'll be reborn. Fresh and new."

Like a loaf. Like a shirt. That's the last thing you heard me say, m'lord. Fresh and new. I'd have done it better if I'd known.
