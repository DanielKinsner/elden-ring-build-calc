## II. What Happened After

I'll do this part quick. I've done it slow enough for the both of us.

She got wisht. That's another of hers: wisht. It's when the colour goes out of a thing and it sits with its shoulders in. She got a cough that came from further down than a cough should come from, and her head stopped being up, and one morning at the mouth of the cave with the light coming in grey across her I said, "Mum," and she didn't.

I don't know what they do, the tribe, with the dead. I know what they did with her. They took her down the beach at low water and left her at the edge of the wrack, where she used to go, and I've thought since that they meant it kindly, that they were putting her with her things. I've also thought they were putting her out. It's the same gesture. That's the trouble with a language that's got no word for how a thing looks. You can't tell a kindness from the other.

I kept the kit. I want that plain. Nobody gave it me; I picked it up off the floor where she'd left it by the mouth, the needle and the roll, and I sat where she'd sat and I sewed. I did the seams the way she did. I did them wrong for a long while and then less wrong. I made a thing for nobody and put it on myself. And for a while nobody minded, or nobody noticed, which in that cave is the same, and I thought that was how it would go on. Small one at the mouth. Hand on his own head, of a night.

Then they minded.

Here are my guesses, m'lord. I said you could have your pick. I'll give them you anyway, and you'll do what you do, which is stand there and let me.

The coat. That I was the only one in the whole heap with a thing on, and a thing with a collar, and that a collar is a way of saying I am not one of you even when you don't mean to say it.

The talking. That I had words and used them, out loud, to the sea and to myself and once, God help me, to a chief, who looked at me the way you'd look at a gull that had asked you the time.

The night. That I stayed at the mouth with my hand on my head while they ran.

Or simply this: that while she was alive there was a big one with her head up standing between me and them, and when she was on the wrack, there wasn't.

I'll tell you what I think, and then I'll tell you it's a guess. I think it was all four and none. I think a heap has a shape, and the shape of ours had a dent in it where I was, and dents get pushed out. That's not a reason. It's what water does to a thing that doesn't fit the bowl.

They've a word. I said they'd no words, and that's near enough true, but there's one they use on purpose. It means out. They said it. The chiefs said it, one and then the other, low, with the knives down by their knees, and the heap behind them said it, and then the heap came and took the coat off me, and the needle out of my hand, and the roll, and everything I had that wasn't skin, and pushed me up the passage to the mouth, and out onto the beach in the day, and stood there in the dark of the cave mouth looking at me, and said it once more, all together. Out. Not to come back. Not ever.

I stood on the beach with nothing on and the sea going in and out. And I'll tell you a strange thing. I wasn't crying. I was thinking: she'd have said something. She'd have had a word for what I looked like, standing there. And I couldn't for the life of me think what it would have been.

I went up the beach and up the cliff and into the country, which I'd never done, and it's big, m'lord. You'll know that. You've walked more of it than any living thing. But you walk it tall and with a sword, and I walked it about the height of a soldier's knee, and I'll tell you what the country looks like from a soldier's knee: it looks like boots. Everything on the roads of Limgrave will kill a small thing for the pleasure of it. I learned the ditches. I learned the long grass. I learned to lie flat and let the boots go over.

I found the hat on the second day.

There's a kind of person on those roads, or there was, that had been rich once, and had walked out of wherever rich people live after the world broke, and had kept walking after they'd died. Slow, grey, shaking things in tatters of good cloth. They don't see you. I'd got so I could walk right past them. This one had stopped for good, in a ditch, on his back, with his hat on. Leather. A wide brim gone soft with rain and a feather in the band gone to a wet grey bone of a feather. And a bit of mantle round his shoulders that had been somebody's fine cloth once.

He didn't need them. I told him so. I said, "You don't need these, cully," and I took them, and I put the hat on, and I stood in the ditch.

A hat makes a person. I knew that from the collar. I want you to understand that I didn't put it on to look like a noble. I'm not simple. I put it on because I'd been a thing with a coat and then I'd been a thing with nothing, and a thing with nothing is what they'd made me, and I'd not be it. That's all. That was the first adjustment I ever made to myself, in a ditch, with a dead man watching, and I've kept it on since, through everything, and it's on now, if you can credit it, on this new head. Of all the things to survive.

Then the clod.

I've thought about this more than about anything except her, and I've less to show for it. I was by the water, on the north side of the lake, where the road goes over on the old bridge. I'd got there because I'd been going toward the big tree in the sky, the one she'd said the city was under, not to reach it, I didn't think I'd reach it, just because it was the one thing in the country I had a word for. And there was somebody on the road.

I asked them for help. That's my whole trouble, m'lord. I ask. I said, "Oi, cully, could you help us out," which is a thing I say, I don't know where I got it, her likely, it's what you call a stranger you'd like to be a friend. And a hand came out of a sleeve. A blue sleeve, I think, or the light was blue, there's a blue that the book-folk carry with them that gets into everything. And somebody laughed. Not cruel. Just amused, the way you'd laugh at a gull asking the time.

And then I was a tree.

That's all I've got. I never saw the face, or I saw it and the tree took it. I've gone over it as bark, as roots, as leaf, and I've settled on this: it might have been one of the blue-light folk from the school across the lake, and it might have been a woman, and it might have been some clod who'd got hold of a thing he shouldn't and tried it on the first small ugly thing that spoke to him. I've settled on clod. It's not the truth. It's just the one I can carry.
