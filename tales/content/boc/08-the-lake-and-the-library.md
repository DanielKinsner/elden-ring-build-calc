## VIII. The Lake and the Library

Down is easier than up. Down the crack in the wall and down the plateau and down the great cliff, and the tree got smaller behind me until it was a tree again, and I'll not pretend I didn't look back at it more than was useful.

The lake at night is a bad place for a small thing. The water comes up over the roads and there are lights in it that aren't lights and men in it that aren't men, and there's the blood, mine, coming up the way it does when it goes dark, and for the first time in my life nobody's hand on my head and nobody's needle to hold, because I'd the needle but I'd my hands full of the hat and the tear in it. I walked the lake with the pot on the boil and my eyes gone red, I could feel them gone, and I didn't run, and I didn't bite, and I'll take that with me, that one. Whatever else. I walked the lake at night with the blood up and I was a seamster the whole way.

The school's on an island. There's a gate on the far side with the blue seal on it, and you'd have needed a key, and I'd no key, and I'll not say how, except that a place built by people who think about the sky doesn't think about its floor. I was in. Stairs and stairs. Book-folk in their hats who looked at me and looked away, which I'd have minded once, and was glad of now. And at the top of it all, a door.

She's on the floor.

I'd thought she'd be on a throne. She's a queen; I'd thought, throne. But the room's a great round library with the books going up out of sight, and there's a chair, a big chair, and she's not in it. She's on the floor at the foot of it, in a heap of robe, with her hair down, and her arms round a thing the size of a barrel that glows.

Amber. That's the word I found for it, in the end. The colour of a thing the sea's had a long time and given back. And it's warm. You can feel it from the door.

And round her, all over the floor, the little ones. In hoods. Dozens. Sitting, lying, curled, walking about with their hands out in front of them like things in the dark, and singing. Not a song. A noise together that goes up and down, and she's got her cheek against the egg with her eyes shut and she's singing it with them, under her breath, and it's the sound of a heap, m'lord. I knew it at the door. It's the sound a lot of small things make asleep round the one warm thing. I'd have known it anywhere.

I stood in the door a long time.

One of them got up while I stood there. Just stood up, off the floor, the way you do from sleep, and looked at itself. Looked at its hands. Looked at the hood on its head as if it had never seen a hood and didn't know what it was for, or whose. Then it sat back down and started singing.

I saw that. I want you to know I saw that and went in anyway.

She didn't look at me. I'll not pretend she did. She looked at the egg. But she said, without looking, the way you'd say it to whatever had come through the door, "Ahh. Art thou come to be born anew?"

I said, "If you please, ma'am."

I don't think she heard the words. I think she heard the wanting. I think that's the only thing she's heard in a long time, from anything, and it's enough for her, and she said, "Be not afraid. There is no pain," and held out one hand, palm up, without opening her eyes.

I took the tear out of the hat band. I'd not looked at it, all the way across the lake. I looked at it now. It's a small thing, m'lord. A drop of something. Not alive and not not. It had a shine in it that moved. I put it in her hand and she closed her hand and said, "Hush, little culver," which I don't think was to me, and the egg went bright.

She was right. There's no pain.

There's a warmth that comes up from the floor and then from the inside, the way the pot comes up of a night, except it doesn't stop at the boil. It goes past. It goes to where there's nothing to boil. I remember the amber going over my eyes, the whole room gone the colour of a thing the sea's given back, and the singing, and I remember thinking, very clearly, in words, the last words I had: there's a seam in the robe under her left arm that's gone. If I'd my needle. And then I hadn't.

And then I had hands.

That's the first thing. Before eyes, before the rest. Hands, on a floor, and I looked down at them the way the little one had, and they weren't mine.

Pale. Straight. Long in the finger. No fur on the knuckle. The nails flat and pink and clean, cleaner than any part of me has ever been. And I thought, God help me, I thought: can they sew. Can they. I turned them over. There was no needle in them. The needle was in the mantle and the mantle was — I felt for it. On. It was on. The hat was on. Of all the things to survive an egg, m'lord, the dead man's hat, gone soft, with the feather. She'd birthed me with it on. I'd have laughed. I found I couldn't.

I found I couldn't do anything with the mouth at all.

I tried your name. Not your name; the word. M'lord. It's the first word in me, it's before Mum now, it's got worn to the front by use, and I put the breath under it and nothing. Air. A throat like a peeled stick that's never made a sound and doesn't know how. I tried Boc. Nothing. I tried Oi, which any fool can say. Nothing.

So I knelt. It was the only shape the body knew. It went down onto the hands on its own and put the face toward the floor and stayed there, behind her, with the little ones singing round it and the egg warm at my back, and I thought: he'll come. He comes to gold lights. There's one by the door.

And you came.
