## V. The Cliff and the Gate

Don't ask me how I got up into the lake country. I'm small and the world has cracks in it, and a small thing that's been kicked enough learns where they are. There's a way up the cliffs north of the big castle that isn't a way. I took it. I had the needle through the mantle where the roll used to keep it, and the hat, and an arm that had come back mostly, and I went up in the grey with my head down, the way you go past soldiers, and came out on top of the world.

You'll have seen it from there. The whole lake under mist, and the school in the middle of it with its blue lights, and the far shore going up and up into cloud toward the tree. I'd never seen so much of anything. I sat on the edge of the cliff and looked at it for the best part of a day and thought: that's where she meant. That's the big place she had the words for.

And there was a gold light on the cliff top. So I sat by it.

That was my whole plan, m'lord, and I'll say it plainly so you don't think it was cleverer than it was. You rested at gold lights. I'd seen you do it. So if I sat by one, on the road you'd have to take, you'd come. And you did. You came up over the lip of the cliff in a coat that had been through the castle and smelt of it, and you stopped, and I got up off my knees and said, "Good to see you! Master."

Master. I'd decided on that on the way up. I'd been calling you you in my head, and cully to your face, and neither would do for the person you'd turned out to be, and I'd not the nerve for the other word yet.

"Do you remember me," I said. "Boc. The demi-human." As if you'd have forgot. As if there was another. "You helped me before. And retrieved my sewing needle."

And I asked you for the thing I'd come up the cliff to ask you for. I said: please, master. Allow me to serve you. As your seamster.

I said I couldn't make anything from scratch. I wanted that said first, before you'd any hopes. I'd never learned. She could cut a coat out of a sail with nothing to go by but the man who'd drowned in it; I could take a seam in and let a seam out and turn a collar and get a cape off a set of shoulders without spoiling the shoulders. Adjustments. I said, "I'm happy to make adjustments to your garments."

You looked at me a while. Then you took the coat off.

I'd like to tell you about the coat, and about all of them, because you never asked and it's the one thing I know that nobody else does, which is what you'd been through, by the cloth.

The first coat had the castle in it. Stormveil, the grafted place. There's a rot up there that gets into wool, a wet grey smell that's got hands in it, and there were burns on the hem from something that spits fire, and a long clean cut across the back that had missed you by the width of the cloth, and I sat on the cliff and took the cape off it while you stood in your shirt looking at the lake, and I read the whole of your week off the inside of the lining. You'd been on your back at some point, in blood that wasn't yours. You'd been in the sea, or the sea had been on you. And there was a seam on the left shoulder that somebody else had done, not well, in a hurry, with the wrong string, and I sat and looked at that seam a long while and didn't ask.

That's what I was for. I want you to see it. Not the capes. Anybody can take a cape off. I was for knowing where you'd been, and putting it right without saying, and handing it back, and having you put it on, and go.

I'll tell you what I read, over the roads. Sea salt, twice. Smoke that was oil. Something that had the smell of a dragon's breath, which is not like fire, it's like iron left out in rain. Red dust that got into everything and wouldn't come out and I gave up on. Wet rot from somewhere low. Blood that had gone the wrong colour. Snow, once, at the end, though I'll come to that. You were never in the same place two coats running and every one of them came to me on a cliff or by a gate with the road in it, and I'd take the road out, as far as a needle can.

I never charged. You'll have noticed that too. I said, "I don't ask anything in return," and I didn't, and I'll tell you why, since I'm telling you everything: because you'd already paid. The mushrooms in the pouch and the needle on the moss. The rest was me trying to make the sum come out, and it never did, and I stopped minding.

There was a bad day at the gate.

You'd gone across the lake to the school by then, and I'd moved. I'd found the gold light by the east gate, the big one with the blue seal on it that I couldn't have got through with a hundred cracks, and I sat there, and the book-folk went by in their hats and didn't see me, which was restful, in its way. And you came back from wherever you'd been with a coat on that I'd not seen before.

And I put my hands on it and it wouldn't let me.

I don't know how else to say it. It was old, that cloth, older than the castle's, and there was a name in it, not written, in the weave, and it knew what it was, and my hands went to the seam and the seam looked at me. You'd got it off one of the old ones, the demigods, the ones whose things were still lying about the country after the world broke. I don't know which. I didn't ask. I could feel the size of the person it had been made for and it wasn't a person, m'lord. It was a thing with a lion's worth of red in it.

I took my hands off. I said, "Master, my apologies." I said, "Your wardrobe includes the garb of old demigods, and I'm afraid I can't make adjustments to them."

You just stood there. I don't know if you'd expected me to. I don't know what you expect of me, ever; I've never known; I've filled it in. And I heard myself say the thing she'd said at the mouth of the cave with the green-gold crest across her knees, and I heard it come out of me in her voice:

"My mum told me once. That a royal seamster would do them up in a jiffy."

And then, in mine: "Just wait, master. Before long, I'll be that good, too."

I didn't believe it. I want that on the record with the rest. I said it because it was the shape of a thing you say. But you went away, and you came back, and you'd something in your hand.

A needle. Gold.

I've told you about hers, curved like a fang, worked until it shone. This was nothing like. This was straight and heavy and yellow, and there was a mark on the shank of it, a crest, stamped deep, a shape I'd never seen and couldn't read, and I stood there at the gate with the book-folk going past and held it and said, "Is that a gift. For my undeserving self."

I said, "Thank you kindly. But what on earth could it — "

And I saw it. I saw what it was. "Oh."

I'm afraid I made a noise, then, m'lord. I'm afraid I said amazing, which is not a word for the likes of me. I said I'd never seen a golden needle, not in all my life, and I hadn't, and I said, "With a spectacular royal crest, to boot," and it was, it was spectacular, it was a crest I'd have given the hat to know the name of, and I asked you if you were certain it was for me, and you were, and I could hardly believe it, and I said so.

And then I heard my own voice go somewhere it had never been. I heard it go up. I said, "Have faith in me, master. I'll polish my craft enough that I deserve this golden gift." I said — I did, God help me — "I'll be the golden seamster, Boc." And I put the needle to the old red coat, and the seam that had looked at me opened for it like a mouth.

I don't know where you got it. I never asked. I've thought since that a thing like that doesn't just lie about. I've thought it belonged to someone who'd had to do with kings and queens and had gone off somewhere and not come back for it. I've thought it had a story in it heavier than the gold, and that you'd walked into the middle of that story to get it, and walked out again, and put it in a demi-human's hand by a gate, and said nothing. That's you all over. That's exactly you.

You asked me, later, at that same gate. Or I said it and you let me, which is how we did things. I said, "Do you think that Mum would be pleased? If she knew. That I'm as good as any royal seamster, now."

And then I heard what I'd said and took it back. "Oh. It's still not enough."

Because it wasn't. I could open a demigod's coat with a golden needle, and I still couldn't do what she did on a rock with a fang and a drowned man's sail. "I need to learn how to sew from scratch," I said. "Like her."

I never did. I'll say that now, so it's said. There's no time left for it and there wasn't then. Some things you're given and some things you learn and some things you'd have had to be born to, and I've been born twice now, and neither time as her.

One more thing about the gate, and then I'll get us up the hill.

Sometimes you'd sit at the gold light and speak to nobody. Soft. Your head turned a bit to one side as if there were someone there at the height of your shoulder, and you'd listen, and say a word, and listen. I never asked. A lord's allowed his ghosts. I'd sit at the edge of the light with my hands on my knees and look at the road and pretend I couldn't hear.

Once, though. Once I'd swear on the needle that I heard a woman's voice say my name. Not yours. Not mine. A low one, careful, like someone stepping on a floor that might not hold. "Your seamster," it said. "Boc." And something after that I didn't catch, and I looked up, and there was nobody there but you and the light.

I've a theory. I've had it since. I think there's a kind of person that sits at those lights that my kind can't see. I think she'd been watching me. I think she told you something about me, that day, that I'd not have told you myself; and I think that's how you knew, later, at the top of the world, what to bring. I could be wrong. I've been a tree; I've heard the wind say my name in the grass. But if there was somebody, m'lord, and if she's the one who told you, then I'd like it passed on that I'm not angry. I'd like her told that the small one knew, and didn't mind being watched. It was the first time anyone had.
