## IV. The Cave Again

I went at low water, in the grey, when the heap would be at its thickest round the fire and slowest.

I'd not been back. I'd said I'd never come back and I'd meant it, the way you mean a thing when it's been said to you with knives. And here's the cave mouth, low and wet, and the moss, and the sea talking in the stone, and I stood in it with the hat off, because a hat's a way of saying I am not one of you and I'd need, for an hour, to be one of them.

It's a long way in, in the dark, when you know what's at the end of it. I knew every stone. My feet knew. I'd gone down that passage a thousand times to sit by the fire at the edge and be tolerated, and the feet went down it now like they'd never left, and the rest of me came after, and the further in I got the more the sea-sound went and the more the heap-sound came, that low breathing of a lot of things asleep.

And there was the fire. And there, by the fire, where a chief had put his knives down, was the roll. Mum's roll. With the needle through it, the way she'd always kept it, the point stuck in the leather so it'd not get lost.

I've thought since about why they kept it. They'd no use for it. They'd no use for the coat, either, and I saw that too, in the heap, under someone. I think they kept them the way they kept string. The way you keep a thing that's been touched by something you don't understand. Not to use. Just to have it not be used by anyone else.

I got my hand on the roll. I did. I want you to know I did. My hand was on it and the needle was under my thumb, and then a foot was on my wrist and the fire got very bright.

I'll not go through the beating. You've seen the marks; you mended nothing, but you saw. They're quick, the chiefs, that's the thing about them, they're quick and they've each two hands and each hand's got a knife in it or a fist, and the heap woke up round them and joined in the way a heap does, and I was on the ground by the fire that I'd been born by, being kicked by everything I'd ever known.

They didn't kill me. I've thought about that, too. It would have been nothing to them. I think they wanted me to go out and be seen. I think a thing that's been pushed out and comes back is a thing that has to be sent out worse, so the others know.

I crawled. I crawled up the passage with the roll left by the fire and one arm not working, and the sea-sound came back slow, and the light came back slow, and there's a place just inside the mouth where a gold light stands. You know it. You rest at them. I can't feel them the way you do; they're not for my kind, whatever my kind is. But I can see them. And I got as far as the gold light and I lay down beside it, on the moss, and I said, "Oh," and I said, "It hurts," and I said the second one for a good long while.

You came.

Of course you came. You've a way of turning up at gold lights. I'd not counted on it, I'd not counted on anything, I was lying on my face watching the moss go in and out of focus and then there were the boots. Your boots. I knew them by the mud.

I got my head up. I said, "Oh, what are you doing here." And then the only thing I had, which was a warning, which I gave you as hard as a thing on its face can give anything: "You must leave this place at once. They'll rush in and beat you to a pulp." I looked at you. "You'll end up just like me."

I want to be clear about that, m'lord, because I've never been clear about it and it's the one time in my life I've been brave and nobody saw it. I wasn't asking you to go in. I was telling you to go. I'd got this far on the strength of being of use and I was lying there no use to anybody and the one thing I could still do for you was keep you out of it. And you looked at me. And you went in.

I heard it. All of it. The sea-sound doesn't carry that far but the other thing does, the thing a fight is in a stone passage, that comes back on itself. I heard the heap wake. I heard the chiefs, the sound they make, that's not a word. And I heard it stop, one and then the other, and then a long silence in which I did a thing I've never told anyone, which is that I cried for the chiefs. Lying on the moss with an arm not working. I'd not have said I loved them. I'd not have said I loved any of it. But it was mine, m'lord, whatever it was; it was the only heap I'd had; and the sound of it stopping is a sound I'd not wish on you.

Then the boots came back up the passage.

You crouched. I remember that. You put yourself down to my level on the moss, which nobody does, which nobody had ever done, and you held something out.

I said, "Wait." I said, "Is that what I think it is."

Curved like a fang. Shining. With the eye at the blunt end you could put a bootlace through.

"You got it back for me. My sewing needle."

And that was the end of my composure, m'lord. I'd held it through the tree and the road and the beating and it went, right there, on the moss, in front of you, and I'm not sorry. I said, "What made you go and do a thing like that." I didn't want an answer. I was saying it the way she'd say a thing to the sea.

I told you. I'd not have told anyone else. "My mum was a seamstress. And that sewing kit was all I had to remember her by." I told you I'd always wanted to be just like her. Sweet old Mum. I said it like that, sweet old Mum, in the voice you use for a thing you can't afford to say properly. And then I said the thing that I've thought since was the truest sentence I ever managed, though I managed it by accident, lying on the moss with the needle in my good hand:

"Then I suppose I can't just curl up and die, can I."

You'd think that'd be a big thing to say. It wasn't. It was the smallest thing. It was a seam. It was: here's the needle, and there's the string, and the one goes through the other, and you're still here, so get up.

I said thank you. I said you were very kind. I said it again, the wanting to be a seamster like my mum, because I'd said it once wrong and wanted it said right. And you stood up, and went off up the beach toward whatever you go toward, and I lay there a bit longer with the needle, and then I got up.
