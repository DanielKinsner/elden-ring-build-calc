## III. A Tree by the Water

You'd think being a tree was like being asleep. It's not. It's like being awake with your eyes shut and your ears open and your feet in the ground, and nothing to do with any of it.

I could see, after a fashion. Not through eyes. Through the whole of me, dim, the way you know where the fire is in a cave with your face turned from it. I could hear. I could hear the water under the bridge and the crows and the boots. I could feel the wind take the top of me and I could feel, right down at the bottom, the wet coming up, and I'll admit there was a comfort in that, in the wet coming up. It was the nearest thing I'd had to a hand on my head.

I don't know how long. The light in that country doesn't change enough to count by. I counted boots instead. Soldiers, in the red, going up and down the road in twos, and the thing with the pot on its head that clanked. Big scaly things that came out of the water at night and went back in. A merchant went past once with a cart, whistling, and I called to him and he whistled through it. And a bald man. There's a cave under the road there, by the water, and a bald man lived in it, and he'd come out sometimes and stand on the bank and look at the world like it owed him and hadn't paid. He kicked me once. Not knowing. Walked into me in the dark and swore and kicked. I've never been so glad of anything. It was the most anyone had touched me in I don't know how long.

And I called out. That's what I did, as a tree. That's the whole of what I did. I called out to every boot that went by.

"Oi. You there. Could you help us out, cully."

Every one. And every one went by. Some slower than others. Some I'd swear stopped, and stood, and looked at the road and the water and the crows and the little wrong-coloured tree, and I'd say, "You, yeah, you there, stop pretending you can't see me," and they'd go on. And I'd stand there with the wet coming up and think: I'm a tree. I'm a tree, and a tree that talks, and that's a thing worth a look, surely, that's a thing you'd stop for. And they didn't. And you go on from there to the only place there is to go, which is: it's not the tree. It's me. It's what's under the bark that they're not stopping for. And you say it, to the road, in the voice you've got left. "Why won't anyone look me in the eye. I'm not that ugly, am I."

You'd say it, m'lord. You would. A year of boots and you'd say it too.

A purple thing grew up beside me, toward the end. A leaf like a bruise, low in the grass. People came for that, sometimes. They'd stop and bend and pick it and put it in a pouch and straighten up and go. Not for me. For the leaf. I got fond of it anyway. It was company.

You hit me.

I want that on the record, m'lord, before all the rest of it. The first thing my lord ever did to me was thump me with the flat of something heavy, hard enough that I felt it in the roots, and it was the kindest thing that had happened to me in a year.

"Ow," I said. And then, because I'm what I am, "What'd you go and do that for."

And then the bark went. I can't tell you how. It went the way sleep goes when somebody says your name, all at once and from the outside, and I was standing in the grass by the water with my feet under me and my hands at the ends of my arms and the hat on, God knows how, and you in front of me, big, in a coat that needed taking in at the waist.

I looked at you a while. I'd forgot how, I think. I said, "Hm? Oh. Yes. I remember." And I did, I did remember, it came back in the wrong order. "Some clod turned me into a tree. And you were just breaking the spell, weren't you."

You didn't say anything. You never did. You'll have to forgive me if I go on as though you'd answered, all through this; I've had a long time to fill in your half, and I've done it, I've done it lovely, and it's probably nothing like you.

"Thank you," I said. "The name's Boc."

I told you about the cave. Told you quick, the way you tell a thing to a stranger: pushed out, told not to come back, ended up as a tree, lucky you came along. I could see you looking at the hat. Everybody looks at the hat. And then I did the thing I'd been dreading since the bark came off, which was to go into my pockets, such as they were, and find what I had to give you for it.

Mushrooms. Ten. I'd had them off the wanderer's ditch and kept them in the mantle and the tree had kept them for me, damp but whole. I counted them into your hand so you'd know I wasn't holding any back. I said I was sorry. I said when they threw me out they took everything I owned, and this was all I had to express my thanks. I said I hoped you could forgive me.

You put them in your pouch as if they were worth having. That's the first thing I'll say for you, m'lord, and I'll say it here where it can't embarrass you: you took ten mushrooms off a demi-human like they were coin.

And then I said the thing that changed everything, which I said to be of use, because I could see already that I'd be no use otherwise, and I could see that you were the kind of person a small thing wants to be of use to. I said: if you could afford to wait a while, I could sneak back into the cave. Bring back something of actual value. Then I'd be of some real use to you.

I said it before I'd thought. And then I'd thought, and I said, "Right. But I'll need a moment."

You waited. You stood there in the grass and waited while a demi-human in a dead man's hat stood with his hands shaking and said, "I-I'm frightened of them. So I have to gather myself." Said, "My knees start knockin'. Just thinking about that god-awful cave on the shore." And you didn't laugh, which is more than the clod managed.

That's it, m'lord. That's the whole of why. You'll have wondered, all these roads, why the little one followed you about. That's why. You hit me, and you took the mushrooms, and you waited.
