## I. The Cave on the Shore

Unconditional love. Unrestrained assurance. It must have been a mother speaking.

— inscription on a clay head that says one thing

M'lord.

Don't sit down yet.

I know. I can see it on you. You've come a long road to get here and there's the gold light humming at you from the floor and your shoulders have dropped that inch they drop when you think nobody's watching. Somebody's watching. You've torn the hem again, by the by. Left side, back, about a hand's width. I can see it from here and I can't do a thing about it, which is a new sort of hurt for me and I'd not recommend it.

I can't talk. You'll have noticed. You said something to me just now, twice I think, and I could not get the words up out of this throat, which is a good throat, straight and long and pale as a peeled stick, and doesn't know me yet. So I'm saying it in here instead, where nobody can hear it, and I've decided to say the lot, from the start, because the little ones in the hoods told me one true thing between all their singing and it was this: it goes. You wake and a bit of it's gone. They didn't say it in words. They showed me. One of them woke up and looked at its own hood like it had never seen a hood.

I've a feeling that when you sit down at that light I'll sleep. And I'd rather have got this said first.

So forgive me starting so far back. You never asked and I never offered. A seamster talks about the cloth.

The first thing I had was the sea in a stone.

That's the cave. You've been in it. You went further in than I ever did, and came out the other side with my needle in your fist and their blood on your boots, and I mended those boots later, at the lake, and didn't say a word about whose blood it was. But you went in as a person goes into a place. I want to tell you what it is to be of it, because there's no one else left who can, and it's not nothing, a place. Even that one.

It's a hole in a cliff on the western shore, low and wet, and the sea gets into the rock and talks in it. Not the waves. Under the waves. A long breathing sound that goes on when the tide's out, as if the stone remembers water. When I was very small I thought that was the cave itself breathing, and that we lived inside a live thing, and that it liked us. I've been a tree since. I know more about being a live thing that gets lived in than I'd choose. But I still think it, some days, that the cave liked us. It kept us dry.

There's moss on everything from the mouth to the first bend, and then it gets dark, and then, a good way in, past where the light gives up, there's the fire. That's the whole of the tribe, that fire. Everyone sleeps around it in a heap, and there's a smell to the heap that I can't tell you, because it's the smell of the inside of my own nose. You'd have smelled it. I saw your face when you came out.

Two chiefs. Brothers, or near enough. Big through the shoulders, quick, with a knife in each hand, and they'd move round the fire with the knives held low, and you learned not to be in the way of the moving. They were teasy as adders, the both of them. That's a word from Mum. Teasy. It means the kind of temper that's always just about to go. Everyone in the cave was afraid of the chiefs and everyone in the cave loved the chiefs, because that's what the two are, in a heap, when you're a small thing and the night is coming. There's other caves, I've heard, that have a mother. A big one, with a crown the book-folk gave her to keep her quiet, and a cape, and a staff that throws blue. The ones that have a mother go mad when she's taken. I heard a dead man say so, on the road by a fort. Ours had no mother. Ours had two chiefs and a great deal of teeth.

And it had mine. My mum. Who was not a queen and had no crown, and who is the reason I've got words to tell you any of this in.

She was bigger than me. That's the first thing. Everybody's bigger than me, but she was bigger in a particular way, at the shoulders and in the way she held her head, which was up. Nobody in the cave held their head up. You keep it low round the fire; you keep your eyes on the chiefs' hands. She kept hers up like she was listening for something over the top of the noise, and I used to think it was the sea she was listening for, and now I think it was probably me.

She had a face like mine. I'll say that plainly because I've spent so long on the subject of my face that you'd think I'd not have room for hers. Ears out like the handles on a pot. Mouth too wide for the rest. Skin the colour of the underside of a fish. But it was her face, and it was the face that looked at me, and so it was the first face I knew and the one every other face got measured against, and I'm afraid that by that measure the whole world has come up a bit short.

And she had the kit.

I don't know where it came from. I asked, once, the way you ask when you're small and every answer is a story. She said the needle was a fang off something that had died in the wrong place. She said it like a joke and I took it like a fact and I've never been able to get the two apart since. It's a big needle, m'lord. You've held it. Curved like a claw, near as long as my hand, with an eye at the blunt end you could put a bootlace through, and it had been worked and worked until it shone even in the dark, because she'd never let it go dull. And there was a roll of tools with it. Little things. A blade the length of a finger. Bone pins. A wedge of something for pushing seams flat. String.

Everyone in the cave had string. It's a demi-human thing, string. We pick it up. Off the beach, off the dead, out of the wrack, and we don't know what for, we just carry it about wound round a wrist, and if you'd asked any of them what it was for they'd have looked at you the way you look at a gull. Mum knew what for. That's the whole difference between her and the rest of them, and it's the whole difference between me and the rest of them, and I've had a lot of time to think about whether it's a big difference or a small one and I still don't know. Everybody had the string. She had the needle. She put the one through the other and made a thing that hadn't been there before.

What did she sew? That's a fair question, in a cave where nobody wore a stitch. The sea brings things. Sails. Coats off drowned men, with the men still in them sometimes. Sacking. A flag once, I remember, red with a gold shape on it that had gone green at the edges. She'd go down to the wrack at low water with her head up and come back with her arms full and sit by the mouth where the light was, and unpick, and cut, and put together. The tribe didn't want it. I want to be fair to them. They didn't want it and they let her do it, which in that cave was a kind of tolerance, and she'd sit at the edge of the heap of a night with a coat across her knees that she was making for nobody.

For me. That's who it was for. I was her only customer, m'lord. A whole tribe that went bare as fish and one small ugly one at the mouth of the cave in a coat made out of a drowned man's sail, with the seams turned in so they wouldn't chafe, and a collar. I had a collar. I want you to picture it. I want you to picture what the chiefs made of a collar.

She'd fit it on me and turn me round with a hand on each shoulder and say, "There. Now you look like someone."

I'll tell you what she called me, and then I'll tell you why it matters more than it should, and then you'll know a thing about me that I've never said out loud, so listen, because I'm only saying it in here.

Nobody in the cave had a word for what a thing looked like. You'd think that was impossible. It's not. You've got words for what a thing is, for danger and food and mine and out, and that's a language, that's plenty, and the look of a thing never comes into it. A chief is a chief because he moves round the fire with his knives low. He's not a handsome chief. He's not an ugly chief. The word's not there to be had.

Mum had the word.

She'd fit the collar and turn me round and say, "There's my 'ansum."

And she'd say it other times too, not only at the collar. Coming in from the wrack. Waking. When I'd cut a seam straight. "My 'ansum." Like it was my name, near enough, or the other half of it. Boc's the name. It's not a word, that. It's the noise a thing makes when you drop it in a bucket. I've decided that's not why she chose it.

I heard that word and I put it where it fit, which was on my face. She was the only one who ever said it. That's true, that's the truest thing I've got, and I've held it in front of me the way you hold a light in a cave, and it's got me through the dark to here, and I'll come back to it, m'lord, when I've the courage. Not yet.

The night I should tell you about, because you've seen what we are at night and I'd not have you think I was excused.

When it goes dark the blood comes up. That's the only way I've got to say it. Something in the middle of you starts to warm, and then it's hot, and then it's boiling, and your eyes go, they go red, I've seen it in the others, and you want to be running and you want to be biting and there's a joy in it that I'm ashamed of and won't lie about. The whole heap would go out of the cave at once like water out of a tipped bowl and run the beach, and whatever was on the beach was sorry.

It took me too. I'll not pretend. But it took me less, or I liked it less, and I'd feel it start, like a pot coming up, and I'd not go. And she'd put her hand on the top of my head, the whole flat of it, and press down, not hard, and say nothing, and the pot would come off the boil. Every night. Every night she did that, until she couldn't.

I've thought that's why. I've thought a lot of things about why, as a tree, which is a great deal of thinking, and that's one: that I didn't run with them. That they came back in the grey with their mouths red and there was the small one by the mouth with a coat on and a hand on his head. I don't know. I'll give you my guesses when I get to it and you can have your pick. I'd not want you to think I know, because I don't, and a thing I've learned from the cloth is that a seam you pretend is straight will pucker on you dreckly.

She knew things. I'll finish with that, about the cave, because it's the part of her I've never been able to stitch to the rest.

She told me once, sitting at the mouth with a coat across her knees that had a crest on the breast of it, worked in thread that had been gold, that there were seamsters who did this for a living. In a city. Under a tree the size of the sky. She said a royal seamster would take a coat like that and do it up in a jiffy. She said the word royal like she'd had it in her mouth before.

Where'd she get that? She'd never seen a city. She'd never seen a tree, as far as I know, that wasn't the wrack. She got the word teasy from somewhere and dreckly from somewhere and 'ansum from somewhere, and I've come to think that somewhere is the drowned. That she sat with the dead at the water's edge and took their coats off them and, with the coats, whatever was in the pockets, and that some of what was in the pockets was words. I've come to think she learned to talk off the sea.

Or she came from somewhere else. A cave with a mother. A place that had been near people. I don't know. You don't ask the sea how it knows the shape of the shore. I've two guesses and I've never picked, and I'd ask you not to pick either, because she's the one thing I've got that I'd rather keep whole than understand.
