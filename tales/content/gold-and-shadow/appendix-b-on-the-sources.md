# Appendix B: On the Sources

*Being the chronicler's account of his witnesses — what each kind of relic knows, what each kind lies about, and how the weighing was done.*

---

A reader who has come this far has watched me weigh evidence on every page, usually in a clause, sometimes in an aside, and has perhaps wondered whether there is a method under the manner. There is. This appendix is that method, laid out on the table the way a surgeon lays out instruments — not because the reader requires it, but because the reader deserves the chance to check my work. History written from relics is a chain of small judgments, and a historian who hides his judgments is asking to be trusted, which is precisely what no historian should ever ask.

So: the witnesses, one class at a time, each with its particular manner of telling the truth and its particular manner of failing to.

## I. The classes of relic

**Remembrances.** The noblest class, and the most dangerous. When a great soul is rendered down at the Hold's own altar, what survives is a distillate of memory — the things that soul could not let go of, pressed into a form that can be read. I have handled more of these than any scholar living, chiefly because most scholars are not living. Their virtue is intimacy: no other relic tells you what the dead *carried*. Their vice is the same thing. A soul is not a ledger; it is an advocate. It remembers the wound and forgets the provocation, keeps the crown and mislays the price. And a remembrance can do something subtler still, which the reader must learn to catch: it can remember a *reputation*. The dead heard stories about themselves too, and believed the flattering ones, and a distilled soul will hand you the story with the same solemnity as the deed. A remembrance is a witness of perfect sincerity and unverifiable accuracy — the most honest liar in the collection.

**Epitaphs and monuments.** Stone speaks for whoever paid the mason. A tomb tells you what the mourners needed to be true; a triumphal relief tells you what the victor needed remembered, in the order he needed it remembered in. This does not make monuments worthless — grief and pride are facts, and expensive ones, and nobody carves a lie about a matter no one cares about. The size of a monument is testimony even when its inscription is not. But the reader should note what monuments never record: the defeat that taught nothing, the son who disappointed, the war that was merely lost. Stone is a medium with no genre for embarrassment.

**Arms and armor.** The inscriptions on swords, the descriptions handed down with a dead knight's mail — these are the barracks-talk of history, and I love them and trust them the way one loves and trusts barracks-talk. Armorers and quartermasters repeat what the camp believed, and the camp believed what it heard, and what it heard had passed through forty tents on the way. Yet this class has one supreme virtue: it is *uncurated*. No queen commissioned the gossip stitched into a soldier's surcoat. When a piece of armor tells you what the rank and file whispered about their own masters, you are hearing the one register of this land's memory that nobody powerful bothered to edit — which is exactly why, as we shall see, it preserves things the official record was scrubbed of.

**Liturgies, prayers, and incantations.** Partisan by construction. A prayer is not a description of the god; it is an application to the god, drafted by the god's own clerks. Everything a faith writes about itself should be read the way one reads a suitor's letter — sincere, informative, and composed entirely of selected facts. Still, liturgy has a use no other class offers: it tells you what a faith was *worried about*. Doctrines are written against somebody. Find the prohibition and you have found the practice; find the reassurance and you have found the fear.

**The testimony of the living.** I have been fortunate in my witnesses and careful with all of them. Miriel, the vow-keeper of Liurnia — patient beyond the patience of his shell — witnessed the royal vows he guards and will tell you plainly which questions he cannot answer, a scruple so rare in this land that I have sometimes wanted to build him a monument, though see above regarding monuments. Enia, the finger-reader, translated for the Two Fingers in this very Hold; her testimony is invaluable and comes with a caution she herself supplied — that her principals have withdrawn into a communion that may outlast centuries, which leaves her a translator of a silence. And there is the grandam of the Hornsent, the oldest and angriest voice I have, who remembers the making of the god of gold from the losing side. A hostile witness distorts, but a hostile witness also *retains*: she keeps every fact the victors found inconvenient, holding each grievance polished and ready. I cite her often, corroborate her always, and dismiss her never.

**The stones and the bodies.** Last, the class I rank above all the others, though it is the only one that cannot speak. A ring carved into the stone of a dragon's ruined city. A corpse the size of a siege-tower still hanging on the capital's walls. A discarded body in a tower, bearing half a mark that should never have been divided. The contents of the jars. These witnesses have no rhetoric, no patron, and no self-regard; they cannot flatter and cannot mourn. They are the only sources in this collection that never lie — and the only ones that never explain. The stone shows you the ring; it does not say who wore it. The great labor of this book has been marrying the two kinds of witness: the ones that talk and shade the truth, and the ones that hold the truth and will not talk.

## II. Three weighings, shown in full

Method is best demonstrated. Here are three judgments from my ledger, worked in front of the reader.

**The Dragonlord's lordship.** The remembrance of the two-headed dragon of the timeless storm records that he *"is said to have been"* Elden Lord in the age before the Erdtree — and those five words, attributed to the remembrance itself, are the only quotation in this book I permit for its wording alone, because no paraphrase preserves what matters about them. The remembrance does not state a lordship. It states that a lordship *was said*. That is not a fact; it is a fact about a rumor — and the distinction is the whole craft. I confess my own record here, because the confession is instructive: I first shelved the dragon's lordship among my certainties, on the grounds that a remembrance had said it. On a second reading I demoted it — the remembrance had not said it; the remembrance had reported the saying of it. On a third pass I raised it partway back, because the talking witness does not stand alone: in the dragon's ruined city the stone itself bears the device of the Ring, a mute corroborator with no motive. So the claim now rests where it belongs — not certain, not mere rumor, but a strong inference from a hedged tongue and an honest stone. Three shelvings for one sentence. The reader may multiply that labor by every sentence in this book and understand why my beard went white in a library.

**Godfrey's fatherhood.** Every tavern, every commonplace-book of the wandering scholars, every casual history will tell you flatly that Godfrey fathered Godwyn the Golden and the Omen twins. Now watch closely, for this is the commonest failure in all historiography. What the relics actually hold is three separate texts: one Great Rune defines the golden lineage as Godfrey and his offspring; another says its bearer was born of that lineage; a third establishes that the Omen brothers were twins. Chain the three and Godfrey's paternity falls out as the conclusion — and it is a *good* chain; I stake this book's dynasty table on it. But no single link names him father. Not one. The claim everyone repeats as if stated is stated nowhere; it is an inference wearing a statement's clothes, and generations of repetition have ironed the seams invisible. Add the mute corroborator — the golden shade that stands its long vigil over the elder twin's corpse, a tableau that reads as nothing so well as a father's — and the chain holds firm. I record the paternity confidently, and I record it as a chain, because a historian who cannot tell his conclusions from his sources will soon have neither.

**The Numen blood of the Black Knives.** The armor recovered from Godwyn's assassins carries, worked into its very description, the rumor that the knives were Numen women of the Queen's own stock. Note the construction: the armor does not assert the blood; it asserts the *rumor* of the blood. A lazy historian throws such a source away as gossip. A careless one promotes it to fact because it is vivid. Both waste it, for a rumor's existence is itself a datum, and a well-placed one. Someone in the capital, close enough to the assassins to dress their memory, believed the killers of the Queen's son shared the Queen's blood — believed it strongly enough that it was set down on the equipment itself, in the one uncurated register I described above, where no royal editor thought to reach. I cannot tell you the rumor is true. I can tell you it existed, where it lived, and what it implies about what the capital suspected of its own queen — and that is not nothing; on some pages of this book it is nearly everything. Weigh the report, not just the reported.

## III. On deliberate silence

The hardest sources to read are the ones that are not there.

Most gaps in this record are honest — moth, fire, and time are diligent archivists of nothing. An honest gap has ragged edges: fragments survive around it, references point into it, the missing thing leaves a stain. But there is another kind, and the reader of the chapter of the jars has already stood before the worst of them. The record of this land gossips about parentage the way sailors gossip about weather; it is the one subject the relics never tire of. Yet for one red-haired son of the Queen, the record observes a discipline it observes nowhere else — remembrance, armor, banner, liturgy, all reciting the mother and all falling silent in the same place, as if the ten thousand witnesses had rehearsed. Fragments do not conspire. When every class of relic fails at the same word, the gap has clean edges, and clean edges are tool-marks. My ledger holds a rule for this, and I commend it to my successors as the most counterintuitive discipline in the craft: a verified absence is recorded as an absence, forever. However plausible a candidate, however satisfying a guess, the father's slot stays empty, because filling it would replace the one thing the record actually attests — that someone did not want it filled. And the Queen herself is the silence entire: ten thousand statues, and not a sentence of her own explaining any deed of her long reign. I have stopped reading that as modesty. An eraser, wielded so consistently for so long, is a document. It has an author, a scope, and a purpose, and I have tried throughout this book to read the erasures with the same care as the ink — for what a power destroys is the most honest record of what it feared.

## IV. Against the single account

Last, a word on the architecture of this book, and then a bequest.

The reader will have noticed that where my witnesses disagree, I do not referee. The braid and the grandam, the two framings of the Blood Lord's abduction, the six traditions of the ending — I set the rival accounts side by side, each linked to its opponent, each pointing at the other across the page. This is not indecision, and I want it understood before I am accused of it. When a historian chooses between two live witnesses, the loser's testimony begins to die; copyists drop it, abridgers skip it, and within three generations the choice has become a fact and the fact has always been true — the reader has seen in these appendices how a chain becomes a statement and a rumor becomes blood. The only defense is structural. Every disputed claim in my ledger carries, written into its own entry, the name of the claim that contests it, so that neither can be consulted without the other tugging at the reader's sleeve. Disagreement, honestly preserved, is not the failure of a history. It is the load the history was built to bear.

And so the bequest. This collection is open. I have graded every claim; I have regraded twenty of them when better reading shamed the first grade, and retracted outright a judgment I had thought among this book's firmest — and each correction was paid for out of the certainty I carried into this library, a purse I found empty by the time the ash reached the shelves, and have not once missed. Whoever comes after me must expect to regrade twenty more — the ledger was built to be corrected, and a correction is not a defeat but the whole point of the instrument. If a new relic surfaces from the shadowed land, weigh it by the classes above and enter it. If one of my inferences fails under a witness I never met, strike it and record why. History is not a monument; monuments, as established, are the least reliable class. History is a house one keeps repairing — the beams I trusted will sag, the room I never finished waits for your lamp, and the door, scholar, whoever you are and whenever you come: I have left it unlocked.
