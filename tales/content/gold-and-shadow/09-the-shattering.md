# Chapter IX: The Shattering

*Wherein the queen who was the law takes a hammer to it; her other self tries to undo the blow and cannot; her children divide the wreck of heaven among themselves and are divided by it; and the age ends as it began — with a summons.*

---

Every order whose death this history has recorded was murdered from without. The dragons' dominion cracked before a fleeing god; the spiral tower burned under a crusade of gold. The Golden Order died differently. No invader breached it, no heresy outgrew it, no outer god pulled it down. It was revoked — withdrawn from circulation by the same authority that had issued it. This chapter concerns the revocation: the act, the failed repair, the punishment, and the war of inheritance that followed. And it must open with a confession of poverty, because the record here is generous and stingy in exactly the wrong proportions. Of the deed, we know nearly everything: the instrument, the sequence, the consequence. Of the reason, we know nothing at all.

## The hammer

**Queen Marika the Eternal shattered the Elden Ring.** She did it herself, and she did it with a hammer — a queen's tool of gilded stone, which splintered against the blow and took slivers of broken rune into its own wounds, so that the weapon and the crime fused into a single relic. The reader will recall what the Ring was: not a crown's ornament but the law of the world made into a thing, the terms on which water ran downhill and the dead stayed dead — those terms, at least, that the Order had not already amended. For an age beyond counting, Marika had been that law's vessel, its living warrant, the body in which it sat. Then she raised her arm and struck it.

As to *when* — the record does not date the blow, but it orders it, and the ordering can bear weight. The sorcerer **Rogier**, who spent his last strength tracing the Night of the Black Knives, names that murder the origin from which the Shattering flowed: first the prince died, then the world broke. Plain sense stands behind him, for no child of Marika's could have seized a shard of the Ring while the Ring was whole, and seize them they demonstrably did. I lean on Rogier and on that reckoning, and on nothing else — the reader should know that the account of these years most often recited aloud, the sermon taught to the returning exiles, tumbles its events together like dice in a cup, and cannot be trusted for sequence. So: the Night first, the hammer after. The interval between them — a day, a decade — no relic measures.

*On the sources, and on the largest silence in this book.* Why did she do it? Here every witness this library owns falls quiet at once, and I have come to believe the quiet is hers. The reading the whole land favors is grief: **Godwyn the Golden** was her beloved son of the golden line, the first of her children ever to die, and dead in the foulest way — soul slain, body left living — and it may be, perhaps, that a mother looked at the law that had permitted this and found it deserved breaking. It is a humane reading, and here I must stop the chronicle and report a fault in the chronicler — for the second time. Twice now — the reader will remember the night of knives — I have caught my hand on this scale, and both times the queen was in the pan. Once is a lapse; twice is a shape: the scale never slips for Radahn or the Praetor, only where Marika stands, and only toward the story I could forgive. A mother breaking the world over a murdered son is such a story, and I want it true; and wanting is not weighing, whatever else it may be. So let the wanting be named again, and denied again the promotion it asks for: the evidence for grief is adjacency — murder, then shattering — and grief merely the bridge we find easiest to build between them. A wish does not petrify a bridge into a road. I set the reading down at the weight the relics give it, which is none — and the pattern beside it, for the reader to subtract wherever this queen is weighed hereafter. Some scholars venture a colder architecture — that the blow was rebellion long in the drafting, a queen striking not at her grief but at the Greater Will that held her, with the Night as occasion rather than cause; they note how much of what follows resembles a plan, and I note in return that resemblance is not testimony, and nothing in the record compels it. The queen who explained no deed of her long reign did not make an exception for her greatest. I will not invent the sentence she declined to speak. She broke the world's law with her own hand; the hand is certain, and the heart is closed to us, and the historian who tells you otherwise is selling you his own heart, not hers.

## The mender

What happened next is the strangest passage in this history. For when the Ring lay in pieces, a god came to mend it: **Radagon**, second Elden Lord, the red-haired fundamentalist who loved the Order with a scholar's whole ferocity. He took up the very hammer that had done the breaking — and the hammer never changed hands, for the one god quarreling with herself has only ever had one pair.

The same god struck the Ring and then knelt in the wreckage trying to fit it back together — one self unmaking, the other undoing the unmaking, the quarrel of an entire age conducted inside a single body. He failed. The relics say the mending was attempted and say it was not achieved, and they do not say why the strength that sufficed to break did not suffice to bind. I decline to dramatize what no witness heard. But I will observe, because it is observation and not invention, that everything the reader needs to know about the Golden Order's end is contained in this one image: it was not overthrown; it argued with itself, and lost.

## The sentence

Punishment followed, and for once this history can cite an officer of the court. **Enia**, the Finger Reader — who interpreted for the Two Fingers in this very hold, in a chamber I pass each morning — attests that the queen's trespass demanded a heavy sentence, and that the sentence was served: Marika was sealed within the Erdtree itself, entombed in the golden heart of her own monument. The Two Fingers pronounced it, speaking — so they had always claimed — on behalf of the Greater Will. Whether heaven decreed the sentence or only heaven's clerks is a question this book must hold open a while longer.

Mark what the sentence made of the world. The god of the age was now the age's prisoner. The Erdtree, which had signified shelter to half of creation, became a jail with one inmate. And the Order's officers had spent their last clear instruction on punishing their own god — after this one act, as Enia describes it, the communion of the Fingers began to slow, and falter, and starve. The land now had a law in fragments, a lawgiver in a cell, and a priesthood straining to hear a voice that no longer answered. Into that vacancy came the heirs.

## The wars of the shards

The fragments of the broken Ring are called the **Great Runes**, and Marika's children and stepchildren gathered them up as heirs gather plate from a dead house — each taking the piece nearest to hand, each finding that the piece took something back. For the runes carried a taint with their power, a maddening residue of broken law, and the record is unanimous that the demigods who bore them were warped in the bearing. What followed was the war this whole land still lies wrecked by: the Shattering. No annal of its campaigns survives, if one was ever kept; what we have are episodes, carved on sword-monuments and remembered in remembrances, and I will give the episodes as the relics give them — brilliant, terrible, and refusing to add up to a whole.

**Starscourge Radahn** must come first, because his legend does. A son of Radagon's first marriage — Carian-born, brother to Rykard and Ranni, stepchild to the queen — he was reputed the mightiest of all the demigods, though I note the reputation reaches us chiefly through men who loved him. Two facts anchor the legend. The first is small and, I find, the key to him: as a boy he learned the sorceries of gravity in Sellia for no grander purpose than to keep riding Leonard, the scrawny steed he refused to outgrow. The second is not small at all: in his prime he made war on the sky, challenged the swirling constellations, and *arrested them* — stopped the stars in their courses above his own head. Why, no relic says; the record hands us a man who halted heaven and forgot to note his reasons. But the Carians counted the cost, for their fate is steered by the stars, and **War Counselor Iji**, who served that house, understood the theft precisely: with the stars chained, the destiny of Radahn's own sister Ranni stood frozen, and would stay frozen until his death. When the Ring broke, this same man took his rune and made Redmane Castle the fist of the south.

Against him came his half-sister. **Malenia, Blade of Miquella** — child of Marika and Radagon both, and therefore — as the capital's hidden stone taught us to say — child of a single god — had carried the scarlet rot in her flesh from birth and spent a lifetime refusing it. Her legend carries a boast, and I give it as legend's own words: *"I have never known defeat."* She marched her Cleanrot Knights into Caelid to meet Radahn's host, and at the **Battle of Aeonia** the two undefeated armies of the age ground each other to the bone — until Malenia loosed what she had spent her life holding shut. The scarlet flower blossomed on the battlefield. Caelid did not survive it. What the flower made of that country, the travelers' accounts in this library describe with one voice, so I will set the scene down as they give it: a body's interior expanded into geography. The trees blistered coral-red, bark splitting into wet bloom, boughs fruiting growths no orchard would own. The rivers thickened to scarlet mire and thicken still; the pools scummed over vermilion; the air hangs with a pollen that settles on the tongue like the first hour of fever. Carrion swells there past the proportion of its own bones and is not permitted to finish dying, and the beasts that feed on it swell after it. Over all of this stands a sky the color of an old wound — not the red of fire; the red of something that has stopped healing — and beneath that sky everything that still lives has learned the country's one lesson, which is to bloom by dying: flesh flowering, flower feeding, decay conscripted into a kind of spring. It is a wound in the east that has never closed, and it rots still. The sage **Gowry** calls it the flower's *first* great bloom — but Gowry is a partisan of the rot, a man who prays for its flowering the way farmers pray for rain, and a witness in love with his subject counts his subject's triumphs generously; I record his ordinal and leave it in his keeping. Neither champion won. Malenia fell into slumber amid her own bloom, and one knight — **Finlay** of the Cleanrot — carried her sleeping body out of the wound she had made and home to the Haligtree, alone, a march the relics honor and do not describe. Radahn survived, if the word will stretch: the rot devoured his wits and left the mightiest of the demigods wandering the dunes he had ruled, eating carrion, a general with no name left in him — stopped, like his stars, in his own sky. **Witch-Hunter Jerren**, his old comrade, attests what he became; what Jerren and the Redmanes eventually did about it belongs with the festival they would one day hold in his honor, and is among the kindest things in this book.

**Praetor Rykard**, Radahn's brother, fought the war by other means, and I must ask the reader to attend, because what he did was not madness, whatever it was besides. Lord of Volcano Manor on Mt. Gelmir, he chose for his weapon the **God-Devouring Serpent**, an ancient terror imprisoned beneath the mountain — old enough that a great spear had been forged in some forgotten age for the sole purpose of slaying it. Rykard did not loose the serpent on his enemies. He went down to it. Below the manor's parlors and courtesies the mountain is already half-swallowed: galleries of rock lacquered smooth and black by old heat, floors that glow along their seams, magma moving under the crust with the patience of a thing swallowed and not yet dead. Down through that gullet of the earth the Praetor carried himself like an offering walking to its own altar, and at the bottom he *fed himself to the serpent* — flesh, will, and Great Rune together, given over in stated defiance of the Erdtree — and became one with the beast: no longer a lord who kept an appetite, but an appetite that kept what it required of the lord, a hunger fit to devour the very gods. Blasphemy, the Order called it; **Tanith**, his consort, who keeps his manor and his cause to this day, would have us hear it as doctrine — a deliberate exchange of manhood for the one appetite the gods must fear. The capital, at any rate, treated it as war: the flanks of Gelmir are littered even now with abandoned siege-works and the dead of a great host, and by every sign that survives, the offensive broke against the mountain and was not renewed. In Rykard's own hall, the old serpent-hunting spear still leans where some hand left it — the one blade equal to the task, brought to the threshold and never driven home.

And in Leyndell itself, the war produced its bitterest symmetry, for the capital was defended and undermined by two halves of a single injury. The reader has met **Morgott** and **Mohg** already, as infants: twin sons of the queen, born Omens — horned, and therefore cursed in the Order's eyes — and hidden away in the Shunning-Grounds beneath the capital, royal blood jailed in the sewers of its own inheritance. In that darkness the twins found opposite answers. Morgott found faith. When the Ring broke he came up out of the dark, took his rune, appointed himself protector of the Erdtree that had never once acknowledged him, and held Leyndell against siege through the whole of the war — the outer fields are a graveyard of trebuchets and heaped bone to this day, and the wall was never carried. Which demigods led those sieges, the monuments do not say. The remembrance carved for Morgott's soul states his tragedy without flinching: he gave the Erdtree his love, and no love was ever returned. He asked nothing of his shard but a throne to guard; he became a king by process of elimination, ruling a capital of veterans and ash-blown banners for an Order that would not have seated him at its table.

His twin stayed below. Mohg had met something in the deep dark — the **Formless Mother**, an outer god of blood, wounded and hungry — and his accursed blood took fire in her service. His war was patient and abominable: he crept into the Haligtree, cut open the cocoon in which the Empyrean **Miquella** slept, and stole his half-brother's dormant body away to a palace sunk beneath the earth, meaning to raise the boy to godhood and stand beside him as consort — first lord of a dynasty of blood. The abduction is certain; the master of this library traced it himself, and his notes lie at my elbow as I write. Its date is not — though Malenia returned from Aeonia to keep vigil over a cocoon still whole, which sets the theft after her fall, if the vigil is rightly told. What Mohg's devotion earned him, the remembrance of the Blood Lord states with terrible economy: however he shared his bedchamber, the young Empyrean never once responded. A courtier below the earth, preaching a dynasty to a sleeping god who would not wake for him — the war's cruelest front had no battles at all.

## No victor

Set the pieces on the map as the fighting guttered out, and read the result. Godrick, last grafted twig of the golden line, cowering behind Stormveil's walls. Radahn mindless in the red dunes. Malenia asleep at the root of the Haligtree. Rykard coiled beneath his mountain, digesting his own besiegers. Mohg underground, courting silence. Morgott alone on the Elden Throne, king of a capital the war had emptied of everything but loyalty. Broken heirs on broken thrones — and no Elden Lord. The Shattering is that rarest of wars, one the record lets us score exactly: every power that entered it was ruined, entrenched, or unmade, and the prize went unclaimed because the prize — a whole Ring, a crowned lord, an order restored — had stopped existing when the first shard was pocketed. The demigods fought an inheritance dispute over an estate that no longer stood.

Heaven's verdict on this result comes down to us in one framing, and I must weigh it with care, because it is the framing everything else gets built on. The sermon of the recall — the account taught to the returning exiles, and through them to every corner of this land — holds that the Shattering meant *abandonment*: that the Greater Will, beholding a war that produced no lord, turned its face from Marika's children altogether. That the teaching was taught is certain. That the teaching is *true* is another matter, for it is a narrator's claim about the mind of an outer god, and no relic countersigns it. What the relics do attest is humbler and stranger: that after the sentence was passed on Marika, the Two Fingers' communion slowed to a trickle — guidance arriving late, or garbled, or not at all, as Enia herself describes — until the Order's living oracles stood like couriers waiting at a door no one answered. Abandonment *because* of the war, as the sermon has it? Or a silence far older, only now impossible to ignore? A discovery made in the Land of Shadow — it waits there for this history, and it will not wait quietly — asks whether anyone had been listening for a very long time indeed. I leave the question standing where the evidence leaves it: upright, and unanswered.

## The last move

And then, far from all of this — beyond the fog, in lands this history has never had cause to map — the war made its true last move, and made it among the dead.

The reader will remember, from the chronicle of Godfrey's fall, the promise the queen left recorded in her own voice at the Third Church: that the grace she stripped from her first lord and his warriors would one day be given back; that after death itself they would return, and wage war, and face the Elden Ring. For an age that promise gathered dust while the exiles and the children of exiles fought, wandered, and died in foreign earth, graceless to the last. Now — with the Ring in shards, the queen entombed, the heirs spent, the Fingers straining at silence — grace returned to them. The guidance came back like light under a door: to the living exiles, and not only to the living, for the summons reached men long buried, and the record does not blush to say so. In distant lands, dead men felt gold touch their eyelids, and rose.

Who moved that piece, the relics do not say — whether the Greater Will reached past its broken children for fresh instruments, or whether a queen in a tree was keeping, at last, an old appointment. I note only that the event and the promise fit each other like a seal and its wax, and that nothing else in the record fits the event at all. The Tarnished were coming home: Godfrey's heirs, the disinherited of the first exile, summoned across the fog to finish a war the demigods could not. The Shattering did not end. It stopped, the way a held breath stops, and the land lay still in the pause, region by ruined region, waiting for the graceless to arrive and inherit the wreck.
