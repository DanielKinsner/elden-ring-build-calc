# Chapter II: The Land of Shadow

*Wherein the world's first holy land raises a tower to heaven, fills its jars with saints, and makes — without ever once noticing her — the god who will burn it.*

---

Before the Erdtree, before gold meant anything but metal, the holiest ground in creation lay in the land we now name only by its punishment: the Land of Shadow. It was not shadowed then. It was the seat of the tower.

Of the people who built that tower we know a great deal, because their works were made of stone and their convictions were made of something harder. They were the **Hornsent** — horned, long-lived, and utterly certain. Where the later Order of gold would preach a tree, the Hornsent venerated the spiral: the braid, the coil, the twining-together of all living things that the oldest faith called the Crucible. Horns and tails, which the golden centuries would teach children to fear as devilry, were to the Hornsent the visible blessing of heaven — the body itself curling toward the divine. Their civilization climbed. Its capital wound upward around the tower-city of Belurat, and above Belurat rose **Enir-Ilim**, the "First Tower," a spiral of stairways and worship-halls with a gate at its summit. The gate is the point of everything that follows, and we shall come to it.

Their devotion did not stop at architecture. It went into jars. Great earthenware vessels, taller than a man, were filled with the living and the dying together — sealed in, shoulder to rib, until in the heat and the prayer the contents forgot their borders: a limb flowering blindly from another body's side, the grammar of the human form erased joint by joint, many bodies made slowly into one. What emerged, when the rite succeeded, was revered as a **saint**. The Hornsent believed, in short, that a mortal body could be *composted into holiness* — and I must ask the reader to sit with that sentence, because the jars were not figurative and the doctrine was not hidden. The sword of one of their own inquisitors states it without embarrassment: sinners and saints alike went into the clay, for in the spiral all flesh was raw material for heaven. This is no slander invented by the Hornsent's destroyers — their own relics, their own liturgical implements, their own cheerful potentates' inscriptions attest the practice. When this history reaches the fires of the Crusade, the reader will be tempted toward pity, and should yield to the temptation. But the reader should also remember the jars.

For the jars needed filling, and for that the Hornsent had found a peculiarly suitable people.

## The village of the shamans

Tucked into the high hinterlands behind the tower's dominion there was a village — unwalled, unfortified, hardly more than a meadow with a chapel in it. Its people were the **shamans**. The relics agree on very little about them, but they agree on the one property that doomed them: shaman flesh *melds*. Where other bodies packed into a jar resist and rot, a shaman's body accepts its companions — knits with them, gentles them, makes the rite work. The Hornsent had a theology that required the mingling of flesh, and here was a people whose flesh mingled. By every sign that survives, the village was harvested for it: its sons and daughters carried down from the meadow and into the clay, generation upon generation, saints made of a people who were never asked. No relic states the raids outright — the jars' own contents, the inquisitors' tools, and the village's emptiness must speak together — but they speak with one voice, and I will treat the harvest as history.

One of those daughters concerns us above all others.

That **Queen Marika the Eternal began her life in the Shaman Village** is written flatly in no surviving text, and I will not pretend otherwise; it is, instead, the conclusion toward which every remaining sign leans. Chief among the signs is a relic of almost unbearable smallness: a braid of golden hair, kept as a holy offering, which its inscription names as the Queen's own — cut from her head and offered *to her home*, a home the same inscription says she could never bring herself to revisit. The braid rests in the shamans' meadow. Set beside it the gesture of prostrate grief that pilgrims learned before her statues — a gesture the stones themselves associate with the word *Mother* — and the meadow's chapel, and the harvest we have just described, and the vessel holds its seal: the god of the coming golden age was born among the jarred people, and lost people she loved into the clay.

As to what she was before godhood found her — here the witnesses grow talkative exactly where they grow unreliable. The knife-print of a later age's assassins would have us believe she came of the **Numen**, a rare, long-lived folk said to hail from beyond this world entirely; the print speaks in the language of rumor, and rumor is what I shall call it, while noting that nothing in the record contradicts it and one thing quietly supports it: the Numen women of her later court were close to her in precisely the way of kin. The aged **grandam of the Hornsent** — the oldest voice this history has, and the angriest — remembers Marika differently, as the low-born shaman girl who was *given the grace of the tower*, raised up within Belurat's own holy precincts, and who repaid the spiral with fire. The grandam is a hostile witness; hers is the testimony of a civilization explaining its own corpse. But hostile witnesses are still witnesses, and her account and the braid's account fit together more neatly than either party would like: a daughter of the harvested people, brought up inside the harvesters' church.

*On the sources, a caution.* The braid and the grandam cannot both be given full weight, for they remember two different women — the relic remembers a tenderness that could not go home; the witness remembers a viper nursed in the tower's bosom. I do not choose between them. I observe only that both accounts require the same first fact — a shaman girl inside the Hornsent world — and that everything Marika later did to that world is more comprehensible, not less, if both are true.

## The son with the serpent

Somewhere in these years — and I say *somewhere* deliberately, for no relic dates it — Marika bore a son. He was red-haired, and there was a fire in him, and there was something else in him besides: a serpent, coiled behind his eye, which his mother sealed rather than cured — the eye plucked out and a seal of grace set in the socket. His name was **Messmer**, called afterward the Impaler, and the remembrance carved for his soul names his mother without hesitation and names his father not at all.

Here is the locked door I promised in the prologue. Understand what kind of silence this is. The record of this land is careless with many things — dates, distances, the fates of minor kings — but it is *lavish* with parentage; lineage is the one subject on which its relics rarely stop talking. The remembrances of Marika's other children name their fathers. Messmer's does not. His armor does not. His soldiers' banners, his knights' oaths, the liturgies of his own crusade — nothing. Some scholars venture the obvious candidates, and I will not dignify the guesses by listing them, because the guesses all founder on the same rock: this is not a gap in the record, it is a redaction *in* the record, and redactions have authors. Someone with the power to decide what ten thousand relics say decided this one thing would be said nowhere. The reader may draw one conclusion with me and go no further: **the name of Messmer's father was not lost. It was removed**, and the queen who left us no explanations is the only hand we know of strong enough to hold the eraser.

Whether the boy was born before his mother's godhood or after it, the relics likewise decline to say, and here I genuinely cannot tell whether the silence has an author or merely a poor archivist. His fire will matter soon enough. His birth I leave where the record leaves it: in the dark, beside his mother's grief.

## The gate at the top of the stairs

All the while, above everything, the tower waited. At the very summit of Enir-Ilim, past the last worship-hall, the Hornsent kept their holiest threshold: the **Gate of Divinity**, the doorway through which — so the tower's whole shape insisted — flesh might pass and come out the other side as god.

I have read the account of one who stood before that gate in later days, and what he saw there I report as he swore it: the gate is *made of bodies*. Not adorned with them — built of them: the fused dead for masonry, torso locked to torso course upon course, arms bracing arms the way stone braces stone, the sainted mortared into an arch — the jars' theology raised to a doorway. Whether it was always so, or became so, no text says. But the shadow-folk's own tradition — and it is their tradition, an attested thing, spoken by those who kept the tower's memory — holds that it was through this gate, up these stairs, that the shaman girl passed; and that when she came down again, or when something came down wearing her certainty, the age of the spiral was over and the age of gold had, without yet knowing it, begun.

Of the passage itself — who opened the way for her, what was promised, what was paid, and to whom — the record is silent, and this silence, at least, I judge honest: those who witness the making of a god do not, on the evidence of this land, tend to survive the career of the result.

What she carried up those stairs, then, by the tally of this chapter: a harvested people; a meadow with a chapel; a braid she would one day send home in her place; a red-haired son with a sealed serpent and no father; and whatever it is that a woman owes a civilization that made saints of her family without asking. What she became at the top of those stairs, and what she did about the debt, this history takes up next — and the reader who has kept the jars in mind will not need three guesses.
