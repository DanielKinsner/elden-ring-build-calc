# Chapter VI: The Two Courts

*Wherein the moon raises a queen over a lake of scholars, gold comes courting with an army at its back, a turtle joins two enemies in marriage, and the chronicler is obliged, at the last, to tell the reader who the bridegroom was.*

---

Every age of this history until now has had one center: a tower, a tree, a throne. This age had two. In the east stood Leyndell, the royal capital, gold-roofed beneath the Erdtree, seat of Queen Marika the Eternal and her Golden Order. In the west lay Liurnia of the Lakes, silver under its mists, where a different royalty answered to a different light. For a span no relic measures, the Lands Between held two courts, two faiths, and two queens — and what ended the arrangement was not a conquest but a wedding, followed by a desertion, followed by a discovery that I have carried through every page of this history like a stone in the mouth and will finally set down before this chapter closes. The reader who wishes to enjoy the wedding should read slowly. Weddings in this land do not stay enjoyed.

## The lake and the academy

North of the grave-strewn lowlands the country sinks, fills with water, and turns to glass: Liurnia is less a province than a flooded world, a lake the size of a kingdom, fringed with drowned churches and standing ruins. At its center, on an island of rock, rises the **Academy of Raya Lucaria** — the great school of glintstone sorcery, where magic is drawn from stone in which, the sorcerers themselves hold, the life of fallen stars still faintly burns. Its scholars studied the night sky with the devotion that Leyndell's clergy spent on the tree. They organized themselves into learned factions, each under its own glintstone crown — Karolos, Olivinus, the double-crowned Twinsages — and they kept their own soldiery, the Knights of the Cuckoo, whose signature battle-spell the academy's own texts dismiss, a little cruelly, as a crude imitation of true sorcery. Nor was the school gentle with its own. The oldest and deepest stream of glintstone learning — the primeval current of the great masters Azur and Lusat — was, by every sign that survives, suppressed as heresy, its masters driven to the far edges of the world. A university that polices its geniuses so briskly may fairly be called a court already; it only lacked a crown.

The crown arrived on its own. In her youth **Rennala** was a champion of lunar sorcery, and the record of her rise — carved for her own remembrance — is precise on a point that later flatterers blur: she did not conquer the academy. She *captivated* it. Her signature working was the full moon itself, drawn down and made to burn before the beholder, and the scholars of Raya Lucaria, men and women who had spent their lives squinting at distant stars, looked at a woman who could summon the moon into a lecture hall and surrendered without a siege. They took her as master of the academy. She took command of its Glintstone Knights. And she raised her own house — **Caria**, which had been royal in nothing but ambition — into a true royal family, so that one woman now wore both dignities: master of the school and queen of the lake. Certain chroniclers, fond of symmetry, number a "first Liurnian war" here, an armed struggle of academy against Caria before the charm. No relic requires it, and the remembrance attests a courtship of scholars, not a conquest of them; I record the war of the numbering-men as their invention and move on.

One doctrine of this moon-court deserves the reader's attention, for it is the quietest boast in the record. From the drowned dark beneath the earth comes a talisman remembering the lost black moon of the city Nokstella, and its inscription states the hierarchy of the night without apology: that moon was the guide of countless stars. The academy studied stars; the Carians *ruled* a moon; and in Liurnia it was understood which of the two leads and which follows. The royal family built its own school of war on the principle — sword-sorceries, blades of conjured moonlight in which spell and swordplay were one discipline — and the **Carian knights** who mastered them never numbered even twenty, yet were reckoned the equals of Leyndell's golden champions. The royal children were schooled by dedicated preceptors; one of these, **Seluvis**, would outlive the dynasty's whole glory, though that sour tale belongs to the dynasty's ruin, and will keep until we reach it.

## Gold comes courting

Of **Radagon** before the war, the record says almost nothing — and I ask the reader to note the silence now, and to keep it, because this chapter will end inside it. A red-haired champion of the Golden Order: that is the whole of his introduction. No relic names his father, his mother, his birthplace, or one deed of his youth. Every other figure of his rank in this history trails lineage the way a comet trails fire. Radagon arrives in the record already grown, already golden, already at the head of an army.

The war itself is among the best-attested events in this book, for reasons the next section will make plain. While **Godfrey**, first Elden Lord, still held the consort's seat in Leyndell — his wars against giant and Storm Lord won, his own fall not yet upon him — Radagon led a great host out of the capital and into Liurnia, and met Rennala's moon-court in battle. Lake-country is unkind to invaders, and the academy's knights were the equal of the Order's; the fighting settled toward stalemate. And then the war ended in the strangest way a war can end, and I state it flatly because the witness is unimpeachable: the invader repented. Radagon regretted his aggression, sued for peace, and sought the hand of the queen he had marched against. Balladeers have improved the scene — in the taverns Radagon looks up in mid-battle, sees the full moon rise over the field, and lays down his arms on the spot. The record supplies the moon, the repentance, and nothing that joins them so prettily; the moment belongs to the singers, and they may keep it.

## The pastor of vows

In eastern Liurnia, above the lake, stands a modest church, and in that church lives the best witness this history possesses. He is a turtle. I wrote in my prologue of one exceptionally forthcoming turtle among Gideon's sworn accounts, and I confess I have been saving him: **Miriel, Pastor of Vows**, steward of the Church of Vows, mitred, patient, and older than most of what he remembers — a creature of perfect gentleness presiding over the site of this history's least gentle marriage. Where other witnesses in this book lie for a faction or misremember for a wound, Miriel loved both houses and wanted nothing from either. When he grieves — and his account is a long grief, courteously delivered — he grieves for everyone at once. I weigh testimony for a living, and I will say plainly: I trust the turtle. The reader will notice that I have now lingered on him a sentence longer than the method requires; I record the lapse so that even my affections may be audited — the most trusted witness in this history is also, and I can defend this no further, the one I simply like.

It was at his church that the two great houses were joined — the Erdtree and the Moon, gold and silver, the courting army and the courted lake. And Miriel's account makes clear what the vows actually were: the wedding *was* the armistice. There was no separate treaty; the marriage was the treaty, peace signed in flesh at the altar rather than ink at a table. The relics bear him out in a form I find oddly touching — a one-of-a-kind ornamental crossbow, crafted to commemorate the wedding, whose inscription names it a token of *reconciliation* between Leyndell and Raya Lucaria. Nobody commissions a commemorative weapon for a love-match. Everyone in both courts understood precisely what had been signed.

## The children of the lake

The marriage held, and for a while the two courts were one family. Three children came of it, and — the reader will learn to treasure such sentences — their parentage is stated outright in the surviving record, all three, without a hedge anywhere: children of Radagon and Rennala, every one later counted among the demigods.

**Radahn** came first in fame if not in birth, for no relic orders the births. As a boy he traveled to Sellia in the east to learn gravitational sorcery, and the reason recorded for it is the most humanizing line in the whole demigod record: he had a beloved horse, undersized for a rider who would grow into a giant, and he mastered the pull of the very stars so that he would never have to abandon it. The general he became — and the day he stilled the heavens themselves, freezing the fate written there, a feat the Carian war counselor Iji attests with awe and dread together — belongs to a later and darker age; but I set the horse down here, where he is still a boy in a court by a lake. **Rykard** was the second son, a lord of the Carian line whose appetite for jurisprudence, and whose later appetites of an entirely different order, this history will reach with reluctance in its proper place. **Ranni** was the daughter, a witch of the dark moon — her mother's inheritance turned a shade colder — and of all Marika's extended family none will matter more to the end of this book.

And Radagon? Miriel and the relics agree on how the bridegroom spent the marriage: studying. The champion of the Golden Order sat in the academy his wedding had reconciled and gave himself to glintstone sorcery — the learning of the court he had invaded — with a hunger the relics frame as a wish to be *complete*. I pass the word to the reader exactly as the record passes it to me. It will be worth remembering shortly.

*On the sources: the sister who walked into shadow.* Rennala had a younger sister, and the record of her is firm where it should be and impossible where it matters. **Rellana** was a Carian princess and a master swordswoman; her twin-moon sorcery is remembered as a keepsake of childhood, two sisters meeting two moons together; and she renounced her royal birthright, crossed into the sealed Land of Shadow, and gave herself to the service of **Messmer the Impaler** — Marika's serpent-cursed son, whose crusade the reader has already watched burn through the land of the spiral — becoming known as the Sword of Messmer. She went, her remembrance says, knowing the moon's light could not ease what afflicts him. Why she went — loyalty, love, penance — no relic ventures. All of that is solid. What it does to my chronology is not. I placed the crusade near the Golden Order's founding, as its evidence leans; yet here is a daughter of the Carian generation, sister to a queen who married generations later by any comfortable count, standing at the Impaler's side. Either the Carian royals endure far beyond mortal spans — and Rennala, who persists to this day, is fair evidence they do — or the crusade ran longer, or later, than its antiquity suggests. The relics permit both repairs and compel neither, and I will not mortar over the largest crack in this history's masonry just to make the wall look finished. The reader deserves to see where the building is unsound.

## The leaving

Then Godfrey fell. The manner of his fall — his last worthy foe, his dimming eyes, the grace stripped from him and his warriors, the long exile that made the Tarnished — I have told at the close of his own age, and I will not re-mourn him here. What concerns us is what followed in Liurnia, and here the order of events must be nailed down, because it is so often told wrong. One hears it in the tellings that Godfrey was cast out first, and that Radagon's Liurnian marriage came after — a wandering champion consoling a moon-queen while the lord's seat stood empty. Miriel's testimony, given at the very church where the vows were sworn, rules this out entirely. The marriage came first, in Godfrey's day. The exile followed. And *then*, with the consort's seat vacant in Leyndell, Radagon rose from his wife's side, put aside Queen Rennala and their three children, returned to the capital, and was made second husband to Queen Marika, King Consort, and second Elden Lord.

He left her one thing. Miriel — and only Miriel; the queen's own remembrance cannot bring itself to mention the object — records that on his departure Radagon bequeathed Rennala an amber egg. What that egg was, and what she made of it, is the grief we are now coming to. As for *why* he left: the pastor of vows, of all witnesses the one who most wanted a reason to exist, states plainly that none survives. The timing explains itself — a throne needed a consort. The willingness explains nothing, and Miriel calls the question what it remains: a mystery. In Leyndell, the new lord completed his symmetry. The man who had learned sorcery in his first wife's court took up incantations in his second — the Erdtree's faith-magic, the other great tradition — so that the two halves of the Lands Between's learning were now mastered by one red-haired man. He had wished, the reader will recall, to be complete.

## The grand library

Of what became of Rennala, the travelers' accounts that leave the lake agree so exactly, one with another, that I will let them build the room before I tell the reader what the room means. The Grand Library at Raya Lucaria's heart is lit, they say, the way a drowned thing is lit: no lamp in it, only a light like cold milk pooling across the floor, the high windows giving back blue glass and drowned silver where a sky should be. In the midst of that light the Queen of the Full Moon drifts above her own library floor, and in her arms she holds the amber egg. She cradles it. She rocks it — the accounts do not omit the rocking, any of them. And about her, in slow orbits, go her scholars: small, soft, unfinished figures in students' swaddling, circling their queen the way lesser lights circle a moon, while she hushes the whole drifting round of them as one hushes a nursery.

That is the room. Now what it means. Rennala did not rage, or remarry, or make war on the capital that had unmade her. She broke — quietly, thoroughly, and in a fashion the academy has been arranging itself around ever since. She withdrew into the Grand Library with the egg in her arms, and there she remains; and the egg is no keepsake but a **Great Rune of the Unborn**, a thing whose inscription promises rebirth into a perfected form, and whose actual issue, by the same travelers' accounts, emerges frail, unfinished, and brief. The contradiction sits in the relic's own text and I can do nothing to relieve it: an engine of perfection that has never once produced any. The scholars that circle her are her devoted, birthed anew, and again, and again. Whether the academy still obeys her as its master or merely keeps her as its most beloved relic, no visitor can say; what is certain is that the school sealed its gates behind glintstone locks, and that somewhere in the shuttering, the distinction between guarding the queen and caging her grief stopped mattering. The court of the moon did not fall. It closed, like a hand around a wound.

Her children were provided for — and the provision requires exact language, because sloppy chroniclers have made a warm family of it. That Radahn, Rykard, and Ranni stood among the demigods of the royal family is stated in the record. That they arrived there *through the remarriage* — elevated when their father became the queen's consort — is nowhere set down in so many words that I can produce; it follows, and I show my reasoning: Miriel's account of the remarriage on one side, the three children's undisputed demigod standing in Marika's extended house on the other, and no other bridge between a lake-court childhood and a golden throne. So I will call them precisely what the kinship makes them and nothing warmer: children of Radagon and Rennala; stepchildren of Queen Marika. Stepchildren exactly. Keep the word, reader. This history's family tree is a ledger, and in a moment you will see why I audit it.

## The statue in Leyndell

In the royal capital there stands a statue of Radagon, second Elden Lord, raised in his honor among the Erdtree's roots. The fundamentalists of the Golden Order — Radagon's own school, matured in his own era — taught an incantation called the Law of Regression: the pull of all things back toward their one true state, the doctrine that error, illusion, and disguise cannot survive a sufficient longing for oneness. It heals, that law; it dispels; it strips falsehood. And some hand — priest, sculptor, dissident, I cannot name it and neither can the record — hid beneath the statue an inscription that only that incantation lays bare, as if to say: this truth is for whoever loves truth enough to insist. The message is three words long, and I have verified it as far as a chronicler in a dead library can verify anything, and I give it exactly, the one quotation this chapter permits itself.

"Radagon is Marika."

The library made no sound when I understood it. It has seemed a degree quieter ever since.

The capital's own stone says it, in the capital's own hidden hand. And the record, once you dare to ask it, corroborates: an echo of the queen's preserved voice, kept in her own bedchamber, addresses Radagon not as husband but as her Order's loyal hound — and as one who had *not yet become* her. Not ally. Not instrument. Becoming. One being, wearing two bodies down the long years; whether one from the first that learned to divide, or two that flowed together like the law's own longing, no relic says, and on that question I decline to guess in either direction.

But sit, as I sat, with what it does to every page behind us. The gold that came courting Liurnia was the same will as the throne it courted for. When Radagon's host marched on the lake, the god of the Golden Order was, in some sense I cannot chart, on both banks of the war at once — the two courts of this chapter's title were one mind quarreling with itself, and the peace that ended the quarrel was that mind marrying its own hand. When Radagon knelt in repentance before Rennala — what repented? When Marika summoned a new consort to the empty seat — who was summoned, and by whom? The queen took a second lord and the second lord was the queen; the god married herself; and Rennala, who charmed an academy and raised a royal house and bore three demigods, was left holding an amber egg by a husband who had never entirely been in the room. I have called Miriel this history's best witness and I do not retract it — but the turtle wed two houses believing he wed two people, and the tenderest testimony in my possession describes a marriage with, at the last accounting, rather fewer parties than the vows supposed.

One thing more, and the chapter may close. The second marriage, too, had issue: twins, **Miquella** and **Malenia**, whom the relics describe — in a phrase I once took for a poet's flourish — as children of a *single god*. The reader now knows it was bookkeeping. What such children are, what they inherit, and what their inheritance costs them and everyone else, is the business of the golden age itself, and I will not run ahead of my sources. I note only this, and leave it on the table where it can be seen: two courts, a lake that held the moon and a city that held the tree — and one face, when the water stilled, looking out of both.
