# Chapter V: Godfrey, First of the Name

*Wherein a chieftain out of the badlands is given a crown, a queen, and a lion to carry; wins every war the young gold requires of him; and learns, when the wars run out, what a god does with a finished tool.*

---

A new god's first shortage is never faith. Faith arrives on its own, the way flies find a feast; what a new god lacks is an arm. Queen Marika the Eternal came down from her tower with an Order to plant in ground that already belonged to giants, storms, dragons, and men, and the planting of it was the work of this chapter's man — a warlord she found outside her own creation, used for exactly as long as the work lasted, and then put away. This age was the Erdtree's violent morning, and it is remembered in gold; but what broke it should be marked from the outset, because it was not defeat. Nothing defeated Godfrey. What broke this age was completion.

## The chieftain and the lion

He was not born Godfrey, and he did not die Godfrey either, though that end lies far past this chapter's borders. He began as **Hoarah Loux**, chieftain of the badlands — a country the record places beyond the Lands Between and describes not at all, as if geography itself lost interest past the fog. Of his stock we know only what his conduct declares: a people whose whole economy was battle. Marika took him as her **first consort**, and he was crowned the **first Elden Lord**. I state this flatly because the relics state it flatly, and from more directions than almost any fact in this history — the remembrance kept of his soul, the crown that survives him, and the patient testimony of Miriel, keeper of the Church of Vows, who of all my witnesses has the least imagination and is therefore the most useful. Note the direction of the marriage, for the grammar of it governs everything downstream: the god takes a lord. Godfrey was Marika's consort. No relic anywhere reverses the arrow, and the courtesies of later chroniclers who write "Marika, his queen" as though she were an ornament on his reign have it exactly backwards. She was the reign. He was the instrument.

An instrument, the record is frank to admit, that needed a governor. Look at any image of Godfrey that survives — statue, shade, or the living man — and the eye goes first to the wrong king. A lion rides him. Not blazoned on a shield, not worn as a pelt: a great white beast, alive, fused across his shoulders like a yoke of muscle and mane, forepaws down over the collarbones, claws set against the chest they could open, jaws hanging at his ear. The mane breaks over the man's head, so that the first Elden Lord looked out at every war he won from under a hood of another animal's fur; each stride he took, he took under that weight, and he slept, if he slept, beneath a heartbeat not his own. The beast is **Serosh**, called the Beast Regent, and the arrangement was a vow, not a trophy: the chieftain's appetite for slaughter ran so far past a king's requirements that a second sovereign was set upon his body to lean against it — a regent for his blood as Godfrey was regent for the realm, drag and bit and counterweight, a leash worn where the hand it leashed could not reach. The axe he bore carried the same oath in its inscription — that its wielder would conduct himself as a lord. Mark the honesty of the arrangement. Other kings hang their restraint on chapel walls and take it down as needed. This one had his forged on — hot iron under a cold governor — and carried it to the end of the world.

Nor did he fight alone. The knights who served him as his elite were men and women marked by the **Crucible** — the old braided current of life from which, the earliest faith held, the Erdtree itself had grown — bearing horns and tails in the age's last years when such things could still be worn at court with honor. Their armor says they served the first Elden Lord; the same armor says what came after, when the Order's gold brightened and its memory shortened, and the Crucible's children fell from knighthood into scorn. That fall belongs to a later, crueler page of this chapter. The knights belong here, at the morning, in honor, riding to the wars.

## The breaking of the giants

Of those wars, the fullest single witness is an object: the **Elden Lord Crown**, whose inscription compresses a career of conquest into a few unbothered lines. The age of the Erdtree began amid war, it says, with Godfrey lord of the battlefield; he led the war against the Giants; he faced the Storm Lord alone; and when his last worthy enemy fell, the hue drained from his eyes. The crown recites; it does not date. Its list I will follow as an order of telling, and the sequence deserves no more authority than that — a crown is a poor calendar, and this one was made to praise, not to reckon.

The Giants first, then. In the frozen north lived the Fire Giants, and they had to die for a reason the record preserves with unusual completeness: they kept a fire that could kill a god's tree. Their fell god — one-eyed, and otherwise unbestiaried — lent them a flame tended in the great Forge atop the peaks, and that flame was capable of burning the Erdtree itself. This is no pious exaggeration by the war's recruiters; the proof came at the end of days, when the same forge lit the same fire and the sky I write beneath turned to ash. Against that fire Marika sent her Lord, and Godfrey led the war that annihilated the giant race — the hero's grave in the peaks still bears the boast by name, and the warriors of Zamor, whose ruins sit athwart the road to those peaks, are remembered by their relics as old hands at giant-killing, which is likely how such hands were first trained.

Annihilated, I wrote, and must at once correct myself, for the correction is the war's whole meaning. One giant was left alive — not from mercy, or not from anything I am prepared to call mercy. The forge-flame could not be put out. Fire that can burn a god's tree does not oblige its enemies by dying, and so the flame required a keeper, and the keeper the Order chose was the last of the people the flame had failed to save. Marika herself pronounced the sentence, and it survives in the remembrance kept of that giant's soul, the only words I will quote in this chapter: "O trifling giant, mayest thou tend thy flame for eternity." Fifty generations later he was at his post yet, alone on the mountain with the fire that doomed his kind. Whether the sword would have been crueler is not a question I can settle; I have weighed it several evenings running and gone to bed undecided.

## The storm, and a silence shaped like a throne

Of the second great feat the entire surviving record is one clause long: Godfrey faced the **Storm Lord** alone. That the duel happened, I state as fact; the crown carries it, and the crown is the most official object this history owns. Who the Storm Lord *was*, no relic anywhere condescends to say — and the strangeness of that deserves a moment. A war is remembered; the enemy is not. It is as if the scribes recorded the axe-blow and mislaid the neck.

What can be reasoned runs thus. The winds of this land pool at one place above all others: Stormveil, on its hill in Limgrave, where the gales still worry the parapets and the hawks still nest in the towers — and where Godfrey's own remote descendant sits in the present age, as though the family kept the battlefield for a trophy. If the Storm Lord fell anywhere, by every sign it was there. And among the hawks of that country the old texts recall a sovereign — a stormhawk revered as king by all the rest, in the days when the winds raged worst. Perhaps that king of hawks was the Storm Lord; the title fits, the country fits, and I can carry the guess no further than a perhaps, because that is all it is. Some scholars venture higher and stranger: that the Storm Lord was Placidusax himself, the dragonlord of the age before gold, on the ground that storms and thrones attract one another. Nothing in the record compels it, and a good deal of dragon-history sits awkwardly beneath it; a guess for idle evenings, and I leave it there. The silence itself I judge ordinary rather than authored — a casualty of time, not of intent — but I record my irritation with it all the same. A historian forgives the record many things. Losing an entire king is not gracefully among them.

## The dragon on the walls

The third war came to the capital's own gates, and this one left a witness too large to file. At some hour after **Leyndell** was raised — and the royal capital by then was raised, walled, and doctrined, its dead already going down into the root-threaded catacombs as the Order's law required — the ancient dragon **Gransax** fell upon the city and rained calamity on it: the only breach of Leyndell's walls in all its recorded history. The proof requires no rubbing or inscription. The dragon's body lies where it died, draped across the rooftops of the capital like a mountain that lost its footing, and every ambitious lie ever told about those walls has had to be told in its shadow. War with the ancient dragons followed. Under whose lordship it was fought and finished — Godfrey's, or that of the consort who succeeded him — the record simply does not say, and I set the gap down plainly rather than paper it: the war needed a capital to exist, and nothing else about its date survives.

What survives instead is the peace, and the peace is stranger than the war. When it ended, the capital did not burn the dragons' memory; it *canonized* it. A knightly cult of the ancient dragons arose within Leyndell itself, red lightning spoken as prayer beside the Erdtree's gold — the cult's own prayerbook dates itself to after the war — and the dragon **Lansseax** shed her scales to walk among the capital's knights as the faith's priestess. Her brother **Fortissax** went further still, and here the tale touches the family: Fortissax became the sworn companion of Marika's son, the prince this chapter comes to next — a friendship the taverns have gilded with a duel no relic records. It is the only wholly warm thing in this family's history, and it needed no improving.

## The children of gold and horn

Marika's children by this first marriage stand at the head of what the relics call the **golden lineage**, and the brightest of them was **Godwyn the Golden** — the capital's darling, the dragon-knights' prince, Fortissax's sworn companion, a demigod so beloved that his epithet is the age's own metal. That Marika was his mother is stated everywhere the family is stated at all. And now, reader, I must stop the procession, because you have just watched me do a thing every chronicle of this land does, and I intend to be caught at it.

*On the sources, and a confession of method.* I called these Marika's children **by this first marriage** — and no surviving text says so. Search where you please: no relic, no remembrance, no epitaph names Godfrey the father of Godwyn, or of the twins below, or of any child at all. What the record supplies is a chain with three links. Godrick's Great Rune defines the golden lineage as Godfrey and his offspring; Morgott's Great Rune says its bearer was born of that lineage; and the children in question are Marika's, in an age when Godfrey was Marika's lord. Each link is sound relic-work. Joined, they carry real weight, and I will write the rest of this history as though the chain holds — but a chain is not a naming, and this family has already taught us — in the person of the Impaler, that red-haired son whose father's name stands nowhere in ten thousand relics — that the space where a father's name should stand can be emptied on purpose and the world go on smiling. There, the silence had the look of workmanship. Here I believe it has only the look of laziness — no hand profits by hiding Godwyn's father, and every sign leans the same way. Still: when this book says *Godfrey's son*, the reader now knows precisely how much iron is in the sentence, and that the two great scholarly ledgers of this age, working apart, weighed the same links and stopped at the same word — *inferred*. I could do no better, and I distrust any history that claims it did.

The chain has two more names on it, and the Order spent an age making sure you never heard them. Marika's line produced **Morgott** and **Mohg** — twin brothers, and this at least is stated outright, on the Great Rune Mohg would one day hold — and the twins were born **Omen**: horned, tailed, marked head to heel with the very aspects the Crucible Knights had lately worn as honor. Mark the timing of the shame. Within living memory such traits had ridden at the first lord's side; now, with the Erdtree waxing and its origins conveniently forgotten, they were devilry. Among the common folk the Order's remedy was the knife, and the count of what the knife cost is coming. Royalty could not be seen holding it, so royalty chose the cellar. Born to the Erdtree's own line, the twins were not excised but erased: confined, secretly and indefinitely, in the sunless drainage-city beneath Leyndell that the capital calls the Subterranean Shunning-Grounds, under restraints forged for each of them by name. The shackles survive. I have read their inscriptions, and I ask the reader to hold one image against everything golden in this chapter: the capital's shining prince above, the mason setting a bolt for his brothers below, and one mother's silence over both. What the darkness under Leyndell made of each twin belongs to the age that earned it. They were down there through all of it. Remember, whenever this book says *the golden age*, that it had a basement.

## What ran downstream

Of the lineage's further course, briefly. **Godrick the Grafted**, in the present age, descends from Godfrey — so his own Great Rune states, though no text counts the generations between. The line's other claimants — a second Grafted, titled by his jail's signage; a warrior wearing the old chieftain's surname — are the register's business. Names are the cheapest relics there are; men take them the way crows take rings.

One more thread, so the weave is not mistaken. Somewhere across these same generations, on the far side of a veil, the queen's crusade in the Land of Shadow — the Impaler's fires among the spiral's towers — was lit, or burning, or lately done; where exactly it sits among Godfrey's wars is a problem the record leaves unsolved, and honest chronology must leave it loose rather than tie it prettily. The official history of the golden age does not mention it at all. The official history of the golden age was very good at basements.

## The hue, and the harbor

Now the ending, which is not a battle. The crown's inscription, having listed the wars, adds the line the whole age turns upon: when Godfrey's last worthy enemy fell, the hue drained from his eyes. Grace did not wait for any decree. The gold went out of the man's sight the moment the work was finished, the way heat leaves iron — and the lion set upon his back to lean against the furnace of him now rode a man gone cold. I find I can add nothing to the image except the observation that no one seems to have found it strange. A realm at peace, and its lord's eyes gone dim, and the court, so far as any relic records, went on with dinner.

Marika did not go on with dinner. Her own voice survives — a spoken echo, lingering at the Third Church that bears her name — and in it she does the thing flatly that the fading had already done in figure: she divests Godfrey and his warriors of grace entire, and drives them from the Lands Between, to fight, and live, and die in a land afar. The first Elden Lord, hero of the giants' war, the storm, and the crown, walked out of the world he had conquered with his people at his back and nothing golden left in his eyes: the first of the **Tarnished**, whose descendants' return, long after, is the storm this whole history sails toward. And the same echo carries one thing more, which must be taken now and held: a promise. The grace taken, she says, will one day be given back. They would be called across the fog, the dead among them, to face the Elden Ring. Exile, with a return postmarked eternity. *Why* — whether she was discarding a dulled tool, or sparing it, or salting champions away outside her own Order against a need she alone foresaw — the record does not say; the echo's gentleness and its built-in homecoming have persuaded some scholars of the long game, and I note the theory, and note equally that a god who explains nothing has left us nothing to compel it. Of Marika's purposes this history has exactly as many explanations as she chose to leave. The count stands at none.

Of the exile itself the relics keep only the far end: a Long March through lands this record cannot name, an axe that broke somewhere in the years of it, and at the march's end a man setting down his crown-name by his own hand — Godfrey no longer, Hoarah Loux again, as though the whole lordship had been a garment borrowed for the duration of the wars. There the relics stop, and the fog holds the rest.

No relic paints the leaving, and I have held to my rule about invention through five chapters now; so let me break it once, openly, and tell you I am breaking it. I picture ships. I picture the graceless host at some grey harbor the record never names, loading a life's plunder that had just been revalued at nothing, and the biggest of them standing at the rail with a lion on his back and no light in his eyes, watching the only world he had ever been necessary to go small against the water. I set that picture down and found, some while after, that I had stopped writing; the lamp had burned a finger lower before I took up the pen again. A historian has no business grieving for a discarded tool. I record that this one did, briefly, and returns to his discipline. Every other man aboard had lost a country. Hoarah Loux had lost the thing a country was *for*. He had been built — bred, tamed, leashed, crowned — around a single purpose, and the purpose was used up, and the god who used it did not even hate him; she had simply finished. What exile does to such a man, the far end of the record already told us, and it is the one mercy in this chapter: he put down the crown and kept the war. The lion stayed on his back a while longer, claws in the old grooves. And the reader who has come to like him — I find, against my training, that I have — should enjoy the harbor while it lasts.

The gold, meanwhile, needed a new lord. The god turned, as gods do, and looked west, where a full moon stood over cold water; and the fog had scarcely closed behind the first consort before the choosing of the second began.
