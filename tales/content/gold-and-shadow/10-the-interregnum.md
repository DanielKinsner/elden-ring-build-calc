# Chapter X: The Interregnum

*Wherein the war ends without ending; the broken heirs sit their broken seats; the land learns to live the way a wound lives; and grace, having measured the great and found no lord among them, goes looking for the small.*

---

The age this chapter surveys has no proper name, because the people in it did not believe they were living in an age. They believed they were living in an interruption — that the war would resume, or the Order would speak, or someone would win. None of it happened. The Shattering did not conclude; it clotted. The demigods, too maimed to finish one another, withdrew each into a stronghold with a shard of god's law and a body full of damage, and the land between their fortresses was left to soldiers who had forgotten their wars' objects and faiths that had outlived their gods' attention. It is the only period in this history in which nothing is founded, decided, or mended — and I ask the reader to walk it with me slowly, fortress by fortress, because everything the end of this book will spend was hoarded here.

I am better placed to write this chapter than any other, and less comfortable in it. This Hold was the counting-house of the age: here the returning Tarnished gathered, and here their intelligence was compiled — on the very shelves that stand at my shoulder as I write, by the light of a lamp that was in all likelihood the All-Knowing's own. It is a good lamp; it has outlasted the enterprise it served. And down the corridor from where it burns, in a chamber I pass each day and do not linger in, the Two Fingers stand yet, vast and mute, the oracle that steered everything this chapter describes. I will come back to them at the end.

## Limgrave, and the lord of borrowed limbs

Begin in the south, where the storm-lands meet the sea. Limgrave had been the seat of old storm culture, then a prize of Godfrey's conquests, and at last a holding of the golden lineage itself — the line of Godfrey and his offspring, as Godrick's own restored rune defines it. In the interregnum it belonged to the last of that line still sitting a throne: **Godrick the Grafted**, lord of Stormveil.

Of Godrick the relics are unusually well supplied, and unusually unkind. He held a Great Rune; he held the strongest castle in the south; and he filled its halls with the work that earned his epithet. Grafting, for the reader spared its acquaintance: the joining of severed limbs — other people's — onto one's own body, in pursuit of a strength nature declined to provide. The Tarnished who began returning to Limgrave in this era were not received at Stormveil as champions. They were received as stock. The castle's own gate-keep, one Gostoc, has left testimony on the hunting and harvesting of the graceless, and Gostoc's contempt for his master runs through every recorded word — a servant's contempt, which is the most carefully informed kind. A line begun by a lion-hearted conqueror ended the age sewing dead men's arms to its shoulders in the dark.

He was not even unchallenged: Kenneth Haight, a minor noble dispossessed of his one small fort, maintained a rival claim to the lordship of Limgrave. Further south, at Castle Morne on the Weeping Peninsula, the enslaved Misbegotten — beast-blooded folk the Order had long used as labor — rose against their human masters, and the rising was never put down.

## Liurnia, and the two mad households

North of the storm-lands, Liurnia's lake country held two royal houses' worth of wreckage in one flooded valley.

The first was the academy. Raya Lucaria had rebelled against its own Carian masters after Radagon abandoned Queen Rennala — a departure the reader has already grieved once — and in this age the rebellion had hardened into a sealed institution. The academy shut its gates behind sorcery, and its soldiery, the Cuckoos, made war across the lake at large; its heresies it hunted as diligently as its enemies, though the exiled sorceress **Sellen** kept the banned star-study of the academy's own founders alive in hiding all the same. And in the Grand Library, at the sealed heart of it all, **Rennala** remained as this book last found her, cradling the amber egg. The lake kept her still; the age asked nothing of her.

The second household was Caria itself: the manor in the northwest, holding out behind its old magical defenses, a court in twilight, defended and diminished. Of what was done in its towers in these years the record says little, and its lady's designs belong to the night of the black knives, where the reader has met them.

## Caelid, the wound left open

Eastward the survey stops being melancholy and becomes appalling. The Battle of Aeonia — the war's great atrocity, whose scarlet flower the reader has already watched open — did not end when the armies did. The scarlet rot Malenia loosed upon Caelid stayed, and through all the years of the interregnum it worked.

What the rot could not corrupt, it recruited. There arose in Caelid a faith of the wound itself: the Kindred of Rot, who exult in decay as a sublime cycle of death and renewal, and the sage **Gowry** of Sellia, who preached — his own recorded words carry it — that rot is a divinity no less worthy than the Erdtree's gold. The same Gowry took in a girl found as a babe in the Aeonia swamp, a girl of Malenia's own blood — sister, daughter, or offshoot, her testimony leaves the category open, and so must I — and tended her toward a flowering with a purpose all its own. Sellia itself, the town of night sorcery where young Radahn had learned the gravitational arts, sealed itself behind warded flames and kept its secrets through the whole age.

And through the middle of all this walked the age's saddest monument. **Radahn** — Starscourge Radahn, who had arrested the very stars in their courses, the mightiest of the demigods by most soldiers' count — survived Aeonia without his reason. He wandered the dunes of his devoured dukedom, mindless, feeding as beasts feed, while overhead the constellations he had halted hung fixed in a ruined sky: even broken, he held the stars. His Redmanes did not desert him. Mark that; desertion was the common coin of this age, and I have entered so much of it in this chapter's ledger that fidelity now reads like an error in the accounts. They ringed the wasteland with fire to pen the rot, and their castellan, the witch-hunter Jerren, had come to believe that a great lord deserved a great death, and that an honorable one could yet be arranged. An age may be judged by what its loyalty has left to hope for. In Caelid, loyalty's last project was a good funeral — and the reader should hear no scorn in that sentence, for I intend none. Every other banner in this survey wanted something from its lord. The Redmanes wanted only that theirs be allowed to end as what he had been.

## Leyndell, the capital that held

At the center of the map, and of every ambition, stood the royal capital — and the capital, alone in the Lands Between, was *held*. The reader knows by whom, though the age itself did not.

**Morgott**, Marika's Omen son, raised in shackles beneath the city he would die defending, had appointed himself the Erdtree's protector during the war and never afterward set the office down. Through the interregnum Leyndell was his: Tree Sentinels on the approaches, soldiery on the roads, the great dead dragon of the old war still draped across the ramparts, and the throne kept — not occupied; *kept* — by a king who ruled from concealment because his own face was, by the Order's law, an abomination. He defended, with his body and the whole of his long vigil, the very dynasty that had caged him in its sewers — and I find I have written that sentence with more tenderness than a survey of garrisons and gates has any use for. Let it stand; the facts beneath it are sound. When the returning Tarnished of this era pressed toward the capital, they were met on the way by a hooded omen calling himself Margit, the Fell One, who broke them with a jailer's efficiency. No text unites Margit and Morgott in so many words; but the shackle forged to bind the one is attested to bind the other, and the voice, the shape, and the grudge are continuous. I have done the reckoning in front of the reader and I count them one man.

Yet the deepest fact of interregnum Leyndell is this: even its defender could not enter the thing he defended. The Erdtree itself had closed. Thorns barred it — the tree's own, by every account — and no claimant of the age found a door. The capital of the world spent the whole interruption guarding a sealed tree with a hidden king, while beneath its streets the Order's oubliettes went on holding everything the gold could not look at: the Omens of the shunning-grounds, an entombed merchant caravan, a defiler the reader is spared for now, and — deepest, and worst — a sealed thing with three fingers, of which more below.

*On the sources, a confession of appetite.* This chapter eats from one larder more than any other: the collection of this Hold, assembled by its master the All-Knowing as intelligence for the hunting of the very lords described here. Intelligence files are a chronicler's feast and famine at once — precise about garrisons, gates, and the weaknesses of bodies, and silent as stones about anything a hunter cannot use. If the reader finds this chapter accurate on fortifications and thin on souls, the reader has discovered what kind of man compiled my evidence, and I would rather be caught than doubted.

## Gelmir, the mountain that eats

West of the capital rose the age's one *growing* power, which is not a compliment. Praetor Rykard, the reader will recall, had ended his war by feeding himself — Great Rune and all — to the God-Devouring Serpent of Mt. Gelmir, becoming the thing he fed. His household did not treat this as a bereavement. Volcano Manor, under his consort **Tanith**, made itself the age's polite abomination: a house of recusants — Tarnished who had renounced the Two Fingers' guidance outright — dispatched under contract to murder other Tarnished, in open defiance of the Erdtree and everything grace was calling its champions home to do. The manor's own letters attest the trade. Among its blades was Bernahl, once a seeker of lordship himself; around its table sat Tanith's daughter Rya, serpent-born by nature and adopted into the household's secret, which was this: below the parlors, the Lord in his serpent flesh consumed the champions his family sent down. The interregnum starved every other power on the map. The mountain alone was fed.

## The world below

The reader has walked the surface. The age's true business was conducted underneath it.

Beneath the earth the Eternal Cities, Nokron and Nokstella, kept the silence of their ancient banishment beneath false stars — silent all through this age, and sealed; the way down to Nokron would not open, as it happened, until Starscourge Radahn died, though no one living understood the lock. The ancestral peoples of the deep river-vales kept their horned worship and their hostility to all comers. None of this was new. What was new — what made the underworld the interregnum's most consequential region — was growing downward from a corpse.

Prince **Godwyn**, first of the demigods to die, had died in soul only; his soulless body, interred among the Erdtree's roots, did not have the grace to lie still. From it, through all the years of the interruption, Death itself spread along the roots as Deathroot, and wherever the root reached, the dead rose. Those Who Live in Death — the age's quietest and most patient faction, recruited entirely from graveyards. Against them the Order fielded its hunters, fiercest among them the knight **D**, who made the extermination of the deathly a sacrament. And within this very Hold there lived his opposite: **Fia**, a deathbed companion of the old southern custom, who warmed dying champions in her arms — an embrace the wary noted was not free — and who held in her heart the precisely contrary faith, that those who live in death deserved not the pyre but a place in the Order. Hunter and companion shared these walls in this era, courteous and irreconcilable. The lords above fought over shards; the argument below was over Death itself, and Death was winning converts.

And deeper than all of it, below even the banished stars, the age's one *founder* was at work. Descend toward Mohgwyn Palace and the dark begins to redden. The palace stands in a cavern no day has ever entered, above a lake that is not water: a wide, patient sheen the color of dark wine, lacquered where the braziers strike it, clotting at its shores as no honest lake behaves. Ruby light climbs the terraces. Incense drifts over the surface the way mist drifts over true water — sweet smoke laid across the iron smell like a coverlet. And above the lake rises architecture built for descendants: colonnades, a mausoleum's dignity, a throne at the head of ceremonial stairs — a court in every furnishing but one, for the dynasty all of it awaited numbered, through the whole of this age, exactly two: one worshipper, and one sleeping god.

**Mohg** — Morgott's twin, the other shackled son, the one who in the dark beneath the capital had found not faith but the Formless Mother, an outer god of wounds — had taken for himself what no other power of the age thought to take: a god. At some hour of the war or after it that no relic dates, the cocoon of the Empyrean Miquella was cut open at the Haligtree's heart, and the sleeper within was spirited away to that red-lit palace. There the Lord of Blood built a dynasty around a bridegroom who never woke: he preached the coming kingdom through his surgeon-priests and his white-masked recruiter of "bloody fingers," he addressed the cocoon in the manner of a courtier before a sleeping king, and — his own remembrance concedes it, which is why I believe it — the young Empyrean never once responded. Whether that silence was the whole of the story, and whose design the abduction finally served, this history will weigh when it follows the expedition into the veiled land, for the accounts that reached this Hold from there are not simple. For now let the interregnum have its emblem: of all the courts of the age, the only one building toward a coronation was a jail with a wedding planned.

## The tree in the far north

One region refuses the chapter's tone, and earns the refusal. In the frozen north, past the snowfields, stood the **Haligtree** — Miquella's tree, watered with his own blood, raised to be everything the Erdtree had failed to be: a shelter for the Order's castoffs, the Albinaurics and Misbegotten and all the gold's discarded children. Come at it through the blizzard and it appears the way mercy itself might appear: a crown of pale branches above the snow-fog, white as candle-wax, holding a light that is not the Erdtree's hard brass noon but something thinner and cleaner — an unalloyed light, gold with everything cruel refined out of it. Then the eye adjusts. The branches have stopped mid-reach, like a hand halted in the act of giving. The light falls and goes nowhere — ripens nothing, raises nothing, only pools in boughs that ceased to grow the day their maker was taken into Mohg's keeping. Through the interregnum the refugees still came; the sick tree still stood; and the rot worked upward through the city in its boughs, patient as damp in a beam.

What remained was the vigil. **Malenia**, carried home from Aeonia by her own knight, slept her long sleep at the tree's crown, the rot in her banked but not beaten, while her Cleanrot Knights — who had followed her rotting body knowingly, which is the whole definition of that order — held Elphael around her against the very corruption their lady carried. The knight Loretta, who had left Carian service to find the Albinaurics a haven, stood guard on the approaches. No power of the age attacked the Haligtree, and none needed to.

## The Hold outside the world

Now, the frame: this Hold itself.

All through the age, the machinery of guidance ground on. Grace had begun to return — I will come to it — and the returning Tarnished gathered here, in this Hold outside the world, where the enterprise wore every appearance of divine administration: the All-Knowing building his files, champions trading rumor of Great Runes, and in the inmost chamber the **Two Fingers**, oracle of the Greater Will, gesturing their slow gestures while the Finger Reader **Enia** rendered them into instruction. Seek the shards. Slay the shardbearers. Stand before the Elden Ring. Every Tarnished of the era took direction, at whatever remove, from that chamber.

Of that chamber I will say less than I know. Testimony reached this Hold late in its life, out of the veiled land, concerning the mother of all Two Fingers — root of every finger that ever gestured — and the condition in which she was found. It is not this age's evidence, and I will not spend it here; it belongs to the expedition that earned it. For this survey a single entry suffices: the Fingers stand in that chamber yet, a corridor from where I write, enormous, patient, moving — and since the testimony came to these shelves, I have not once gone in. Every history has a passage its author walks quickly.

So picture the age entire, as the evidence leaves it. Above: a graftsman in the south, two mad households on the lake, a wasteland worshipping its own infection, a hidden king guarding a tree that will not open, a mountain quietly eating heroes. Below: Death budding along the roots, and a blood-lit palace rehearsing a coronation. Far north, a mercy stalled at half-grown. And over it all, out of a chamber in this Hold, guidance — confident, continuous, and possibly connected to nothing.

Then grace moved. Not to the demigods; the golden line of it, which every returning exile learned to see hanging in the air, did not point at any of the broken heirs. It pointed past them. It gathered the disinherited — the descendants of Godfrey's banished warriors, the graceless, the maidenless, some long dead in far lands and summoned anyway across the fog. One of these, a knight called **Vyke**, came nearer the Elden Throne than any Tarnished of the age — nearer than any before the stranger this book is marching toward — and then, at the threshold of everything, went down beneath the capital instead, to the sealed thing with three fingers, and took its brand, and ended as a burned madman haunting the lake country. His own armor, our best witness, poses his reason as a question and declines to answer it: for his maiden's sake, that she might not burn — or lured by a whispering the relic does not name. The whisperer had a voice in those years: the flame's recruiter Shabriri, remembered as the most reviled slanderer in this land's history, his eyes long since put out for it, went about the age wearing the corpse of a fallen hunter called Yura and talking, by every account, with terrible good cheer. Some scholars venture that the old entombed merchants' caravan was his slander's work as well; I leave it among the theories nothing compels.

Vyke was the sanctioned path walked nearly to its end — by a champion with his maiden beside him and grace at his back — and it broke him a step from the door. That is the interregnum's last and most instructive relic: proof that the golden guidance had already spent its best candidate before the story this whole book exists to tell had even begun. The age of broken heirs was over; it simply had not stopped. What follows belongs to the nobodies grace pointed at — and to the one nobody who did not turn aside.
