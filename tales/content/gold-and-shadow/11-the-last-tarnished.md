# Chapter XI: The Last Tarnished

*Wherein a nameless stranger walks out of the sea-fog into the wreckage of the golden age, and the wreckage — thrones, festivals, conspiracies, dying kings — arranges itself into a road; and the road ends at a wall of thorns.*

---

Every historian dreams of the subject who leaves too much behind — the queen with ten thousand statues, the crusader whose remembrance was carved by grief itself. I have been given the opposite dream: the most consequential person in this entire history is its least documented. No relic bears this person's name — no epitaph, no banner, no inscription; not one of the seven rooms around me, assembled by a man who made *knowing* his title and his religion, holds so much as an initial. Gideon Ofnir kept a file on every demigod and every heresy in the Lands Between, and on the one visitor who ended all of them he kept nothing. The record says only *Tarnished*, which is a category, not a name; there were hundreds, and this book has already buried several. Whether the stranger was young or old, man or woman or neither, no two testimonies agree, and most do not trouble to say. There is no face; there is no country, only the sea-fog the Tarnished all crossed, and the fog keeps no ledger. I have settled on **the stranger**, and I ask the reader to hear in that word both of its meanings: the one who is unknown, and the one who does not belong.

I have spent my working life on named greatness, trusting a proportion I never thought to examine: that consequence leaves testimony in its own measure — that the great are the well-attested, and the silent are the small. At the hinge of the entire age, the proportion fails. The largest deed in this history has no doer in the record. The floor of my profession did not break beneath that fact; it gave, the way a stair-tread gives once under the boot, enough to change how a man climbs.

I know the stranger, then, the way a fisherman knows the stone flung into his pond while his back was turned — never the stone, only the rings still widening. What survives is aftermath: a grafted king in pieces on his own floor; a festival ground gone quiet; a wolf's body cold outside a witch's empty tower. The hand that made them passed through this land like weather, and it is by the damage that I will chart the storm. One irony deserves recording: in a country where a god raised ten thousand statues of herself, the person who mattered most has not one.

These seven rooms feel different to me now. Gideon Ofnir set out to know everything, and the files prove how nearly he did — a demigod's fevers here, a heresy's genealogy there, every door in the Lands Between catalogued to its hinges. Then the one visitor who mattered walked through his own hall and the All-Knowing made no entry. His collection is complete the way a net is complete, and the catch it was woven for passed through without touching a knot. I work inside that net. I have begun, on some evenings, to notice the holes.

One fixed fact opens the account, and it is fixed because the first witness would not shut up about it. When the stranger came ashore in Limgrave, a white-masked war-surgeon was waiting at the roadside — Varré, he called himself, a man whose courtesies all had hooks in them — and his greeting survives: the newcomer was graceless, guideless, and *maidenless*, lacking even the finger maiden who turns a Tarnished's slaughter into strength. The sneer measures the beginning: the stranger arrived owning nothing this land counted. Remember the mask; we shall come back to whose errands the mocking surgeon ran.

The second fixed fact is the accord. At a site of grace in those same fields, the stranger was met by a girl who should not have been possible — burned, bodiless, one eye sealed, arriving like a thought rather than a traveler. She named herself Melina, and proposed terms with the brevity of someone who has rehearsed them a long time: she would play the maiden's part, turning runes to strength; she would lend a spectral steed; she would carry the stranger to the Roundtable Hold, the gathering-hall of Tarnished champions that stands outside the world. In exchange, the stranger would one day take her to the foot of the Erdtree. What she wanted there, she did not say — and what she wanted there is the hinge on which this entire history turns. That account — and her mother, and her purpose — waits at the foot of the Erdtree, and we shall stand there together before this book is done. For now let her be what the stranger knew: a quiet passenger, and the better half of a bargain.

*On the sources, before the thrones begin to fall.* The reader deserves to know why this chapter, alone in this book, cannot walk in a straight line. Grace guided one walker, and the walker kept no diary; the witnesses each saw one bend of the road and assumed it was the road. The accounts place the stranger in Caelid before Liurnia and in Liurnia before Caelid, and disagree about the order of the demigods' deaths. What the record does fix — because the Order's own machinery fixed it — are the gates: no Tarnished entered Leyndell without two Great Runes restored, by the Finger Reader Enia's own protocols; Nokron did not open until Radahn died; the thorns were not found until Morgott fell. So I will give you the gates in their certain order, and between them the contending accounts, merged never; a history that pretends one road through an open country is only a route drawn afterward by someone who was never lost.

## The first thrones

Stormveil fell first — on this, unusually, the accounts agree, if only because Stormveil was nearest the sea. Its lord was Godrick, last dribble of the golden lineage, who grafted the limbs of the defeated onto his own body and had lately begun on dragons. The stranger went through his storm-wracked castle and through him; what is left of Godrick the Grafted is a Great Rune and a warning. Of the aftermath, one tradition — attested by the fort-lord Kenneth Haight and the turncoat gate-keep Gostoc, neither a liar though both self-interested — holds that the warrior Nepheli Loux, cast off by Gideon Ofnir, took Stormveil's masterless throne and ruled it well. The rune the stranger carried to the divine tower off Limgrave's coast and woke, and with that act the demigods' war acquired its executor.

Liurnia's rune was taken more gently. In the Grand Library of Raya Lucaria the stranger found the wound this history has already watched being made: Rennala, queen of the full moon, unhealed — the amber egg in her arms, Radagon's parting gift, if *gift* is the word for what a man leaves behind in place of himself, and around her the scholars who had volunteered to be reborn as children. The library's defenses were not hers; a protective enchantment, laid — the relics of the place agree — by her absent daughter Ranni, fought in the mother's stead. When it broke, there was no queen to kill, only a woman holding an egg. The stranger took the Great Rune of the Unborn and left Rennala alive in her bed of books, still cradling the amber. Few of this history's mercies were free; this one, so far as the record shows, was.

## The festival of a dying sun

What happened at Redmane Castle deserves to be remembered exactly, because nothing else in the Shattering's long horror resembles it. Radahn — Starscourge Radahn, who had halted the very stars and never outgrown scrawny Leonard, whom this history left rot-mad and eating corpses in Caelid after Aeonia — could not be cured and could not be left. So the old witch-hunter **Jerren**, Radahn's companion-at-arms, did the one thing a soldier could still do for him: he declared a festival.

Understand the country this happened in. Caelid by then was the rot's own garden — forests gone soft with bloom, the air itself carrying sickness — and in the middle of it, a castle put up bunting. The Redmane banners flew from every tower; shields were scrubbed and repainted and hung along the walls the way a hall is set for a feast; braziers burned in open daylight, as if light itself were being spent on principle. Champions came — among them Blaidd, the wolf-knight of Ranni's little court, and a living war-jar called Alexander — and the castle threw a *wake for a man still breathing*: Jerren's convocation held that a warrior of Radahn's stature deserved to die in combat rather than rot standing up. Call it what it was — the last chivalry of a rotted country, burning its best story as an offering: everything the land still remembered about honor, spent on one afternoon so that its greatest son could be killed while the word *warrior* still fit him. The stranger fought in that festival. At its end the general was dead, and the festival did not cheer; it went quiet, the way a sickroom goes quiet when the labored breathing stops.

And that same night, witnesses across Limgrave saw a star fall.

Attend closely: this is where the chapter's two histories touch. Radahn's private war against the heavens had been, all along, a lock: the stars steer Carian fate, and while he held them nothing written in them could move. His death released them. The fallen star tore open the earth of Limgrave and laid bare a stairway into **Nokron**, the eternal city beneath the world — and the person for whom that door opened, whose frozen fate resumed the instant the general stopped breathing, was not at the festival. Ranni the witch profited from Radahn's death precisely to the degree that she was absent from it. I do not say she engineered the festival; Jerren's grief was his own. But the war counselor Iji and Blaidd both attest that their lady needed the stars unbound and Nokron unsealed, and both required her brother dead. When a death this convenient arrives this punctually, a historian is permitted to raise one eyebrow, and I raise it here.

## The witch's war on the Fingers

What follows is pieced from the saddest cluster of relics in this collection: a wolf's greatsword, a giant's mirrored helm, and the testimony of a court that died serving its lady. The stranger descended into Nokron and brought Ranni what she had waited an age for: the **Fingerslayer Blade**, the eternal city's ancient treason made metal, a weapon fashioned to cut the servants of the Greater Will itself. With it in hand, the witch moved to finish what the Night of the Black Knives began: not a throne, but an escape — the severing of her soul from the Two Fingers appointed to steer it.

The price was paid first by her household. Preceptor Seluvis, her puppet-maker, kept a cellar of once-living dolls; the accounts differ on whom he schemed to add to it, each naming a different victim — a warrior of Godfrey's line, the caged defiler of Leyndell, Gideon Ofnir himself. He died when his lady left, or by her sentence for scheming to dose her into docility; the accounts differ there too, and he is mourned by neither. Blaidd, the wolf given to Ranni by the Two Fingers at her Empyrean selection — companion and fail-safe in one skin — was locked away by Iji, who says why plainly: the Fingers' leash was strangling his friend's own will. The wolf broke free, and the leash won. He was found dead outside Ranni's empty tower, sword in hand, raving against the masters who built him; and Iji, who wears the blame in his last recorded words, was found afterward at his post, burned, amid the corpses of Black Knife assassins whose sender no relic names. The stranger, meanwhile, carried the war to its end: down through Nokstella, across the lake of rot, past the Baleful Shadow — the Fingers' own assassin-breed, sent to do to Ranni what Blaidd could not — and at last to the cathedral of Manus Celes, beneath whose floor the relics take over. What lies there still is a pair of **Two Fingers, dead** — the only such corpse in the record, the Order's living instrument cut down like meat. That Ranni's own hand dealt the blow is inferred by everyone and seen by no one; the scene is arranged like a confession, and I read it as one, noting that arranged scenes are this land's favorite art form. One account carries the tale a step further: the stranger climbing to the witch's side, and a cold ring changing hands. What was promised with it, I have sworn not to guess.

## The dreamers in the roots

The Roundtable Hold, in these same seasons, hosted a quieter horror. Among its champions sat a pale woman named **Fia**, a deathbed companion — one whose embrace comforts dying heroes by drawing warmth from the living — and a knight called **D, Hunter of the Dead**, whose hatred of Those Who Live in Death was armor-deep and then some. Those Who Live in Death the reader has met already — the unquiet consequence of Godwyn's half-death, rising in the roots beneath the world. D hunted the symptom. Fia loved the cause. One morning the Hold found D dead with a weathered dagger, and Fia gone; no witness saw the blow, but when a woman vanishes in the same hour as her enemy's last breath, the inference writes itself, and the Hold wrote it. She went where everything in her story was always going: down, to Deeproot, where Godwyn the Golden lies tangled in the Erdtree's roots — first of the demigods to die, his soul gone and his body dreadfully industrious. There, with a cursemark recovered from Ranni's discarded flesh — the two halves of that murdered night meeting one last time — Fia lay down beside the prince and dreamed. Out of the dream came two relics I can touch: the remembrance of **Lichdragon Fortissax**, Godwyn's sworn companion, corrupted in his long fight against the Death rooted in his friend, and destroyed by the stranger — who entered the dream by what door I cannot say; and a mending rune, born, its own inscription states, of the deathbed companion and the Prince of Death: a law that would fold true Death back into the Order that once expelled it. Fia is not recorded again after her labor. One account gives her body a last visitor: D's twin, handed his dead brother's armor by the stranger's own charity, standing over her with his brother's face and his brother's blade. The accounts differ on whether this happened; none differ on what it would mean.

## Three words on a bridge

Of all the roads in this chapter, the one I return to at night is the shortest: a priest and a silent man standing on a broken bridge east of Leyndell, looking at a statue. The silent man was **Goldmask**, held to be the era's greatest student of the Golden Order's flaws — a wordless ascetic in cloth-of-gold whose fingers worked the air endlessly, computing, his admirers said, the Order's arithmetic where it failed to balance. The priest was **Corhyn** of the Roundtable, who left the Hold's comforts to be the great man's mouthpiece. They had come to a contradiction concerning Radagon that Goldmask could not resolve, and the resolution, when the stranger supplied it — casting the Order's own law of regression, the pull of all things toward unity, before the capital's great statue of Radagon — was three words. The statue's hidden inscription, read in that incantation's light, says: *Radagon is Marika.*

The reader has been carrying, since the church of vows, the question its keeper Miriel confessed he could not answer — why Radagon abandoned Rennala, what he was going *to*. Here is the answer the bridge received: he was going to himself. The second Elden Lord and the queen he served were one flesh; the impossible marriage was a god wedding her own reflection; the amber egg was left by the same hand that would one day break the world. What interests the historian is what the revelation did to the two men who received it. Goldmask did not despair; he *computed*. If a god could waver, could be two quarreling people in one skin, could break her own Ring — then the flaw was not in the law but in housing law inside a god, and he began devising a mending rune to cure exactly that: order made immutable, beyond divine caprice. Corhyn broke. The faith that had carried him out of the Roundtable could not carry three words; the accounts of his end differ — raving against his master, say some; simply lost, say others. One man heard that god and queen were one and redesigned heaven; the other heard the same sentence and lost his footing on the earth.

Two more threads, briefly — one held at arm's length. Beneath Leyndell, in a gaol the Order built for him, sat the **Dung Eater**, whose seedbed curse mutilated souls out of the Erdtree's cycle of rebirth entirely, and whose stated ambition — preserved in his own words, which I decline to savor — was a rune to curse every birth to come, so that a defiled world could despise no one. The accounts differ on the stranger's dealings with him, and for once I am glad of the fog. And on Mt. Gelmir, Volcano Manor fell: the recusant house of Lady Tanith, whose letters of contract — several rest in this collection, which raises questions about this collection — sent Tarnished to murder Tarnished in their god-defying lord's name. That lord was Rykard, Rennala's son, who had fed himself to the God-Devouring Serpent rather than kneel; the stranger reached him — as the manor's guest or its enemy, the accounts differ — and ended serpent and praetor together. Tanith was found afterward at the corpse, feeding, which her own recusants report without judgment and I report the same way. Some accounts carry the stranger further still — to the Haligtree at the world's frozen edge, and Malenia's last bloom — but that thread is knotted into Miquella's story, and I will not tear it loose here: it will be paid where he is paid, in full, and the reader may keep this page as the bond.

And below everything, in a palace of blood, a dynasty ended before it began. **Mohg, Lord of Blood** — Morgott's Omen twin, whom this history left curled around a stolen, sleeping Empyrean, dreaming of a consort-king and a bloodline of his own — died at the stranger's hand in his wedding-hall of a mausoleum. The white-masked surgeon of the First Step had been his recruiter all along; the first voice this chapter recorded was the herald of its buried monster. What Mohg's death set loose belongs to another country, and is no part of this chapter's account.

## The Omen King, and the wall

Leyndell came last of the gates, as the Order's own law required: two Great Runes to pass the capital's seal. The stranger entered under Gransax's dragon-corpse, past walls only ever breached once, and was met first by a golden ghost: a shade of Godfrey, First Elden Lord, set like a watchman at the approach by a hand no relic identifies. Hold that image — the first lord, guarding the stair; this land does not post its ghosts idly.

On the throne sat the king this book has been keeping in the dark since the sewers first closed over him, and I give him his chapter's end because no one else earned it the way he did. **Morgott, the Omen King** — Marika's son, Godfrey's by every chained inference of the lineage-texts, born horned and cursed and caged for it in the sewers of the city he would die for. His reign is unrequited love with a crown on it: the boy the Order shackled underground grew into the Order's last defender, serving a grace that never once favored him. By every sign that survives — the same weapons wound the same flesh, the same voice continues the same sentences — the hooded fiend called Margit who ambushed lord-hopeful Tarnished on the roads was Morgott himself in a beggar's veil, prosecuting the capital's defense from a ditch; no text states it, but the relics lean together and I will treat it as history. When the stranger reached the throne room, the veiled king rose out of his beggar's shape and gave the age its funeral oration — branding every shardbearer this chapter has buried a traitor to the Erdtree, and naming himself the last of all kings. Then he fought with his cursed blood for the city that had caged him, and died for the tree that had never looked at him. The reader may judge which of Marika's children loved her Order best; the historian only notes, dryly, that it was the one the Order kept in a hole.

Past the dead king, the stranger walked up to claim what every gate had been opening toward — and here this chapter's road, and every road in the Lands Between, ends at the same masonry. The Erdtree would not open. Before the stranger stood a wall of thorns — grown, not built: living gold hardened into a prison. Briars thick as bridge-cables crossed and recrossed the way in; the sap had set in them like resin, honey annealed to brass, so that what glowed like the tree's own light met the hand as metal. It did not bar the door the way a gate bars it, for a gate implies a key. It was the tree *clenched* — scripture shut against its reader — vast beyond siegecraft, proof against every blade and every common flame the record knows. The Two Fingers at the Roundtable bent themselves into communion and went silent; Enia the Finger Reader confessed the Greater Will's answer might be longer coming than a human life. The stranger now walked on private judgment, at the top of a world with no further gates. Grace had led its champion to a locked tree and stopped talking.

The silence left a stranger with no name, every throne behind and a wall ahead, and one quiet passenger still holding half of an old bargain. The thorns would yield to nothing in this chapter. They would yield to fire — a particular fire, from a forge this history has visited once before, bought at a price I have been withholding since Limgrave. But between the thorn and the flame, this history owes a debt in another country, where a long-sealed door was opening and a god's abandoned body had begun to move. The stranger's war pauses here. Turn the page, reader, into shadow; the burning will keep.
