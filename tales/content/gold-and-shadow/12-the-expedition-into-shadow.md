# Chapter XII: The Expedition into Shadow

*Wherein a god unmakes himself piece by piece along the road to heaven; a fellowship wakes from love as from a drug; the erased son dies cursing his mother's name; and the guidance of ages is weighed, and found, perhaps, to have been the ringing of a cracked bell.*

---

While the stranger was breaking the demigods one by one in the Lands Between, a second history was happening in the country where this book began, uncounted centuries after we left it, and the two histories are one story wearing two coats. Let me fix the hour plainly, for it matters: this expedition began after Starscourge Radahn died at his festival in Caelid, and after Mohg, Lord of Blood, was slain in his palace under the earth — and it ended before the Erdtree burned. What concerns the expedition came to me on the lips and in the packs of those who went into the Land of Shadow and came out again. They were few. None came back whole, and no two came back agreeing, and I have spent more nights with their testimonies than with any other bundle in this collection, because what they describe, if it happened as they swear, is the closest this history ever came to beginning again. One parallel thread reaches this chapter by a different road — from a remembrance, a flower, and the stranger's few words — and I will declare the change of evidence when we come to it.

It begins, as the age of gold did, with someone climbing toward a gate. But two roads must be laid beside each other before the expedition can be followed to its end: the road of the god who shed everything, and the road of the sister who would not be shed.

## Part I: The twins divided

### What he left on the road

**Miquella**, called the Kind, has entered this history twice already: once as the golden child of the golden age, the Empyrean whom no cure could age; once as the sleeper stolen from his own Haligtree — for it stands confirmed, by the Blood Lord's own remembrance and the testimony of this library's late owner, that Mohg cut the cocoon from the tree's heart and carried it to his palace, meaning to raise the child-god with himself as consort. The theft is undated. Whether Miquella himself willed it — whether the abductor was, from the first, the abducted's instrument — perhaps, and I give the word its exact weight. What no one disputes is what was found when the stranger's war reached that palace: a cocoon already vacated, save for one withered arm. Miquella had gone ahead, into the sealed land, and left a piece of himself behind as a door-handle.

That arm was the first relinquishment. It was not the last. Across the Land of Shadow the expedition found golden crosses raised along his road — gold, in a country of soot and extinguished color, each a struck match in the gloom — inscribed in his own voice with what he had set down at its foot. They are not milestones; they are stations of self-amputation, and I want the reader to walk them as the witnesses walked them, because the sequence is the man. At the first cross on the Gravesite Plain, the first of his flesh. More flesh before the gates of a moonlit castle. At Belurat, tower settlement of the Hornsent, an arm — and those who match the Belurat cross to the withered thing in the cocoon make an argument I find hard to refuse, though no inscription closes it. In the ancient ruins of Rauh, the last of his flesh. And then the crosses stop recording the body and begin recording the soul. On the Cerulean Coast, his doubt and his vacillation. Above a great fissure in the earth, his love. On the spiral rise of Enir-Ilim itself, the last station before heaven: all of his fears.

Set beside these the relic recovered at the foot of the shadow-tree — his own Great Rune, abandoned, broken, empty of every blessing but one — whose text states his purpose without decoration: he had come to the shadowed tower forsaking everything, his fate included, to bury an original sin and be reborn a god. The reader has met that phrase before; it was the company Marika chose for her hidden son. I do not know what the sin was, and I will not guess; but the word *bury* has now been used of it by two generations, and buried things in this history do not stay so.

Consider what the crosses describe. A man who sheds his doubt cannot reconsider. A man who sheds his fears cannot be warned. And a man who sheds his love — the cross above the fissure reads, in his voice, "I abandon here my love" — has decided in advance that whatever he does next will be done to people he no longer has the equipment to pity. The Kind unmade his kindness on the road to godhood and called each amputation a gift to the world he meant to save. Some of the returned told me this with awe. Others had understood it.

For the love was not a feeling. It was a person — or half of one. At the bottom of that fissure, past a knight made of the land's clotted dead, the witnesses describe the shadow relenting at last into violet: a garden banked in blooms the color of a bruise, and in their midst a colossal wilted flower with human faces asleep among the petals, sleep lying over them like warm water over the drowning. This was **St. Trina**, the saint of sleep, whom the old rumors could never decide was a girl or a boy. That Trina *is* Miquella — the discarded other half, the love itself, left where the cross says the love was left — no single text states, and I will show my reasoning as I am sworn to: the poisoner **Thiollier**, her one devotee in the company, spoke of her as his lord's other self; the cross stands directly above her garden; and the old sword-rumor had tangled their names together an age before. Three relics, one conclusion leaning out of them. I adopt it. And it is on Thiollier's testimony — a man who drank her nectar past the edge of death to hear her — that we have her words: that godhood would be a cage for Miquella; that a caged divinity is beyond anyone's saving; and that to kill him would be a mercy — his forgiveness, she called it. The discarded half of the god, pleading from a crack in the earth for the death of the half that walked on. I have recorded many testimonies in this book. I know of none sadder.

### What would not be left

The reader has walked the crosses and knows the inventory: flesh, doubt, love, fears — a self itemized and set down piece by piece on the climb. Now mark the word that appears at no station on that road. Miquella had a twin — **Malenia**, born of the one god in the one flesh, cursed in the birth as he was cursed, the rot to his changelessness; his Blade, all her life, by her own unbroken choice. No cross records that he laid her down. A man cannot shed what he does not carry, and by every sign that survives, Malenia was never carried: she had planted herself, long before, at the root of his tree — and while the god unmade himself in shadow, station by station, the one thing that would not accept abandonment was keeping it still.

Her fall is the thread this book declined, amid the stranger's war, to tear loose from her brother's story. This chapter is that story, and I pay the debt here, though it came out of no witness's pack: the two histories are one story wearing two coats, and this seam is where the coats are sewn. No witness left that room save the one who caused what happened in it, so I build the scene, as I once built a murdered night out of a dagger and two wounds, from the three relics it left: the remembrance pressed from her soul, carried back to this Hold and rendered in the chamber down the corridor from where I write; the few words the stranger let fall here touching that fight; and the word of those since come down the frozen road, that a scarlet flower stands at the tree's root yet. A soul, a handful of words, a bloom — I put nothing in the scene the three cannot hold.

She met the stranger at the lowest root, beside the slashed cavity where her brother's cocoon had hung — a vigil kept over an emptiness, by a woman no relic says was ever told what the emptiness meant. Her sword-arm and both legs were gold, prosthetics fitted so that the rot's amputations need never retire the blade; the rot itself was in her as it had been since her first breath — banked, not beaten, coral at the seams of her, the fever her knights' sealed armor was forged to keep out. Of how she fought, the relics speak twice: an heirloom engraving of a girl born into the accursed rot, who met a mentor with a flowing blade and gained wings of unmatched strength — it names no one, and I know of one woman in this history who fought winged and rotting both — and her own blade, drawn out of the remembrance, keeping the name of its art: a dance of water-fowl. Whoever named it saw wings. Her boast the reader has heard already, in its own words, in the chapter where a country died proving it. By her own accounting she was unbeaten — and the accounting was honest; it simply never entered what her victories cost the ground they stood on.

What every channel agrees on is the flower. At some hour of that fight the rot she had spent a lifetime refusing stopped being refused — spent as a weapon, or claiming its arrears; no relic adjudicates — and opened its second great bloom at the root of the tree raised to be rot's opposite. It unfolded around her like a throne built in a single breath, petals the breadth of doors taking the Haligtree's pallid light and returning it scarlet; for one interval the woman, the flower, and the title waiting inside both were impossible to separate. Out of it she rose renamed. The remembrance pressed from her soul does not say Blade of Miquella; it says *Goddess of Rot* — coronation or symptom, I hold it at the ledger's weight, for the sage of the rot counted the blooms and preached that a third would seat her as a true goddess, and the count is a worshipper's arithmetic, and there was no third. She died at the second. Her death, the stranger's spare words carry in this shape: she yielded to the only opponent her accounting would admit — one she named worthy, at last, of the word *lord* — and her final words were not for the victor. They were an apology, to her dearest brother, whom she had served past the end of his needing her; and a warning, that the rot would bloom again in its season. I will not write that she was defeated. She left the word out of her ledger to the last, and examining the entry, I cannot fault it.

Hold the section's two ends together, for they are the chapter in miniature: in the shadow, a god shedding everything that could ache; at the root, the one thing he never managed to shed — loyalty of her order is not carried in the hand, and cannot be set down at a wayside by the man it follows. Whether her fall ever climbed the stairs to him, whether she ever learned what he had gone to become, the record keeps two silences; between them stands the flower.

One account more belongs at that root, and it is a payment. In the wound's own country this history met a girl found as a babe in the Aeonia swamp — of Malenia's blood; sister, daughter, or offshoot, her own testimony declines to rule, and so must I — tended by the sage of the rot toward a flowering with a purpose all its own, to be met in its season. The season fell in these years, and the tending had a doctrine under it: the sage was the flower's partisan, raising a bud toward the vessel's crown Malenia had refused for a lifetime. **Millicent** chose otherwise, armed with a craft of Miquella's own: a needle of unalloyed gold, forged against the meddling of outer gods — forged, its inscription confesses, for Malenia, whom it could not save. It saved something smaller, which I have come to think the harder work. Her road ran north and ended, where else, at her blood's own tree, and there the accounts divide: in one telling she stood off buds of the same bloom that came against her as sisters, then drew the needle out with her own hand and died as herself, whole, rather than ripen into anyone's goddess; in another the stranger's blade turned on her, and I give that telling no more floor than this clause. One tradition carries the needle onward — into the scarlet flower standing where the Blade of Miquella fell, the brother's craft driven at the last into the sister's bloom. I say only that the accounts place it there; and that of everything left standing at the Haligtree, a golden needle in a scarlet flower is the entry I would keep.

That was the road running north while Miquella's ran upward. The two do not meet again. The company, which did not know what had happened at the root, returns us now to shadow.

## Part II: The company in shadow

### The company that woke

Miquella did not cross alone, though he crossed first. Behind him came a fellowship, and the reader should hear their names, because their unraveling is the truest measure we have of their lord's power. **Needle Knight Leda**, last knight of an order she herself had purged, who led them. **Sir Ansbach**, once Mohg's own pureblood knight — an old, bloody, clear-eyed man who had tried to free his lord from Miquella's enchantment and been enchanted for his trouble. **Redmane Freyja**, Radahn's soldier, whom Miquella had healed of the rot after Aeonia. **Moore**, a gentle forager who kept company with the meek kindred of the rot. **Dryleaf Dane**, a monk who fought with his hands and said almost nothing. **Thiollier**, whom we have met, serving the saint and not the god. And one whom the accounts name only **the Hornsent**: a survivor of the tower people, marching beside Erdtree faithful because his lord had promised him vengeance on the Impaler — and because, like all of them, he was charmed.

I use the word technically. Miquella's power to compel love was not courtly rumor; the All-Knowing whose library I sit in feared him above every Empyrean for precisely this. Midway through the expedition something broke — the witnesses describe a distant shattering, felt rather than heard — and the fellowship's affection dissolved with it. His Great Rune had broken; the charm went with the rune; and every member of that company chose freely, some of them for the first time in years.

What they chose is the indictment. Leda, given back her suspicion, re-chose Miquella — harder — and began to measure her companions for killing. Ansbach woke to what he had known before the charm took him: that his dead lord was to be *used*, and how. Freyja went looking for the truth about her old general. Thiollier went down the fissure to his saint. Moore, asked to choose for himself, found he scarcely could. The Hornsent chose vengeance, and then vengeance without a floor. Blood followed within days.

*On the sources, a difficulty.* The returned agree the fellowship turned on itself; they do not agree who turned on whom, and I will not pretend they do. In one telling Leda's needle found the Hornsent first, judging him rabid; in another her ambush fell on old Ansbach in the archive-keep. Freyja stands at Leda's side in some accounts of the last battle on the tower stairs, and in others never left the storehouse where she read Ansbach's letter about Radahn's fate. Moore is dead in one account, absent in a second, and in a third stands sadly on the stairs with the rest. Dane alone appears in every roster of that final remnant, silent to the end. I set the versions side by side and choose none; the lesson survives every variant. A love that requires enchantment is not love, and when the enchantment lapsed, the kind lord's company did what companies of the un-charmed have always done in this history: it killed itself over him.

### The warden's end

The land they crossed to do all this was the land where this history began, and I confess the witnesses' descriptions cost me some composure, for I had assembled that country out of relics and here were men who had smelled it. Belurat stands yet, thick with the dead and the near-dead of the tower people. The gaols still hold their fused and crawling failures. An aged grandam of the Hornsent still keeps a shrine-room there — the same angry voice, I am satisfied, that first told this history of the shaman girl raised in the tower's bosom, still cursing the golden line. And through it all Messmer's army still burned. The reader will recall that I left **Messmer the Impaler** sealed in the Land of Shadow as its warden, his crusade done. The returned corrected me: not done. Smoldering. His soldiers still hunted Hornsent survivors as though the order had been given yesterday, because no one with the authority to rescind it had ever come.

And here I owe a correction that will not wait for a quieter page. When first I weighed the grandam, I named her a hostile witness — the testimony of a civilization explaining its own corpse, I said — and discounted her accordingly. The expedition carried my discount into her country and came back without it. The land they walked was, article by article, the land her anger had described: the tower thick with its dead, the gaols at their patient work, the fire burning with no order behind it. So let the discount be retracted by name: I priced her voice low because it was angry, and the anger was carrying facts. The correction costs me exactly what it should — every page built on her being half-wrong must now bear her being right. And I will confess the resentment too: it is a weary thing that the witness my history keeps proving right is the one who curses everything my subject built.

What reached the warden, in place of the authority that never came, was the stranger. The accounts of Messmer's end agree enough that I state them plainly — noting first, because weighing witnesses is my trade, what some of the tellers wanted: the Hornsent of the fellowship had crossed a world for precisely this death, and a wish that strong can dress itself as memory. I have kept to what every returned account holds in common, and what they hold in common needed no dressing.

He received the intruder in his own keep, seat of the garrison that had never stopped burning, and before any blade moved he asked his question — the witnesses render it in nearly one voice, and I paraphrase only as far as their agreement runs: he looked at the graceless creature come climbing toward lordship and asked whether his mother could truly intend her light for a thing so wholly without it. Attend to the grammar of that. The question was not put to the stranger. It was put to *her* — asked upward, past the intruder, by a son whose questions had gone an age unanswered — and when no answer came he did what this history's unanswered children have always done: he supplied one himself, and his answer was fire. The fire, every account agrees, did not behave as honest flame: it sought, it followed, it came around a guard the way a thing with appetite comes around a fence — gold-red, his mother's colors, worn like a uniform he no longer believed in.

The fire failed him. And then, before the witnesses, Messmer did the thing his entire life had been arranged to prevent. He reached into his own face — into the socket his mother had emptied when he was a boy — and tore out the seal of grace she had set there, and crushed it in his fist. The reader has stood at the other end of this image: a divine hand plucking out a child's eye and stopping the wound with gold. Through all the shadowed centuries that seal had been the last golden thing in the whole dark country. It went out by its bearer's own hand. What rose in its place, the accounts insist, was not a man armored in serpents but the serpent at last, wearing what remained of the man — the shoulders relenting into coil, the flame gone from his mother's gold-red to its own base color, the fire kindled from below that his own order's relics never dared call a blessing — and the warden fought his last hour as the thing he had been mutilated in boyhood to contain: not her son with a curse in him, but the curse, with her son in it.

He was killed. He died blessing Marika, and then, with his last strength, cursing her by name; the blessing was his office, the curse was his inheritance, and he discharged both with one breath. Few deaths in this book earn the word *earned* twice over as his — the war criminal, and the child whose mother carved out his eye and hid him with her sins. With him ended the first war of the golden age — the war his mother desired, commanded, and erased — the only way a war with no author left could end: not rescinded but outlived, its general dead at the hand of neither side. The grandam, told of it, is said to have laughed.

Two consequences of that death shaped everything after, and one reaches far beyond the shadow. The first: from the Impaler's remains the stranger took a kindling of his flame, and with it burned the Sealing Tree that closed the way up Enir-Ilim — the warden's own fire opening the door he warded. The second is a sentence attached to that kindling, and I give it exactly the weight the relic gives it: Messmer, it records, bore a vision of fire the relic likens to that of an unnamed younger sister. A sister. Fire-visioned, like him; unnamed, like his father. The reader who has stood with me at that locked door will feel the draft from this one. I know of a woman in the stranger's history whose purpose was burning, whose eye was sealed, and whose mother waits inside the Erdtree; her fire is close now, and we shall meet her in it. The kindling does not name her, and I will not do the relic's work for it. But remember, when that fire comes: the shadow kept a second child of Marika's, and the record admits she existed, and stops.

The rest of the old land the witnesses could report only in pieces. In the ancient ruins of Rauh the expedition found **Romina, Saint of the Bud**, whose church the crusade had burned. Her remembrance is explicit and I envy its economy: in the ashes she discovered a twisted shard of divinity and wove it into the scarlet rot, that buds might root in scorched remains. Rot as consolation; decay as the one faith fire cannot burn down. She sat astride Miquella's road, something between a woman, a centipede, and a flower, and she died there.

And below everything, in woods where no light and no grace reach at all, the witnesses spoke of a manse — a scholar's house, book-lined — where one **Midra** had harbored the Frenzied Flame. Not the crusade but the Hornsent's own inquisitors had come for him, ages before Messmer; they drove a golden greatsword through his head to stall the yellow fire, and left him alive on its point, and his lady Nanaya bade him endure — a word his remembrance records as a curse. He endured for centuries, impaled at his own hearth among his own books. There is no dry line to be had at that man's door. One account swears that at the last he stopped enduring; that he rose, a lord of the mad flame entire; and that the stranger put him down. I record that rising as the testimony it is; the impalement, the endurance, the waiting, the relics confirm. I am a man who sits alone in a library asking questions the record refuses to answer, and I will say only this professionally: frenzy is what that posture becomes when the questions ask back. I have burned my notes on the Abyssal Woods, and I decline to apologize.

### The cracked bell

I come now to the discovery I have delayed, because it sits under this entire book like water under a floor.

East in the shadow lands stands a cathedral, Manus Metyr, and in it the expedition met **Count Ymir**, a high priest of the Fingers — those oracle-hands through which the Greater Will's grace guided Marika's order, named its Empyreans, and beckoned the Tarnished home. Ymir had come, as I have, to mistrust his sources. By the ringing of bells at ruins shaped like vast dead hands he called up the thing he sought, and beneath his cathedral the witnesses beheld it. A body that is mostly hand: fingers in the place of a face, fingers carrying it over the stone, fingers trailing behind like the tail of the star it once was — as though something had been told, once, that a hand is how one signals, and had made itself all signal. It moved in a drowned-silver light no candle lent it, and against its breast it cradled a small turning heaven, a microcosm of stars, held as a mother holds an infant — which is what the relics name her: **Metyr, Mother of Fingers**. Her remembrance, carried back, states her nature flatly, and flatly I repeat it. She is the Greater Will's daughter, the first shooting star to fall on these lands, and mother of every Two Fingers that ever raised a guiding hand. And the staff taken from her states her condition with the same terrible flatness: she once received her parent's signs through the dark of the cosmos; she was broken, and abandoned; and broken, she kept waiting, down all the ages, for a message that never came.

The reader sees where this leads, and I must be the dull man who walks rather than leaps. *If* Metyr was broken before the age of gold began, then every syllable of guidance in this history — the grace that steered Marika's order, the Fingers that named the Empyreans, the lights that led the stranger and every Tarnished home to the very hold I write in — was noise from a dead line: a beacon-fire signaling on, faithful and meaningless, long after its far watcher stopped tending the answer. That is the conclusion Ymir preached: the Fingers misbegotten from the start, the golden line built on a mishearing. And here is exactly how far the evidence goes, and not one step further: the relics confirm that she was broken and abandoned, and that she waited. No relic says *when* she broke. The guidance may have been true for ten thousand years and static for the last century; it may have been static from the first morning. Ymir believed the worst, and Ymir — weigh the witness — ended by declaring himself the Fingers' new mother and dying in the attempt, which is not the death of a disinterested man. The returned say the stranger slew Metyr in her own microcosm, still waiting.

I meant to leave the matter there. It would not stay left. Past midnight came a recognition I record because I would rather not: the Fingers spent an age attending on a silence and reading instruction out of it — taking the no-word for the old word still in force, hearing more perfectly the quieter the cosmos grew. And what else is a historian? I sit with a record that has stopped speaking and build conclusions from what it does not say. I have taught the reader that a locked door has a shape, that a redaction has an author, that this land's silences are speech. Perhaps they are. But the Fingers believed much the same for ten thousand years, and if their communion was noise — a cracked bell rung over a dead line — then what is a man who makes history out of the gaps in the record? I have no answer that survives the asking. I set the question down unresolved — hiding it would be a redaction of my own — trimmed the lamp, and went on; it is the only method left when the method is the thing in doubt.

So I will not tell the reader that the grace of gold was static. I will tell the reader that I sit in a hold that grace built, writing by what I had all my life assumed was, at whatever remove, *light* — and that I now check the flame more often than I used to. This book's theology does not fall. It is merely no longer certain it was ever standing, and the difference between those two conditions is where the rest of my nights have gone.

### The gate, a second time

All roads in that country climb, and the expedition ended where the spiral had always pointed: Enir-Ilim, the First Tower, the spiral stair, the Gate of Divinity at the top of the world. On those stairs the last of the fellowship made its stand — Leda and her remnant, Dane beside her as always, against the stranger who was climbing to interrupt a god. They died there, all of them, needle and knight and silent monk, the accounts differing only on the roster; and old Ansbach, in the versions where he still lived, climbed the rest of the way at the stranger's side and fell before the gate, naming the graceless intruder a true lord.

Of what stood at the gate, the confirmations are as solid as anything in this book, because Ansbach had done the archival work himself and his findings survive. In the Impaler's own storehouse the expedition had recovered a scroll of the tower people's secret rite, wherein a god's return to the world must be ushered by a lord, and the lord's soul requires a vessel of flesh. Ansbach read it, understood the whole architecture at a stroke, and set it down. Radahn's soul, freed at the festival where the stranger slew his rotted body. Mohg's corpse, freed of its owner in the palace under the earth, carried into shadow with the cocoon. A vessel, and a passenger. Those who stood on the tower's last floor confirm what the scroll promised, and nothing stranger stands in this book: at the threshold of heaven, two dead men sharing one grave-light. The thing at the gate wore the Lord of Blood's remade flesh — his horns, his very bloodflame, still burning in a body whose owner was gone — and it fought with the Starscourge's arms and fury, one man's soul quartered in the other's corpse. **Promised Consort Radahn**: borrowed flesh standing where a vow was owed — the childhood wish of the golden child, honored at last in a corpse.

The wish, I say, is on record — his remembrance preserves it: the boy Miquella saw in his lord brother the strength and kindness their broken family lacked, and wished from his heart that Radahn would one day stand consort to his throne. What is on no record anywhere, in any relic or any witness's mouth, is Radahn's answer. Freyja insisted that endless war would have suited her general — a mourner's comfort, not a document. The record holds a question asked of a living man and a summons served on a dead one, and between them, silence. I state the silence and leave it standing: whether the mightiest of the demigods came willing to his brother's wedding, no one now alive can say.

And then the gate opened, and the returned who saw it will not be talked out of what they saw: Miquella came through it a god — young, radiant, whole in the way of things that have cut away everything that can ache — and embraced his consort. The stranger killed them both. The new god and the promised lord, ended on the threshold, before their age could draw its first breath; and in a garden far below, the witnesses say, a purple blossom folded and did not open again, and the saint of sleep passed with the half of her that had refused to stop walking.

The reader has stood at the top of that tower once before, at this history's beginning, watching a shaman girl climb out of the jars' shadow and come down a god. Hold the two ascents together, for they are this book's whole span bent into a circle. The same land. The same spiral. The same gate of fused and sainted dead. Mother and son, a world and an age apart, each shedding a self on the stairs — she her mortality, he everything else — each climbing to correct the world from above it. She came down, and her correction is the history you have been reading. He did not come down. Between the two ascents the difference is one figure on the stair: a nobody. A graceless, exiled, unnamed nobody, of the kind this family had spent an age discarding — the Impaler asked, dying, whether heaven could really intend lordship for such a creature, and I begin to think the question answers the book. What the nobody did next — the burning that ended the world I was born into — the witnesses of the shadow could not tell me, for they had already turned for home.

The tower keeps its gate. The gate, as ever, keeps its counsel.
