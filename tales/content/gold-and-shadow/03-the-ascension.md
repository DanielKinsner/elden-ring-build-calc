# Chapter III: The Ascension

*Wherein a daughter of the harvested people climbs a stairway as flesh and comes down as law; a beast out of the deep sky is made the order of the world; and the new order, drawing its first breath, forbids the world to end.*

---

Every age of this land is remembered by what it raised up — the spiral, the dragons' storm, the tree. The age that opens in this chapter must also be remembered by what it struck out. Between the moment a shaman girl stood at the top of Enir-Ilim's stairs and the moment her Golden Order stood complete, three things were accomplished that no power before her is recorded to have attempted together: a mortal woman became the living seat of the world's law; that law was laid open like a patient on a table and its oldest clause cut away; and death — true death, the ending of things — was taken out of the world's constitution and locked inside a sword. Historians of a gentler temper call this the founding of the Golden Order. I find it more exact, and more honest to everything that followed, to call it the first amending of reality — drafted, passed, and enforced by the amended.

## What came down the stairs

That Queen Marika the Eternal passed through the Gate of Divinity at the summit of Enir-Ilim is written flatly in no surviving relic, and I will not pretend it is. I hold it true all the same, and the reader is owed the reasoning, so here it is, stone by stone. The gate stands at the summit of the land where her life began — the same soil, under the same sky, as the meadow and the jars. The gate is the only engine for the making of gods attested anywhere in the record — not a temple where godhood was prayed for but an apparatus where it was *conferred*, whose working instructions survive in a scroll I shall cite again. The keepers of the shadow-land's memory, hostile and otherwise, hold as their tradition that she passed it — and it is the same tradition that criers carried abroad in later days, so that even those who never saw the tower learned that the queen's godhood was made in the realm of shadow. And an age afterward another Empyrean would climb those same stairs, for that same purpose, by that same rite — which settles at least what the gate was *for*. No one of these proofs will bear the conclusion alone. Set together they make an arch: each stone leans on its neighbors, and the weight of the question passes through all of them to the ground. Pull out any single stone and the arch stands; pull out the conclusion and you must explain, without it, why every stone leans the same way. I confess the arch was a pleasure to close. So I state it as the end of reasoning, not the reading of a record: at the top of those stairs, Marika of the Shaman Village became a god.

Of the passage itself the honest historian can say almost nothing, and must say so loudly, because others have said so much. The scroll of the secret rite teaches that a god does not simply walk through; a god is *ushered in*, and the ushering is the work of a lord. If the rite Marika used was the rite the scroll preserves, then someone stood beside her at the threshold — and on the identity of that someone the record does not whisper, does not hint, does not even gesture. It stops. No relic names her lord. No relic confirms she had one. The man she would later raise up as her first Elden Lord — Hoarah Loux, the chieftain out of the badlands, whom the golden age knew as Godfrey — belongs to the years after the descent; nothing in the record sets him on the tower, and the reader who assumes it commits the oldest sin in historiography, which is tidiness. I do not believe this particular silence was made, as the silence around Messmer's father was made. I believe it was total from the first hour. Whatever attended the queen at that gate did not come down the stairs to testify.

One theory I will let into the room, on a short leash, because it will not stop scratching at the door. The gate, as the one witness who stood before it swore, is built of the fused bodies of the sainted dead. Some scholars venture that those bodies were the jarred saints themselves — that the engine of her godhood was raised from the harvested flesh of her own people, and that Marika rose to heaven on the very meldings the Hornsent had made of her kin. It is a theory of terrible elegance. Nothing in the record compels it, and I decline to build on it; but I record it, because if it is true, then everything she did afterward was done while standing on her family.

## The star, the beast, and the law

What, precisely, did she become? Here, for once, the record grows generous, because the golden age taught its own cosmology and taught it flatly. The liturgy of the Elden Stars — the oldest of the incantations of gold, by its own account — preserves the doctrine: long ago, the Greater Will sent "a golden star bearing a beast into the Lands Between," and the beast in time became the Elden Ring. The sentence rewards slow reading, for it names three things that lazy chroniclers melt into one. The star was the vehicle. The beast — the Elden Beast, vassal of the Greater Will — was the passenger. And the Elden Ring is what the passenger *became*: not a ring as smiths and jewelers know rings, not a treasure in a vault, but the law of this world given living form — the charter by which things are what they are, hold the shapes they hold, and pass in the order they pass. This land is governed as a body is governed by its animating soul; the Elden Ring is that soul, and the soul is a creature, and the creature was sent. Stars had fallen on this land before: Metyr, mother of the Fingers, named first of the falling stars by the letter of her own remembrance. Whether her fall or the beast's came earlier hangs entirely on that one word *first*, and I hang nothing further on it.

Godhood, in the Greater Will's dispensation, meant this: the law would live in her. No surviving inscription applies a word like *vessel* to Marika, and I flag that plainly. But every tradition of her reign — the tellings her church spread, the awe her images were tendered, the manner in which every later claimant to godhood sought not a throne but a *carrying* — treats the Ring as being of her and within her, so that to approach the law was to approach the queen. The signs converge from too many directions to refuse: the beast that became the law made its residence in the woman who came down the stairs. She was not the owner of the Elden Ring. She was its address.

## The terms of the office

It must be understood — because everything in the ages to come turns on it — that godhood was not a crown Marika seized. It was an office she was admitted to, and offices have patrons. Above all sat the Greater Will, a power that in the entire surviving record of this land never once appears, speaks, or signs its work; it acts through emissaries, as it always has, and its emissaries in the matter of gods were the Two Fingers, the hand-shaped children of Metyr. The relics of later ages speak of Empyreans — candidates fit to succeed Queen Marika as god of a coming age, each chosen by their own Two Fingers — and in so speaking they tell us what Marika herself was: the Fingers' god, the predecessor of the whole system, an Empyrean who had already crossed. Yet a gap remains, and it is a strange one. The record shows us the machinery all around her — the Fingers, the candidates, the office — and never once the moment the machinery chose *her*. Of Marika's own selection, if selection there was, no relic speaks.

What the record does preserve is the proof of office. To its chosen, the Fingers' order gave a shadow: a beast bound to the Empyrean as companion, guardian, and — as more than one shadow's fate would prove — leash. Marika's shadow was **Maliketh, the Black Blade**, given to her when she was raised up under the Two Fingers' order; and the inscriptions of his war-gear, as rendered in the common tongue, name him more than her keeper. They name him her half-brother. So the terms of godhood, tallied honestly, ran thus: a silent patron beyond the sky, oracles shaped like hands, a law living in her body — and a brother at her heel, bound there by the same power that raised her, half kin and half instrument, in whatever proportion the power that bound him found convenient.

*On the sources, a caution.* Two kinds of relic speak for Maliketh, and they do not say the same thing. The remembrance kept for the Black Blade's soul carries the bond and the bitterness — the shadow, the vault he was made into, the betrayal he tasted — and says not one word of brotherhood. The kinship line lives instead in the inscriptions of his armor and in the ritual-text of the Black Blade, and I note with some weariness that half the scholars who cite it attribute it to the remembrance, which does not contain it; a historian who will not check his shelves is a rumor-monger with better handwriting. There is a deeper seam still: readers of the older hand in which the line was first set down argue that its word for brother may mean kin-by-oath rather than kin-by-blood. I give the kinship as the common tongue gives it — half-brother — and I leave the seam showing, as an honest tailor leaves the selvage.

## The queen with gloam eyes

The young Order did not grow up unopposed. Before it stood complete it met, somewhere in those unnumbered years, an enemy fitted exactly to its throat — and of that enemy the record preserves a title, a weapon, a priesthood, and a defeat, and almost nothing else. She is called the **Gloam-Eyed Queen**. No name survives beneath the title, no face, no origin, no grave; I state the silence and can do no more with it. The ritual-text of the black flame — the closest thing her faith left to a scripture, kept now among the trophies of the order that broke her — would have us believe she was an Empyrean, chosen as Marika had been chosen; the text itself speaks in the manner of a thing repeated, a *said-to-have-been*, and so I give the claim to the reader exactly as the relic gives it to me: as the tradition of her own defeated faith, not as fact. If the tradition spoke truly, then the wars of this section were not a rebellion against heaven but a quarrel *within* it — two candidates of the same Fingers' order, and only one throne.

Her instrument was fire, and the word misleads at once, for every other fire this land remembers is a shedder of light. Hers was a swallower. The survivals of her faith let the image be staged: a flame black to its heart, edged in soot and indigo, that threw no glow upon the faces gathered at it but drew the light in after the fuel — so that a thing burning in black flame did not brighten as it died, but went out, color first, the way a room goes when a veil is drawn across its one window. Her priesthood wore their theology on their backs. The Godskin Apostles served her — their own swaddling-relics attest whose apostles they were — and they went clad in vestments whose surviving names say plainly what they were sewn from. And the black flame was no ordinary fire in doctrine any more than in color: the relics state without hedge that it could slay gods. Consider what that meant in the world this chapter has been building. The law had come to live in a woman; the woman could die. Every article of the new golden arrangement hung on flesh — and here was a queen whose priests carried, wrapped in the skins of gods, the specific fire for burning that arrangement down.

It was the shadow who answered. That **Maliketh defeated the Gloam-Eyed Queen** the record states flatly, on the inscription of the greatsword men called Godslayer's — her own blade, or her order's, taken and kept as such trophies are kept. Of the war itself: nothing. Its battles, its years, its cost, the manner of her end — an entire war against god-slaying priests survives as a handful of inscriptions and a captured sword, and the historian is left holding the outcome like a coin found in an empty room. I permit myself one grumble per chapter; this is it.

## The surgery

Now the centerpiece, the act by which this age must be judged. **Queen Marika removed Destined Death from the Elden Ring.** The Rune of Death — the clause of the living law that governed true and final ending — was cut out of the Ring she carried, and given to Maliketh, and Maliketh sealed it within his black blade. So much the relics of the Black Blade state outright.

Let the act stand in the light a moment before it is argued over, because the words are small and the thing was not. The Ring, as the liturgy of the golden star has already taught us, is no smith's work; it is a creature — the law alive, resident in the flesh of the surgeon herself. What the relics describe, then, described plainly, is an operation on the living body of reality, and the mind can only stage it in the palette of its age: hard noon; the law laid open like the patient it had always been; scripture gone molten at the incision, running gold as resin runs from scored pine; and one rune — the oldest clause, the one that ended things — lifted out entire, still living, still hot with jurisdiction, for nothing in any record suggests that Death could be killed. It was not destroyed. It was extracted, wrapped in a brother, and buried in a sword. And where the clause had been, the gold closed over the absence and healed the way flesh heals over a splinter it cannot expel — proud, raised, harder than the skin around it. This is not my embellishment; it is the Order's own confession, preserved in the rune-lore of a later and stranger piety, which dates the Order's very existence to that operation: the Golden Order was *created* by the confining of Destined Death. Not adorned by it. Not strengthened by it. Created. The Order, by its own account, is the wound's scar tissue.

The relics record one further consequence, and from it a cautious inference. On the day the rune was sealed, the black flame lost its god-slaying heart — the fire that had threatened gods dimmed into an ember that could merely hurt them. From this sign it is tempting, and perhaps correct, to place the Gloam-Eyed Queen's fall before the sealing, her disarmed faith proof of the operation's success; but no relic orders the two events, and I will not date what the record does not. Nor will I repeat the common assertion that all this was done early in Marika's reign; no surviving text says when it was done at all. The seal is bounded in time only by what later leaked from it — a theft, and a night of knives, that wait far down this history, in its darkest hours.

Two things should be said about the surgery before the reader is allowed to admire it. The first concerns the surgeon's assistant. The remembrance kept for Maliketh's soul does not record the sealing as an honor. It records, in substance, that his Empyrean found in her sworn shadow one real use — a place to hide Death away from prying eyes — and that she betrayed him even so. What the betrayal was, the remembrance is too bitter or too discreet to spell out. But mark the shape of the arrangement: the law's most dangerous clause was not destroyed but *housed*, in the body and blade of a person, exactly as the law itself was housed in a person. The new order kept both its god and its death within a single family.

The second thing is the plain one, and it is this chapter's spine. A woman went up a stairway and came down as the law of reality — and the first recorded act of that law, once it lived in her, was to forbid endings.

## A world forbidden to end

By every sign that survives, gold rose over the Lands Between in the wake of her descent — the great tree above the capital-to-be, the grace-light in men's eyes, the whole architecture of the age to come — while the land of her birth was left its tree only as shadow, the Scadutree. The keepers of the shadow-land's tradition go further and hold that the Erdtree itself was *born* there, in the realm the tower had ruled; I give that as their tradition, which is how it reaches me. However the tree came, the old world went out around it like lamps at dawn. The dragons' lordship of Placidusax was already antiquity, a prior order under a fled god, and the golden centuries would finish it into legend. The Crucible — the braid and coil of all living things, the holiness of the age before — was recast in the new dispensation as devilry, its horns and tails made marks of the accursed, so that the very blessing the Hornsent had venerated became, under gold, a deformity to be feared. The economy of it deserves a plain sentence: the new faith did not merely defeat the old one; it repossessed the old one's heaven and reissued it as hell.

And death itself was reorganized. The old rites of ending — the ghostflame kilns where the Deathbirds burned the dead beneath the Twinbird, said to be envoy of some outer power the record never names — were displaced, their fires orphaned, their custom driven to the margins and the night. In their place came Erdtree Burial: the dead of the golden order interred among roots, in catacombs threaded through with the tree's own reaching, watched over by stone dogs, and required — the word is doctrinal, not poetic — to remain within the Erdtree's cycle. What that meant for the dead themselves the relics tell only obliquely, and I assemble rather than assert: a world whose law had no clause for endings was a world where the dead did not entirely go. Spirits linger in this land in numbers no other country's chronicles report; the departed return to the tree, and the tree returns them; burial became not a farewell but a deposit. Whether the dead of the golden age found this a mercy is a question no relic answers, because no relic thought to ask them.

Here, then, is the age as its founding left it — and one item stands yet unpaid in its tally: the meadow, the jars, the braid sent home in a woman's stead — what a daughter owes a civilization that made saints of her family without asking. A god whose law now forbade endings still intended, by every sign this history can gather, to deliver one ending in person. The first full exercise of her new estate was not a founding but an erasure, and it wore her son's face. The payment climbed the stairs she had come down, robed in fire.
