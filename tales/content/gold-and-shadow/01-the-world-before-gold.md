# Chapter I: The World Before Gold

*Wherein the world is found to be older than its god; a dragon keeps a throne warm for a deity that ran; two stars fall, and one of them breaks; whole cities are put to bed without a sky; and death — not yet stolen — is kept by birds.*

---

Every chronicle owes its reader a procession: this happened, and then that. I must open this one by defaulting on the debt. The deep past of the Lands Between survives the way a plowed-under field survives — everything turned up from it is genuine, but the plow has ruined the rows. The relics of the eldest age tell us *what* was, with surprising confidence; in what order it was, they decline to say, and I will not invent a sequence merely because a page reads more handsomely with one. What follows is therefore not a march but a survey: the powers that held this world before gold meant anything, grouped by kind, each given its proper weight. Where one thing demonstrably preceded another, I say so. Everywhere else, the reader should imagine these ancient things side by side, like strangers in the same dark room — which may, for all any relic tells us, be exactly what they were.

One ordering, and only one, is safe: all of it came before the Erdtree. That is the single fixed wall of this chapter, and everything in it leans there.

## The age of blending

Begin with the shape life itself first took, for even that was different before gold.

By every sign that survives, the eldest epoch of living things was not an order but a mingling — an age the old faith called the **Crucible**, in which all life was blended together, borders between kinds not yet drawn. I reason here from relics rather than recite from one: the incantations that persist from that devotion grant a man a tail, or horns, or a throat of fire, as if the body remembered owning them; the armor of the knights who later bore those aspects calls the Crucible the primal form of the very tree that displaced it; and the traits themselves — horn and tail and beast-feature — were revered in ancient days as the visible residue of life's beginning. Set those relics together and they hold one another up like the courses of a dry-stone wall: no single stone carries the claim, and the wall stands anyway.

Two things about the Crucible must be said now, because the whole book stands on them. First: the age of gold did not replace nothing. The blended order was the world's *native* condition, and the reader should hold that thought through every later age in which a horned child is called accursed — the Omens and the Misbegotten of the golden centuries are this age's orphans, punished for resembling their ancestors. Second: where the Crucible sits against the other powers of this chapter — the dragons above all — no relic will say. The two may have been neighbors; the two may have been one long afternoon of the world. The plow has been through that row.

## The lords of the storm-city

Of all the powers of the old world, one held a title this history is not done with — and the relic that tells us so is the most carefully worded in Gideon's whole collection.

There is a city that flies. **Farum Azula**, seat of the ancient dragons, hangs to this day inside a perpetual maelstrom, and it is not ruined the way other cities consent to be ruined: it is a collapse that time declined to finish. Its masonry shears loose and never lands. Bridges end in open wind; stairways pour their steps out into the storm, and the steps do not fall but keep station, circling the city like slow moons of cut stone. A dome the size of a hill drifts past the temple it once roofed. Every span and doorway of the place is cut to a dragon's measure, parapets sized for wings rather than shoulders, and the lightning there does not strike and finish as our lightning does — it stands, rooted between cloud and pavement, columns of it, holding up nothing. At the still center waits **Dragonlord Placidusax**: scale like weathered brass, worn to a shine at the edges of the plates, wings ragged where wings were torn from him, the stumps of severed necks crowded beside the two heads that remain — a king-shaped remnant coiled on his plinth outside the ordinary passage of time, waiting. Testimony from those who walked that broken sky in the recent wars reached this Hold, and I have no cause to doubt the walking. And here the record makes us work. The remembrance kept of his soul does not state his rank; it reports it. He "is said to have been" Elden Lord — so the remembrance itself, hedging, in an age before the Erdtree — and a chronicler who flattens that hedge into fact has begun lying early. I confess I take a workman's comfort in that hedge rather than any frustration: even a dragon — even a two-headed lord of dragons throned outside time — can be weighed by a careful man, provided the man reads the scale honestly. What I may honestly add is that the hedge is the *only* timid thing in the evidence. The office is named, and named as ours: Elden Lord, the same title Godfrey would carry, worn ages before Marika drew breath. And the flying city's own stonework bears the ring in relief — the sign of the very order the later world thought it had invented. By every sign that survives, the lordship was real: some whole arrangement of the world, with a god at its head and a dragon as that god's lord, stood complete before gold. I state it as reasoning, not as record, and I invite the reader to weigh the evidence relic by relic.

Of the god itself the record is briefer and stranger, and here — the irony of the sources — we leave hearsay for plain statement: **the god fled**. It abandoned its own Elden Lord, for reasons no relic anywhere condescends to give, and Placidusax took his seat in the storm beyond time and began to wait for its return. He waits still. Every later age of this history — Marika's rise, the golden centuries, the Shattering, the burning I watched from this library's window — happened while a two-headed king sat outside time, holding a throne for a runaway. Who or what that god was, the record is silent; I have found nothing, and I have looked with the appetite of a man who knows the question matters. Some scholars venture that the fled god was the very power whose emissaries would fall upon this land as stars, arrived early and departed disappointed; others, that the god *is* the storm, and never left at all. The theories are good supper conversation. Nothing in the record compels either.

The dragon age had its civil wound as well. A dragon called **Bayle the Dread** rose against the Dragonlord, and the duel maimed both: Placidusax lost heads and wings to it, and the rebel's ruined body, found in later days roosting atop the Jagged Peak in the Land of Shadow, still carries two of the Dragonlord's severed heads, jaws locked where they bit and died. For once the relics are the combatants themselves. Every witness frames Bayle's roost as an exile; for most of the rest we have one witness, who deserves naming. **Florissax**, priestess of Dragon Communion at the Grand Altar, is an ancient dragon wearing a woman's shape, and by her own telling a worshipper of Placidusax. From her comes most of what this history knows of the duel, of Bayle's treason against the brood, of the claim that his descendants are the lesser fire-breathing drakes of the present age, and of the strange long vengeance that followed — dragons in priestess form teaching mortals the heresy of eating dragon hearts, which reads, though no one quite says it, like the ancient kind raising human hunters against the traitor's offspring. Mortals paid a toll for the lesson: heroes who indulged too deeply in communion were cursed to crawl the earth as magma wyrms, swords still in their hands. And one hunter must stand here for all of them: **Igon**, broken on the Jagged Peak road, who gave his body to the hunt for Bayle and his testimony, magnificently, to the record.

*On the sources, a caution.* Nearly everything in the last paragraph flows through two witnesses, and neither is calm. Florissax is the voice of the loyal church describing its heresiarch. Igon is a man Bayle ruined, and his account is a wound talking. I use them because they are what exists, and because on the central facts — the duel, the maiming, the exile — the worshipper and the victim agree; but the reader should know the color of the glass we are looking through, and correct for the tint. And one thing neither voice settles: whether Bayle struck before the god fled or after it. Nothing orders the two events.

## The falling stars

Into this old world — before it, during it, the relics will not say — things began to arrive from outside. They came as stars, and they did not come empty.

The first is stated with rare flatness: **Metyr**, daughter of the power called the **Greater Will**, was the first shooting star ever to strike the Lands Between. From her body came the **Two Fingers**, every one of them, and the crawling fingercreepers besides — she is mother to the entire family of hand-shaped oracles that would one day stand behind thrones, name candidates for godhood, and beckon the graceless home. When, under gold, a pair of severed fingers speaks for heaven, the reader should remember where fingers come from: a star, and a mother.

A second arrival is likewise plainly attested — a golden star, sent by the same Greater Will, bearing a beast that would become the living law of the world — but its story is the hinge on which the age of gold turns, and I will not spend it here. What this chapter owes the reader is the ordering, and honesty about how thin it is. That Metyr came first rests on a single word: her remembrance's *first*. Read literally, first means first, and I incline to read it so; but no text sequences the two stars against each other, and calling the golden star a *shooting* star at all is my category, not the relics'. The inclination is reasoned. It is not knowledge.

One more thing about the first star, and I set it down gently, because its full weight belongs to the far end of this history, among the last things I shall have to tell. The mother of the oracles did not stay whole. The relics of a much later age found her broken — damaged in some fashion no text explains, and gone an immense span without any new word from the parent that sent her, waiting all the while for a message that was not coming. When that breaking happened, whether the channel was dead for ages or only lately, the record refuses to say, and I refuse to guess, for more history hangs on that one unanswerable question than on any other silence in this book. Here I note only the fact, and the small cold draft it lets into every room of the house: the first thing heaven sent us was an envoy, and the envoy broke.

Not everything that fell brought order. **Astel**, called Naturalborn of the Void — a malformed star, born in the lightless gulf — came down upon one of the great cities of the deep (of which more directly) and destroyed it, taking away its very sky. Which city fell, no relic names. And humbler stones brought humbler wonders: the greatswords of the **Onyx and Alabaster Lords** carry their masters' own account, that the stone-skinned ancients rose to life where a meteor struck, long ago — the swords may be believed or doubted, but it is their story, and with the falling stars came the arts of gravity that sorcerers mine to this day.

## The banished, the buried, and the deep faiths

Beneath the earth of the Lands Between lie two cities under false night skies — **Nokron** and **Nokstella**, the Eternal Cities — and their emptiness is a verdict. Their people, the **Nox**, committed high treason: they invoked the wrath of the Greater Will itself, and for it they were driven under the ground, sentenced to live beneath a manufactured night and await an age of stars and a sovereign of their own, the promised **Lord of Night**. The treason is not rumor; its proof survives as an object. Hidden in Nokron the recent age found the **Fingerslayer Blade** — a weapon expressly made to wound the Greater Will's own vassals, the Fingers, and named in its keeping as the evidence of the Eternal City's crime. What the treason *consisted of* — the forging, the use, some larger project the blade merely survived — the record does not say. A people were buried alive with the murder weapon, and no indictment was filed. Some scholars venture that Astel's fall upon an Eternal City was the Greater Will's own sentence executed, punishment delivered as a star; the pairing is neat, and neatness is all it has — no text draws the arrow, and I leave the two facts unwired. Nor can I honestly place the burial against the Erdtree's rise: whether the Nox thrived and fell before gold, or their long night ran beneath it, the relics will not settle even that. In their dark the silver tears — quicksilver creatures that wear the shapes of other living things — teem to this day, and whatever the Nox intended by cultivating them, intention is more than the record grants. Older or stranger still, the nameless ruins called Uhl stand in those same depths, a civilization reduced entirely to its name.

The deep places kept gentler tenants too. Under Siofra's false stars a horned people tends the **Hallowhorn Grounds**, monuments of antler and bone, and worships the great **ancestor spirits** — deer-like, patient, and expressly beyond the coming tree's purview, for the remembrance of their regal spirit says plainly that life sprouting from death is their way. An order of gold would one day be built on the removal of death; here, under the ground, an older faith had already made death the root of life and asked no one's permission.

And the world above had its own old furnaces. In the far north lived the **Fire Giants**, and they did not pray to gold either: they borrowed the power of the **Fell God**, a one-eyed god of fire, and kept a flame whose one recorded property should be read twice — it could burn the Erdtree. A tree that did not yet exist already had its executioner waiting in the mountains. The giants' war, their annihilation, and the single cursed survivor belong to the conquests that raised the golden order; the theft of the fell flame by the one called Adan, Thief of Fire, is a stray spark of the same fire in a later age. I record here only the standing fact of them, and one private observation: I write these lines under a sky the color of that flame's work, and I confess the old one-eyed god no longer feels ancient to me at all.

## Death, wind, and the last stray legend

Before the tree, death had servants of its own. At night, at ruined graveyards, the **Deathbirds** kept the kilns — undertaker birds wreathed in **ghostflame**, the cold blue fire of the old rite, said (the relic of their barbed rods puts it so) to rake the ashes of the dead from the fires they tended. Burning, not burial, was the old way, and the dead went out of the world by flame and into the keeping of whatever waits — the old faith knew a spirit-tree, the **Helphen**, that guides the dead below, and by every sign the steeple belongs to this elder order and not the golden one. Above the Deathbirds the record sets the **Twinbird** — *sets*, but hedged: the shield-makers' account would have it that the Twinbird is mother to the Deathbirds and envoy of an outer god, a god of death whose name no relic anywhere pronounces. An unnamed god, served by a twinned bird, tended by gravekeeper birds with rakes: that is the entire surviving constitution of death's first kingdom. The reader will want to know what became of it. The coming of gold answers — the new order did not merely defeat the old death; it *unplugged* death itself — and the birds haunt every age after, still lighting their fires at night in the ruins, a faith outliving its own jurisdiction.

The wind, too, had a sovereign once. Limgrave's great crag answered to a storm in the days before the fortress on it answered to anyone, and the nearest thing to a portrait of that old power is a hawk: the **Stormhawk King**, revered — his own remembrance-ash says it — by all other hawks as sovereign, in the days when the winds of that place raged as they never have since, and too proud to answer any man's summons. When the first lord of the golden age came, he faced a **Storm Lord** there, alone — but that is Godfrey's feat, and it keeps to its own age. Who the Storm Lord was, the record never says — a king of hawks and a lord of dragons have both been proposed, and the wars of gold will weigh the claimants.

Last, one legend that fits nowhere and must live here, because a history that has no shelf for strays loses them: it is told that a fairy once gave a flowing sword to a blind swordsman, and that with it he sealed away an ancient god that was rot itself. No date, no place, no names beyond these; the legend is attested, the event beyond all dating — necessarily older than every rot this history will meet. When rot returns to these pages, the reader may take this stray out again and wonder, as I do, who did the sealing, and how long seals last.

---

That, then, is the inventory of the world before gold: a native order of blended life; a flying city and a lordship attested with a hedge; a throne held for a god that ran; a rebel dragon rotting on a peak with his king's teeth still in him; two stars, one of them a mother, one of them a law, the mother broken on some unrecorded day; cities buried alive with the evidence; a faith that grew life out of death, and birds that kept death's fires; a flame in the mountains that could kill a tree not yet planted; and a wind that has never blown so hard again. No thrones of gold anywhere in it — and an office, *Elden Lord*, already invented, already vacant, already waiting for the family this history is actually about. This history goes next to the holy land where that family's mother was born, and to the people who filled their jars with her kin.
