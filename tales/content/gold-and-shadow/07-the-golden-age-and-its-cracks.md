# Chapter VII: The Golden Age and Its Cracks

*Wherein the Order reaches its noon; the dead are given to the roots of a tree; three children are named to succeed a living god; and every flaw that will one day break the world is set, politely and in plain sight, into the gold.*

---

Every history of a collapse must pass through a chapter like this one, and the historian dreads it, because he knows something the age did not. The Golden Order at its height was, by every measure its own relics propose, a success: fed, lawful, luminous, victorious over every rival it had deigned to notice. Its god had reigned so long that permanence had stopped being a claim and become a habit. And it was already broken. Not failing — broken, in the way a bell can be cast with a flaw deep in the bronze and still ring beautifully for years, its one dishonest note pitched too low for any ear that has never heard a bell crack. This chapter is an inventory of the flaws. The age itself never made one, which is the most golden thing about it.

## The noon of the Order

Begin with what the age believed, for its beliefs were its architecture. By the era of the second Elden Lord, faith in the Erdtree had hardened from worship into scholarship: the discipline the relics call **Golden Order Fundamentalism**, whose scholars held that the Order could be *studied* — derived, proved, perfected — like the geometry it so resembled. Its great text was the Principia; its twin pillars were the law of regression, which teaches that all things yearn to converge upon the One Great, and the law of causality, the chain of consequence linking every deed to every other. Hold both laws in mind, because the age's tragedy can be stated entirely in its own theology: a family that would not converge, and a chain of consequence that no one in it ever once escaped.

The queen herself blessed the enterprise. Among her recorded echoes — those fragments of her actual voice that survive where almost nothing else of her mind does — is one urging the faithful to search the Order's depths, past the old days of blind belief; and another, preserved at her own bedchamber, that addresses Radagon, her second lord, as the Order's "loyal hound." The reader who has seen the evidence of the queen's two bodies knows why that endearment freezes the blood: the queen who demanded the Order be understood was speaking, in her own chambers, to her second body — speaking to it as to a servant, and observing, in the same breath, that it had not yet become her. Mark the shape of the arrangement: at the exact center of a theology of convergence sat a being who had contrived to be Two — who, on the evidence of that echo, did not much like herself — and precisely one person in the world was positioned to know it.

Under that strange double crown, the realm ordered itself as no realm here had before. Leyndell ringed the Erdtree in walls and gold; and the Erdtree, not content with the living, took charge of the dead. This was the doctrine of **Erdtree burial**: the dead were interred in catacombs threaded through with the great tree's roots, watched over by stone watchdogs, and required — the word is the doctrine's, not mine — to remain within the Erdtree's cycle. Understand what had been constructed. Death itself had been made an office of the crown. The reader will remember the deeper cut beneath the doctrine — the founding surgery, told in its place: Destined Death removed from the Elden Ring by the queen's hand and sealed away in the black blade of her own shadow, Maliketh. The catacombs were the public face of that operation — a realm in which dying had been reduced to a change of estate. The age called this plenty, and by its lights it was: no age before it had ever promised its subjects that even the grave was orderly. The historian, who writes in the ash of the promise, will only observe that a law against death is not the same thing as an answer to it, and that debts deferred are not debts paid.

## The golden prince

Of all the heirs the age produced, it loved one without complication. **Godwyn**, called the Golden, was Marika's son — the relics say so everywhere, without being asked — and by every sign the golden lineage's own definition supplies, Godfrey's: the lineage is named as the first lord and his offspring, and Godwyn is its scion in every text that styles him. He is the one member of the family whose epithet carries no shadow, no irony, no cage. The capital's very faith bent around him. After the ancient dragon Gransax hurled himself upon Leyndell — the only breach of those walls ever recorded, and told already in its place — the war with the ancient dragons ended not in extermination but in peace, and from that peace grew a thing the Order's fundamentalists can never have entirely approved: a knightly cult of the dragons *inside* the capital, red lightning taught as a holy incantation, the dragon Lansseax taking human form as its priestess. At the center of the reconciliation stood the prince: **Fortissax**, Lansseax's brother, was Godwyn's sworn companion, dragon and demigod side by side in arms.

The singers, of course, have improved the tale — in their version the two met first as duelists, and the prince befriended his fallen foe. It is a good story; nothing in the record supports it; the remembrance that names their companionship names no duel, and I leave the fight to the taverns. What the relics preserve is stranger than the song: an heir so secure in the age's affection that he could bind the Order's old enemies to it by friendship alone. If the reader wishes to know what the Golden Order looked like when it worked, it looked like Godwyn. Hold the image. Nothing in this history stays gold at that brightness for long.

## The city's cellar

This history has already named it: the basement the golden age dug beneath its own capital. Now go down: beneath the capital that raised monuments to its dragon-peace, the age kept its other face in the dark.

The Order that had made horns the mark of devilry — the reader has walked through the crusade that doctrine descends from — did not exempt its own. Children born **Omen**, horned and tailed as the oldest blessing of the Crucible once ran, were not suffered to grow: among the common people the horns were cut, and the cutting was usually fatal. But an Omen born royal could not be quietly buried, and so the dynasty improvised. Marika's twin sons **Morgott and Mohg** — twins the relics state outright; sons of Godfrey by the same chained signs that name Godwyn's father — were born Omens both, and the realm that sang above them was never told. They were confined beneath the capital, in the shunning-grounds under the sewers, under shackles forged for them purposely. The golden lineage had produced, in one generation, the realm's most beloved heir and its two most hidden, and the difference between their fates was decided at birth by the shape of the skull.

What the two prisoners did with the dark is the fork in the whole family's road. **Morgott** looked up through the sewer-grates at the Order that had chained him — and loved it. There is no other honest verb. The remembrance carved for his soul records a life spent as the Erdtree's self-appointed defender, asking nothing, receiving nothing, holding the capital for a faith whose first doctrine was that he ought not to exist. The age never knew it owned such a servant. **Mohg** looked into the dark instead — and the dark looked back. Somewhere far beneath the shunning-grounds he came before a being the Order's maps have no province for: the **Formless Mother**, a presence outside the Erdtree's gold entirely, and at her touch the accursed blood in his veins took fire. The relics state the encounter and the bloodflame flatly; what was said, and what was promised, they do not record. A prince the Golden Order refused to mother had found, below its foundations, something that would not refuse. The full price of that adoption came due long after. Mark here only the doctrinal event, this chapter's quietest and worst: an *outer god* — a power of the same foreign order as the one behind the Erdtree itself — had recruited inside the royal family, directly beneath the throne, and the age noticed nothing.

## The twins of one god

And then there are the children of the marriage that should not have been possible.

Marika and Radagon — queen and consort in a union whose true nature perhaps four souls in the world understood — had children together. Twins: **Miquella and Malenia**. The remembrance kept for Malenia's soul names them children of a single god, and every witness who speaks of their birth speaks in the same breath of affliction. Miquella was born into an eternal childhood — a body that would not age, however long it lived. Malenia was born with the scarlet rot already inside her, an occupying power in her own flesh, eating her from the first hour outward. These are stated things, and I state them.

*On the sources, a caution.* What no relic states is the sentence every reader has already composed: that the single-parent birth *caused* the curses — that the twins were malformed by their own begetting, divinity folded back on itself like bad steel. The remembrance sets the two facts side by side, single god and paired afflictions, and stops; the witnesses do the same; and the leap between them, however short it looks, is a leap. Some scholars make it without breaking stride. I decline to, for one reason: this family's record has taught me that its most obvious explanations are the ones most often planted. The pairing itself is real testimony — someone thought the two facts belonged in one line. Of the causation, the relics are silent.

What is certain is what the births *qualified* the twins for. The collector in whose library I write — Gideon Ofnir, who will enter this history properly near its end — left notes asserting that those born of a single god are eligible for a rarer title than demigod, and the events bear him out: both twins were named **Empyrean**. The word must be exact, for it is the hinge on which the rest of this family's ruin turns. An Empyrean is a candidate to *succeed Marika* — to become the god of the next age, chosen for the office by the Two Fingers, those mute enormities through whom the Greater Will is said to conduct its business here. From the moment of naming, an Empyrean's life is an estate in probate — a successor's body kept against the day the current god is done with the world, watched by powers that do not explain themselves. The age of plenty named three such children. It did not ask any of them.

Miquella's answer to his inheritance is the most documented, and the most quietly damning of the Order that reared him. Raised inside his father's fundamentalism, he abandoned it — the relic that records the apostasy gives the reason without decoration: the Order's whole luminous geometry contained no cure for his sister. What he did next, no one else in this history thought to do: he went looking for a *better gold*. The relics call it **unalloyed gold**, and the name is the whole metallurgy: gold with nothing else in it — none of the Erdtree's honeyed warmth, no thread of the Greater Will worked into the casting. Hold a sliver of it up in the mind's light: needle-bright, a splinter of hard noon so pure it reads cold; a metal that offers the gods no seam to grip, proof against the meddling of outer powers as such. From it he wrought needles to hold the rot in his twin at bay. Mark the ambition beneath the mercy: the boy-Empyrean had concluded that the gods themselves, his own patron included, were a contamination one might engineer *against*. And he planted a tree: a sapling, watered — the inscription is explicit — with his own blood, meant to grow into a new Erdtree for everyone the old one had discarded, the Albinaurics and the Misbegotten and the cast-off. The Haligtree's full story runs far past this noon; it is enough to record that in the very noon of the Order, the gentlest of Marika's children was quietly building the Order's replacement, and calling it a refuge.

Two more entries from the twins' childhood belong here rather than later. First: a remembrance kept at the very end of this history's road records that Miquella, in those years, looked at his half-brother **Radahn** — Radagon's son of the Carian marriage, then growing into the realm's red-maned prodigy of war — and saw in his strength and kindness a lord; and wished, as a child wishes, that Radahn might one day be his king consort. A child's admiration, pressed like a leaf in a remembrance. What was eventually done with that wish is perhaps the most terrible single thing in this book, and it will keep. Second: Gideon's notes return again and again to one property of Miquella — an innate power to compel affection, attested in relics derived from him, branches that bewitch men's hearts whole. The All-Knowing called him, for that reason, the most fearsome of the Empyreans, and I pass the judgment on under its author's name, adding only this: every page of testimony about Miquella that survives was written by someone who had met him. The reader now knows the discount to apply, and will find no page where applying it is comfortable.

Malenia's answer to her inheritance was narrower, and nothing about it was chosen. But before the rot is judged, let it be seen, for no other power in this history so disarms the eye. Scarlet rot does not present as decay. It presents as flowering. In flesh it opens the way coral opens under warm water — branching, vermilion at the lip and paling toward gold at the root, bloom laid wet upon bloom like bruised petals, the whole dusted with a fine pollen that carries the fever off it the way heat comes off a brazier. Where it takes hold, a body becomes a garden working at some enormous task: vivid, feverish, almost tender. It is the one affliction in this history whose witnesses reach for the language of shrines before the language of plague — and the chief of those witnesses is the sage Gowry, a man to be handled with tongs, for he reveres the very power he describes: he names the rot the influence of an outer god in its own right, as foreign to the Erdtree as the Formless Mother below Leyndell, and he calls its endless cycle of bloom and decay a divine order. The reader who has just watched it bloom on the page may understand, for one uncomfortable heartbeat, how a man gets there. Then let judgment resume, for the garden's soil was a living woman, and every petal was paid for out of her flesh. Gowry worships; but on the rot's nature the needles corroborate him, for Miquella forged them against exactly such meddling.

What held the garden in check was nothing the Order supplied. It was three things a hand could touch: the prostheses of worked metal that answered, one by one, for what the rot took of her limbs; her brother's needle of unalloyed gold, worn in the flesh itself, the one metal in the world the thing inside her could not argue with; and the knights who closed themselves around her. An engraved talisman from these years tells of a girl born into the accursed rot who met a mentor with a flowing blade and gained wings of unmatched strength; the girl is unnamed, but I read her as every scholar reads her. She became her brother's sword — the Blade of Miquella, the title she carried to the end — and about her gathered the **Cleanrot Knights**, who deserve their sentence in the age's ledger: knights who sealed themselves in lily-marked armor to serve a woman whose mere proximity was slowly killing them, and counted it an honor. One of them, **Finlay**, earned her place in this history by a deed of carrying done in darker years.

And one legend, because the age should have read it and did not: the stray legend shelved at this history's very beginning may now be taken out again. An old charm preserves the tale of a blind swordsman who, in some era before all our eras, received a flowing sword from a fairy and sealed away an ancient god that was rot itself. A legend only; I gave it as one then, and give it as one now. But if it remembers anything true, the power inside Malenia was not new to the world — it was *returned*, an old prisoner with an old grudge, patient the way only sealed things are patient. The golden age looked at the rot in the queen's daughter and saw an illness. It may have been a siege.

## The third Empyrean, and the leash

The third name the Two Fingers called was not born of the impossible marriage at all. **Ranni**, daughter of Radagon and Rennala of Caria — Marika's stepdaughter, with not one drop of the queen's blood — was named Empyrean beside the twins; the fact is certain, for we have it from Ranni's own account, given long after with nothing left to gain by lying. Or so the ledger read before the queen's second body came to light. If Radagon was Marika, the *stepdaughter* carried the queen's own blood by another door, and the Fingers' inexplicable taste in candidates may be no mystery at all. Why the choice fell on her, when the stated qualification was birth from a single god and the face of the record gave her two parents in the ordinary way, no relic explains — but the silence reads differently now than it can have read to the age: the machinery of heaven may simply have been consulting a page of the family record that no one beneath it had been shown. Either way the naming should have troubled the age — a candidate outside the stated rule, chosen without one word of explanation. There is no sign the age permitted itself the trouble.

With the title came the gift, and with the gift a cold the reader will feel one sentence before I explain it. At her selection Ranni was given a companion: **Blaidd**, the half-wolf, loyal beyond the ordinary meaning of the word — and built, by the Fingers who supplied him, as a fail-safe. The Carian war counselor Iji, who loved them both and is our witness, states it without flinching: should the Empyrean stray from the Greater Will's design, the shadow is compelled to turn upon her. Now set the arrangement beside its precedent. Marika, when she was the Fingers' candidate, was given the beast Maliketh — shadow, guardian, and the eventual keeper of sealed Death itself. The institution had not changed a gear in a divine generation: choose the vessel, then chain the vessel to a beloved executioner. It is the tenderest instrument of control this history has to show, and the exact measure of what the powers above the Order believed a god-candidate to be — not an heir, but a risk. Of shadows given to Miquella and Malenia, the record says nothing at all; whether the twins went unleashed, or their leashes merely left no relic, is a genuine dark patch, and I mark it as one.

Ranni looked at her appointed destiny — her body a vessel on a shelf, her will a formality, her dearest companion a knife pointed at her by design — and by her own later account was unwilling. Her fate, meanwhile, had been locked from an unexpected quarter: her full brother Radahn, who had mastered gravity in Sellia as a boy so as never to abandon a scrawny and beloved steed, grew mighty enough in these years to arrest the very stars in their courses — and Carian fates are written in the stars. The amber heavens over the golden age were not a wonder; they were a stopped clock, and the witch it had stopped was taking her time deciding what she was.

## The cracks, counted

Let the ledger of the noon be totaled, then, as the age itself never totaled it.

*Item:* heirs. The realm now held Godwyn the beloved; Morgott and Mohg in the dark below it; Messmer, unspoken, in a land struck from the maps; Radahn, Rykard, and Ranni of the Carian bed; Miquella and Malenia of the impossible one — and above them all a god who did not age, did not abdicate, and had removed the one power that might have retired her. Nine claims, and no throne ever to be vacant, in a theology whose highest law was convergence upon the One.

*Item:* death. Forbidden — removed from the Ring, sealed in a shadow's blade, its public office parceled out to catacombs and doctrine. The age read this as its crowning mercy. The reader may instead ask the fundamentalists' own question — where a chain of causality leads when its final link is locked away — and note that there now existed, by the age's own arrangement, exactly one blade with the sealed power to kill a god, guarded by exactly one grieving beast.

*Item:* the gods outside. A rot-power resident in the flesh of one Empyrean; a Formless Mother whispering to a chained prince under the capital's floor; and gold itself now proven — by the queen's own gentlest son — to be alloyed, meddled-with, *answerable*. The Order taught that it was the world. The evidence, patiently accumulating in its own royal nursery, was that it was a jurisdiction.

*Item:* the crown. One being, two bodies; a queen who spoke to her own consort-self as to a dog she was fond of; the flaw in the bell's bronze, wearing two faces at every rite and quarreling, on the evidence of the one echo we have, in the only chamber where no one could hear.

No jest survives that last entry; the inventory is doing its work on the clerk as well.

That is the inventory, and I will not pretend the age could easily have read it; such ledgers are compiled by men sitting in the ash of the thing counted. The gold rang true to every ear that mattered. The granaries stood full; the catacombs kept their doctrine; the prince was loved, the twins were tended, the witch was patient, the stars were stopped. It is the stillest moment this history contains, and the historian, who knows what a stopped clock is for, begs leave to hold it one sentence longer than it deserves.

The next sound in the record is knives.
