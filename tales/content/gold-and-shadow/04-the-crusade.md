# Chapter IV: The Crusade

*Wherein the age of gold sends its first army into the old holy land, wins its first war with fire and the stake, and then spends the rest of eternity insisting that the war, the army, and the general never were.*

---

Every dynasty in every history I have read keeps its first war like a bride keeps her wedding ring: polished, displayed, worn to every feast. The Golden Order kept giants' skulls in its capital and carved its founding conquests onto its Elden Lord's very crown. And yet the golden age's first hidden war — the first it fought beyond its own borders — appears on no monument, in no liturgy, in no children's rhyme of the Erdtree's long noon. It was fought in the land of the tower, against the people of the jars, by a red-haired prince at the head of knights who loved him; and when it was over, the war was not commemorated. It was *amputated*. This chapter is the history of that war. The reader who has kept the jars in mind — the clay, the melded saints, the harvested meadow — was promised they would not need three guesses as to what Marika did about her debt. Here is the answer, and it is worse than guessing.

The command itself survives, and survives flatly, which is remarkable when you consider how much labor went into unmaking everything around it. **Queen Marika ordered the purge of the Hornsent.** The horned folk's own survivors say so — the aged grandam, the old voice we have already heard explaining her civilization's corpse, names the goddess and not the general as the author of her people's ruin. And the general's own armor says so from the other side, in the plainest engraving this history possesses: he made himself a symbol of fear and dread, the helm's inscription runs, "undertaking the cleansing crusade she desired." *She desired.* Two words, cut into the steel his own knights polished. Whatever else was burned, redacted, veiled, and buried in the years that follow, no one thought — or no one dared — to scrape those two words off.

The general was her son. **Messmer**, whom this history last saw in the dark beside his mother's grief — the red-haired boy with the serpent coiled behind his eye, the boy whose mother plucked that eye out with her own divine hand and pressed a seal of grace into the socket, the boy whose father's name was not lost but removed — that boy grew, and was given an army, and earned the only epithet the record permits him: **the Impaler**. I ask the reader to weigh the shape of this. Marika had, by the time of the crusade, other instruments. She had a consort who wrestled giants for sport, and armies gilded in her own grace. She sent the son with the sealed serpent — the child of the Land of Shadow, born of whatever she had been before the gate — back into the Land of Shadow, to burn it. If there is a colder sentence in this history I have not yet had to write it.

## The armies of the unsung war

Of the war's date, the honest thing must be said first: the relics refuse to give one. But they give something nearly as good, which is a *floor*, and the floor is load-bearing for everything in this chapter, so I will show my reasoning in full.

Messmer's inner order were the **Fire Knights**, and their armor remembers where they came from: the noble houses of Erdtree high society, whose sons and daughters were cast out of their ancestral homes for the crime of pledging themselves to the serpent-prince. Stop on that sentence. For there to be Erdtree nobility to recruit, there must be an Erdtree; for noble houses old enough to disown their children, the golden order must already have manners, precedence, and things to be ashamed of. The crusade was therefore no prelude to Marika's godhood, no last act of her mortal grudge — it was a war of the *golden age*, raised from the golden age's own drawing rooms, marched out of a world of grace into a world of spirals. The reasoning stands on the knights' own armor, and I invite any reader to push on it. I note, for completeness, that one tradition of scholarship would put the crusade earlier than the Order and all its history; it rests, as far as I can determine, on the arrangement of entries in a single compiled chronicle, and the order of lines on a page is not the order of ages in the world. I set it aside.

Beside the Fire Knights marched the **Black Knights**, and their harness tells a quieter, sadder story: etched into the black steel is a small golden tree — not the Erdtree, but the **Scadutree**, the shadowed tree of the land they were sent to burn — granted, their armorers recorded, as a consolation. Consolation for what? Their own equipment answers: for a war without grace or honor. I know of no other army in this history whose smiths engraved an apology into the breastplate before the fighting began.

And behind the knights came a third body of men the record calls a penal battalion — the condemned of the golden order, spent in the shadowed land, and buried afterward where the Scadutree could not see them. Even the war's graves were chosen for concealment. The reader should hold onto that detail; it is the first appearance of a policy.

## The burning of Belurat

Of the war itself the land is the chief witness, and the land testifies in char. Belurat burned — and the reader must let that sentence open before weighing it, because the first holy city of the world was built as a spiral, its streets winding upward around the tower's root in the very coil of its creed, and fire climbs. What the Hornsent had raised as a prayer, the flame read as a chimney. It went up the winding streets the way their theology said the spirit went up — turning, ascending, gathering — soot rolling ahead of it in indigo billows, smoke drawing veil after veil across the holy precincts until street after street was shut away from the sky, the dyed cloths and painted lintels of the tower folk giving up their colors to the one color that remains when every other has been extinguished. The stone kept its shape; stone does. Everything the stone had held became smoke, and the smoke became the country's weather.

Where the fire found the jars, the jars answered it. Clay that had been fired once, to seal saints in, was fired a second time, to break; and what the spiral had spent generations pressing together — limb into limb, name into name — the heat unbound in a night, if the ruin tells its own tempo truly; broken jars stand in that ash yet.

And against the burning skyline the stakes stood in rows. See them first as the army must have seen them, before this history weighs them: sharpened timber laden with the dead, fence upon fence ringing every position the crusade held, horned silhouettes black and regular against the glow. Regular — because impalement in such numbers is not a way of killing but a way of publishing; the spike became the general's heraldry and his title both because the spike was the message and the whole scorched land was the page. Those body-laden fences stand yet. The land was the chief witness, I said; the land has kept its testimony raised. And the grandam of the Hornsent, our angriest and oldest voice, needs no paraphrase from me: her goddess commanded it, and her people's tower-city, their saints, their inquisitors, their potentates, and their children went into the fire together.

Here the chronicler owes the reader the hardest paragraph in this book so far, and I will not shirk it. This history has already asked you to sit with the jars — with a civilization that packed the living into clay, limb melding into limb, and called the result sainthood; that harvested an unwalled village of its daughters, generation upon generation, because their flesh accepted the rite. The Hornsent were that civilization. And the crusade was *this*: a people — not an army, a people — burned, spitted, and swept from their own holy land at the command of a woman whose family had gone into their jars. I have read historians who resolve such symmetries into justice, and historians who resolve them into tragedy, and I decline both comforts. The jars do not excuse the stakes. The stakes do not excuse the jars. The relics record two horrors, one answering the other, each set down by its perpetrators with the same terrible absence of embarrassment. If the reader wants a moral, the reader has come to the wrong land.

Two touches of light survive from the burning, and because they are nearly the only ones, I set them down with care. First: when the crusade's path reached the ancient stone ruins of **Rauh** — the spiral-carved bones of the civilization older than the Hornsent themselves — the Fire Knights went to their prince and argued for the ruins, and Messmer listened, and Rauh was spared the torch. An army that petitions its general for the life of an enemy's ancestors, and a general who grants it, is not an army of monsters, which is precisely what makes the rest of the record so difficult. Second, and less gently: a church in the crusade's path was burned to the ground — no other torch was abroad in that country — and the woman who survived it, **Romina**, gathered from the scorched remains a twisted element of the divine and wove it into the scarlet rot, that its buds might root where the fire had passed. The golden histories, which do not know this war happened, likewise do not know that the rot's sainthood was kindled by it. Wars that are erased do not stop having consequences; the consequences merely go unexplained.

## The serpent and the fire

What did Messmer's soldiers see, when they looked at the man they followed into a war their own armor called graceless? By every relic they left, they saw a lord worth exile. The Fire Knights gave up names, houses, and homelands for him. His common soldiers took his badge into penal graves. This history contains lords who were feared, lords who were bought, and lords who were believed; the Impaler, on the evidence, was *loved*, and I record it without irony, because the record does, and because it is the detail his mother's Order worked hardest to make unthinkable.

They loved him, and most of them did not know what he was. The serpent his mother had sealed in his boyhood was still in him — the fire he wielded was no gift of the Erdtree, and the relics of his own order treat its origin as a thing kindled from below rather than bestowed from above. Some scholars venture further: that his flame was kin to the flame of ruin, the fell fire of the one-eyed god of the giants, which alone among fires could threaten the Erdtree itself — a tidy theory, joining the Order's two great fire-fears in a single bloodline, and nothing in the record compels it. I leave it to the theorists. What the record *does* give is this: two of his own Black Knight commanders, **Andreas** and his son **Huw**, learned of the serpentine nature their prince carried, and turned on him, and were entombed for their defiance. Loyalty that survives a graceless war and breaks on a secret — that is the measure of how well the secret was kept, and of what it cost to keep it.

One further relic from the prince's own effects, and I will let it stand almost bare, because it points forward out of this chapter like a brand carried toward a dry field. Among the things taken from Messmer's line is a kindling, and its inscription says that he had a younger sister, and that the two of them alike bore visions of fire. Her name the record withholds. Whether the withholding has an author I cannot yet say — the reader knows by now that in this family, an absent name is never to be skimmed past — and this history will have cause to speak of a fire-fated daughter. Mark the kindling, and wait.

## The knight from the wrong century

Now the chronicler must confess a wound in his own chronology, and show only where it bleeds. Among the swords sworn to Messmer the remembrances name his devoted companion, **Rellana**, the Twin Moon Knight, younger sister to **Rennala**, queen of Caria — a knight of the golden afternoon serving a war of the golden dawn, and the relics of his commander **Gaius**, boyhood rival to a Carian grandson, whisper the same trouble. This problem will be weighed where Caria enters this history, and nowhere sooner; for now the reader need only carry the paradox: **the crusade is certain; its place in time is not**, and a war whose beginning cannot be dated and whose ending never came is exactly the kind of war one can hide.

## The veil

For hidden it was. This is the fact the entire chapter has been walking toward, and it is as certain as anything in this book: **the Land of Shadow was concealed from the Lands Between.** The first holy land of the world — the tower, the ruins, the graves, the garrison — was curtained out of sight and out of reckoning, and the golden centuries proceeded as though the map had always ended where the veil began. And with the land went the war. I have searched every relic of the base golden record that has passed through this library — the remembrances, the epitaphs, the regalia, the liturgies of a civilization that carved its lord's lesser duels onto his crown — and the name Messmer appears in none of them. Nor Hornsent. Nor Belurat, nor the tower, nor the crusade. The reader has met this eraser before, in the remembrance that names a mother and no father — there it took one name off one son's soul. Here is the same instrument at full stretch: not a name this time but a *war* — an army, an enemy, a country, a son — rubbed out of the histories so completely that the golden age never had to confess it had a first war at all. The silence is total, and the silence has the shape of intent, and I have stopped pretending such shapes are accidents.

I will admit what that discovery did to me, because the method I promised the reader includes the historian. The night I satisfied myself that the war had been removed rather than lost, I set down my pen and walked the seven rooms of this collection — the finest testimony ever assembled in the Lands Between, every relic of it passed through other hands before mine. I was not looking for anything. That is the trouble. A hand strong enough to take a war out of the record of the world would not have strained at taking something smaller out of a library, and a removed thing leaves no socket where it stood; the shelf closes over it and shows no seam. I found nothing missing. I ask the reader to notice, as I did, that this is exactly what I would have found either way.

When the veil fell, the relics do not say outright, but they lean, and I will show the reader which way. The crusade was raised openly from Erdtree society — noble houses do not disown their children over a war no one can perceive; the grandam counts the concealment among the betrayals that *followed* the purge, a second injury after the first; and the remembrance carved for Messmer's own soul says that his mother, afraid of what she had made, secreted her son away within the realm of shadow — the completed war's general, hidden with the completed war's evidence. Every sign that survives, in short, puts the veiling *after* the burning: first the crusade, then the curtain. I record, as duty requires, that some scholars would reverse the order and have the land veiled before ever the crusade began, so that the whole war was fought inside the dark; their case rests again on the arrangement of a compiled page, and again I decline to let a scribe's line-order outvote the grandam and the remembrance together. As for *why* — here the record closes its mouth. Perhaps the veil was drawn to hide the purge itself, the evidence of what gold had done in its first morning; the abandoned land and the concealed graves make that reading easy, and no text makes it certain. Perhaps a mother who had burned her birthplace could not bear to have it on the map. The queen who left us no explanations left none here either, and I will not put words on a silence she built so carefully.

*On the sources, a caution.* The reader should know how thin the ice is beneath this section's chronology. That the land was hidden is bedrock. That Marika's hand hid it is inference — strong, convergent, but inference: she is the age's god, the land held her home, her victims, her son, and her secret, and no rival with the power to curtain a country appears anywhere in the record. And the veil itself was never absolute. Rellana crossed it, whenever she crossed; the garrison within was maintained, provisioned, and commanded for what the Shadow Keep's confiscated relic-hoards suggest was a very long time; and in days close to my own, others have crossed it again — but their crossing belongs to the age of ash, and I will not reach for it early. The veil hid the land from sight and from memory. It does not appear to have locked the door. Whether that was mercy, oversight, or design, the record — the reader will be unsurprised to hear — is silent.

So ends the first war of the golden age: not with a treaty, not with a triumph, but with a curtain. Consider, at the last, what was on each side of it. On the outer side, the Lands Between, gold from coast to coast, beginning the long bright centuries this history must next describe, innocent of the crusade in the precise way that a scraped page is innocent of the lines it once held. On the inner side: a burned holy land; a people of horns reduced to survivors and hatred; a shadowed tree; graves placed where no tree could see them; and a red-haired prince who was loved by his knights and feared by his mother, left standing warden over the ashes of the war she desired — the seal of grace in his eye socket the last golden thing in the whole dark country. Marika did not extinguish her son's fire when she hid him. She shut it in, still burning, and walked away to her bright kingdom — a wound not healed but bandaged, with the torch that made it sealed up inside the dressing. The golden histories say nothing of what smoldered under the bandage for ten thousand years. This history will.
