# Chapter XIII: The Burning

*Wherein the tree no blade could open is opened by a daughter; Death is let out of its long confinement through a single wound; and the chronicler, having written a whole history at a safe distance, runs out of distance.*

---

Every chapter before this one I wrote the proper way: at arm's length, from relics, about the dead. This chapter I cannot write that way, because I was in it — a small, unarmed, unconsulted part of it — and the voice now changes. History is what a man assembles; what follows is partly what a man remembers, and memory is a witness I have spent this whole book teaching you to search for weapons at the door. Search me too.

Three sources feed this chapter. First, what I saw and heard in the Roundtable Hold itself, which sits outside the world and yet burned with it. Second, the words the stranger spared us — for the last Tarnished passed through the Hold all that year as a needle passes through cloth, and left us threads: half-sentences, a name. They were few. Third, what the ash preserved, gathered by those who went into the capital when it cooled. Notice how thin that is. Elsewhere my claims stood on stone and gold; this chapter stands on smoke, on hearsay, and on a frightened man's memory of a bad year; the frightened man is me. I have kept the rules. Watch me twice as closely while I keep them. Where these three disagree, I will say so. They disagree at the worst possible moment, as the reader will see.

## The tree that refused

The stranger took Leyndell. That is history now, and I have told it as history: the golden shade at the gates, the Omen King dead before the throne he had guarded all his despised life. What came next was not victory. The stranger climbed to the Erdtree expecting a door and found a fist: the trunk sealed behind thorns, clenched against all comers. No blade opened them; no common fire so much as warmed them. The tree that had summoned the Tarnished home to claim it had, at the last step, refused to be claimed.

I remember how that news arrived at the Hold, because it arrived twice. Once as words. Then as silence — for the Two Fingers, the great dumb oracle past the smithy, drew inward and stopped answering, and Enia the finger-reader told any who asked that they communed with the Greater Will, and that the answer might outlast every mortal in the building. She said *centuries* the way other people say *tomorrow*, and I remember laughing — the last laugh in my notes of that year. Then, to her credit, she said the honest thing underneath it: act on your own judgment. It was the whole apparatus of guidance — grace, maidens, Fingers, the summons across the fog — conceding, in one tired sentence, that no one was steering. The reader who remembers Metyr, mother of those Fingers, broken beneath her cathedral awaiting messages that never came, may wonder how long no one had been steering. So do I. The record does not say.

## The forge, and the old curse's arithmetic

One fire in the world could still burn the Erdtree, and this history has told you where, and who kept it.

Recall the wars of the first lord: when Godfrey's armies annihilated the Fire Giants in the northern peaks, their forge-flame — the fell fire that could burn the Erdtree itself — would not go out by any art the Order possessed, and Queen Marika spared the last of the giants and cursed him to tend the flame he could not quench, alone, forever. I wrote, when I set down the curse spoken over the last giant, that no relic gives her reason, and none does; call it mercy or a longer sentence than death — the texts permit both and endorse neither. But observe the arithmetic. The curse preserved, through every golden century, the one fire in creation that could destroy the golden centuries' god-tree — kept it lit, kept it watched, kept it *findable*. What is certain is this: when her tree clenched shut against the champion her own recorded voice had promised to recall, the road led straight to the fire she had refused to let die. The stranger crossed the mountaintops, killed the last keeper of the Flame of Ruin — the sentence commuted at last — and stood before the Forge of the Giants. The curse had waited out its author. It repaid itself in full.

What the forge required next, no relic had to say, because the girl had come along precisely to say it.

## The daughter

I must now assemble a person, and I ask the reader's patience — because I find, sitting with my pen over her name, that I would rather weigh anything else.

Her name was Melina. What is certain of her comes largely from her own telling, repeated by the one who heard it: that she was burned already, and bodiless, when she met the stranger at the edge of Limgrave; that she was born within the Erdtree, at its foot; that her mother, inside the Erdtree, gave her a purpose long ago, and that she was seeking the strength to see it done. She struck an accord — the maiden's part, runes into strength, in exchange for passage to the tree — and she kept it. At the wayside churches of Marika, pilgrims heard her do a thing no other creature in the record does: she spoke the queen's own remembered words aloud, as though the silence of ten thousand statues had finally found one small mouth.

Now the weighing. Her mother is never named — not by her, not by any text I hold. But set the signs together. She was born within the Erdtree; the only mother this history has ever placed inside the Erdtree is the queen sealed in its heart. Her purpose was given by that mother, and her purpose — we are coming to it — was to burn the tree, which is to say, to finish what the imprisoned queen's shattering began. She alone carried Marika's voice. And from the Land of Shadow, one further relic, which the reader has met already, carried home with the expedition's witnesses: the kindling borne by Messmer the Impaler names a younger sister who, like him, bore a vision of fire within her. The sister is certain; the relic states her. That the sister is Melina — kindling to her name, fire behind a sealed eye, mother in the tree — no text says, and I will keep faith with my rules: it is the conclusion toward which every surviving sign leans, and I hold it as I hold her mother's identity — firmly, with reasoning shown, the door left open for a better-armed scholar. If I am right, the family this book has followed across ten thousand years ends as it began: with Marika's children spent on Marika's purposes.

Some scholars venture further still, and name Melina the Gloam-Eyed Queen — the ancient godskin empress whom Maliketh broke, mistress of the god-slaying black flame — reborn or persisting behind that famous closed lid. The eye, the affinity with Destined Death, the vow we shall shortly hear of, all rhyme. But rhyme is not record: nothing in any text joins the two names, and I set the idea on the shelf where I keep handsome guesses. The shelf has grown long this year; I no longer browse it for pleasure.

At the forge, then. The purpose her mother gave her inside the Erdtree was this: the tree burns only if a body burns first. She was the kindling. She had always been the kindling. And the account I believe — I will show you the other in a moment — says that at the Forge of the Giants she took her leave gently, took the Flame of Ruin into herself, and said, in words that survive only as the stranger let them fall, that whatever her mother had designed, the choosing was her own. Then she burned.

I was in the Hold when the sky changed. I will come back to that. First, my duty by the fork in the record.

*On the sources, at the fire.* The accounts differ on whose flame took the tree, and this is no quibble; the two versions describe two different worlds. The gentle account is the one I have just given: Melina, kindling by her own consent, her purpose fulfilled, the stranger bereaved and blameless at her pyre. The terrible account runs otherwise. It says the stranger had gone down, before ever crossing the mountains — down beneath the capital, past the sealed door of the Three Fingers, that other and older guidance whose whole creed is that existence was a mistake to be melted back into one — and had taken the frenzied flame into their own flesh. There was even a voice that urged the bargain: a dead swordsman's mouth worn by something calling itself Shabriri, promising that if the stranger carried the flame, the girl need not die. In this account she does not die. In this account she looks at what her companion has become, and her sealed eye opens, and she departs with a promise instead of a farewell: that she will hunt the flame-branded lord to the end of any road, and deliver what is owed — Destined Death itself. And the tree burns anyway, with the stranger for kindling. I cannot choose between these accounts, and this is not modesty; every other fork in this book, I have walked both paths and told you which had the firmer ground. Here the ground is the same: both versions come through the same thin channel — what was said, and not said, at the Hold — and the stranger's words touching the forge were fewer than on any other subject, a datum I decline to interpret in front of you. The tree burned. A woman made of fire and grief was at the heart of it. Whose will lit it, and whether she lies in the ash or walks somewhere still, carrying Death on a leash of her own vowing — the record holds both, and I must too.

On what the flame did once it had the tree, the accounts agree, because every land could see it. The Erdtree did not burn as wood burns, from the bark inward. It caught from within. The witnesses — pilgrim and deserter alike — describe the same impossible order of events: first a deepening of the trunk's gold, resin going to ember behind the bark, light climbing under the surface the way blood moves in a hand held against a lamp. Then the branches took it, one, then tens, each limb kindling along its length from the inside, fire running through them like veins under translucent skin, until the crown that had been the sky's one fixed gold stood remade in hotter metal — and, brightening, began to die. By full dark there was no night in the Lands Between: black sky, and against it the tree entire — a second sun, born at dusk, already setting. The gold went from the leaves first, and they came down as cinders; then from the light itself, which soured from honey to brass to the color of cooling iron. What settled afterward on every land alike was gray. The world's gold had spent all of history refusing to be any other color. It took one night to become ash.

I had built a finer sentence for this. I carried it for years, the way a man rehearses what he will say at a deathbed for fear of arriving with nothing. It was a good sentence, and I will not write it down. The choice is mine, not the pen's: the ash took the thing itself, and may keep the words. The tree burned. Everyone alive looked up. Those are the sentences that survive.

## The city outside time

The burning opened no gate that any account describes; it simply removed the stranger from the world. The next report places the last Tarnished in Crumbling Farum Azula, the drowned-in-sky city of the ancient dragons, which the reader walked with me in the age before gold, and which no living road reaches. How the passage was made, the record does not say, and I flag the silence honestly: no author here, no hand on the eraser — a hole of the kind time chews in every archive.

But mark where the fire delivered its survivor: Farum Azula, eternally mid-ruin, its towers falling and never landing — and at its heart, in the storm beyond time, the oldest king in this history, Dragonlord Placidusax, who is said to have been Elden Lord in the age before the Erdtree, under a god of his own; abandoned by that god for reasons no relic gives; coiled into the storm to await its return, and waiting yet. I opened this book's account of the world with that grief. Whether the stranger crossed the storm and stood before him, nothing reports; I prefer — and admit I merely prefer — the thought of the two passing unmet.

## The Black Blade

What waited on the required road through Farum Azula was not the Dragonlord but a beast in clergyman's rags — the last of this history's long-running disguises, coming off. The reader has met Gurranq, the Beast Clergyman, gnawing his endless meal of deathroot in the Bestial Sanctum. Gurranq was Maliketh, the Black Blade — Marika's shadow, bound to her when the Fingers raised her up, and by the surviving inscriptions her half-brother besides, though the older wording may mean kin-by-oath rather than blood, and I will not pick a meaning the texts declined to fix.

Recall what he carried; it is the hinge of everything after. When Marika cut Destined Death out of the Elden Ring so that her Order need never truly die — the surgery this history has already witnessed — it was Maliketh who received the excised thing, sealed in the black blade of his own body, and Maliketh who bore the consequence: a life spent as the vault of the world's confiscated mortality, guarding the wound where Death used to be. His own remembrance calls what his god did to him a betrayal. His centuries of penance — begging deathroot from strangers, devouring the seepage of his own failure after a fragment was stolen to make the knives that killed a prince — I told in their place. In Farum Azula the long custody ended. The Beast Clergyman fell, and rose again as the Black Blade for one final duty — the duty of being the last lock on the door. Of the duel the record keeps almost nothing, and I will not furnish a room the relics left bare. This much the inscriptions allow: the blade he drew at the end was the one this history has already named — his own body's, black to the heart, Destined Death kept so long in his flesh that the flesh had taken its edge. He was killed.

And Destined Death came unbound. Say it as flatly as the relics force me to: the surgery was undone. What Marika removed was returned; the deathless Order's founding exemption expired in a single moment, in a falling city outside time. Death came back not as an army, nor a verdict, but as weather returning to a country that had walled out the sky for an age — no herald, no border, no first place, only everywhere it had always belonged. Those who have written of it since all reach for the same borrowed storm: grave-cold lightning, black sap rising in old roots, the pallor of flesh remembering what flesh is for. Gods, from that hour, were mortal. The reader has spent ten thousand years of this history watching one family arrange the world so that sentence could never be written. It has now been written. In Leyndell they knew it without a messenger, because the sky told them: ash began to fall like a gray snow that would not melt, and did not stop.

## The ash, and the Hold

The Hold burned too. It sits outside the world, and it burned anyway, which taught me more than any relic in this library: there was no outside anymore. I remember the fire arriving not as a blaze but as a smell, and then as a glow at the seam of the smithy door. Then the ash found the library. It came in without violence — under the sills, through the shutter-seams, a gray sifting, patient as its source had not been — and it settled on everything: on the lamps, which went small and brown, so that I read as through dirty glass; on the All-Knowing's seven rooms of testimony, the first reader I ever saw treat every source the same; and on my own pages. I laid my hand flat on the chapter then drying and felt grit move across my own sentences, and understood what the smoke had failed to teach me: the subject of this history had entered the room where it was being written. A method is a room. I had believed mine had no door the world could use. The ash did not need the door.

The smith Hewg — chained a lifetime to the task of forging a weapon fit to kill a god — worked on while it came, though his memory guttered in stretches; some days he knew his own name, some days only the task, as if the task were the deeper inscription and the man the shallower one. His last coherent plea, preserved by those who heard it, was not for rescue but for use: take the masterpiece and slay a god. Whether it was good enough, he did not live to learn. I answer from the aftermath, too late for him to hear: yes. It is the only claim in this history I did not check against anything, and I would make it again.

The spirit-tuner Roderika would not leave him. No remembrance will be pressed for a smith and a girl who stayed, and no great rune marks the choice; by every standard the gold ever taught, the entry is useless. I record it anyway. Of all the entries in this chronicle, it is the one I would fight longest to keep. A history that keeps only what gold was spent on is a ledger, not a history.

Gideon Ofnir had already gone. The master of this library had followed his knowing into the ash-drowned capital, and made his last stand in the corridors of the fortified manor — halls built, for reasons no text explains, as a mirror of the Hold itself, so that he died, if the reports place it truly, in a room shaped like the one I am writing in. He had concluded — and these are his convictions as they were repeated, not facts — that the whole summons was a wheel: that Marika desired the Tarnished's struggle without end, and no Tarnished would therefore ever be permitted to become Elden Lord. Knowing everything, he chose to die fighting the disproof rather than watch the wheel turn. What exactly he had learned, no surviving word says.

I have spent these years among Gideon's books, mistrusting them by the method he never quite practiced — for the All-Knowing collected knowledge the way a miser collects coin, as a defense against spending it. This book is built out of his shelves, and it exists to disagree with him: the sources he hoarded, read honestly, do not prove that no one may be Elden Lord; only that we cannot yet know who, or whether, or at what price — a humbler despair, and a more useful one. I am aware, most evenings, that he would consider this theft. The dead owner of a library is owed one thing only: that his collection be read better than he read it. I have tried to pay it.

## The warrior

One trial remained, and the record of it is the cleanest thing in this chapter, as if the world wished to end on a legible page.

Godfrey, first Elden Lord — whose whole arc this book has told: the tamed warlord, the spent purpose, the dimmed eyes, the exile that made the very word *Tarnished* — stood in the ash before the throne room, in flesh, at the head of no army, to claim with his hands what marriage had once granted him. He was found not fighting but kneeling, over the body of the Omen King — his son, if the reasoning this history laid out over the first lord's line holds, and I believe it holds — the cursed child who had defended to the death the Order that never blessed him. Then the first lord rose and offered the last battle, and partway through it Godfrey did the thing I admire most in this savage chronicle: he stopped being Godfrey. He crushed the beast regent Serosh — the old lion bound to his back a lifetime ago as the leash on his bloodlust, the condition of his crown — and with it shed every borrowed regality, and fought his final fight as what he had been before any queen improved him: Hoarah Loux, warrior, bare-chested, roaring, first of his name and of nothing else. He had carried a kingdom's worth of titles across two exiles and a resurrection, and at the end set them all down to die honest. He was defeated. I decline to write *he lost*.

The stranger walked past the broken lion, past the man who had been two names, through the char where the thorns had stood, into the root-heart of the world — where the queen hangs in her own tree, and where whatever remained to happen, happened.

And there my knowledge ends — at that door as a shoreline ends at the sea: not because nothing lies beyond, but because I cannot walk on it. The stranger did not come back through the Hold. No relic has come out of that room to me — only traditions, six of them, each sworn to by someone, each incompatible with the rest; and this book will end by laying all six before you and defending none.
