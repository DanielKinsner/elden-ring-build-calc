# Chapter VIII: The Night of the Black Knives

*Wherein veiled women carry stolen Death into the sleeping capital, the first of the demigods dies without dying, a witch dies without ending, and the golden age is over before the sun is up.*

---

The age this chapter closes rested on a single amendment to the nature of things: under the Golden Order, death was not final. The dead of the Lands Between were gathered into the Erdtree's roots to circle back into its golden cycle, and the one power that could make an ending *stick* — Destined Death, the Rune of Death, the oldest and least negotiable clause in the world's law — had been cut out of the Elden Ring by Queen Marika herself and locked away. An empire of the deathless, ruled by a god who could not die, peopled by demigod children who could not die, worshipping a tree that recycled everyone else. It had stood so for longer than any dynasty in this book, and its subjects had stopped being able to imagine the alternative.

What broke it was a burglary.

I have written chapters of war, of crusade, of whole civilizations put to the fire, and none of them did to this land what one night's work with knives did. So the reader will forgive me if I proceed here less like a chronicler and more like a magistrate. A murder inquiry wants three answers: *with what*, *by whom*, and *why*. This is the rare crime in our history where the record hands us the first answer complete, the second half-veiled, and the third written in two competing hands — and I intend to lay out all three exactly as far as the relics carry them, and not one step past.

## The instrument

Begin with the weapon, because the weapon is the part of this crime nobody disputes.

When Marika removed the Rune of Death from the Elden Ring, it did not cease to exist; law cannot be uninvented, only impounded. The rune was given into the keeping of **Maliketh, the Black Blade** — her shadowbound beast, the inseparable guardian bound to her at her ascension, whom the inscriptions of his own arms name her loyal half-brother — the reader has stood at the queen's ascension and seen the seam in that word, and knows that either reading puts him as close to the queen as a creature can stand. Maliketh sealed Destined Death within his sword, and with that sealing the black flame of the god-slaying cults lost its power to kill gods, and the Order's deathlessness became not doctrine but fact.

Mark, though, what the sealing quietly accomplished. Death had been a condition; Marika made it an *object*. An object has a location. A location has a keeper. And a keeper — even a keeper who is the god's own devoted kin — is a thing that can be robbed. The Order's proudest security was also the whole of its risk, gathered into one blade in one guardian's grip, and the empire slept for centuries on the assumption that no one would ever be clever enough, patient enough, and cold enough to make the attempt.

Someone was. We do not have to guess who, because she told us.

## The heiress who declined

**Ranni**, called the Witch, was a daughter of **Rennala**, the Full Moon Queen of Caria, and of **Radagon** — which made her, after her father abandoned the Carian court to become Marika's second consort, a demigod stepdaughter of the god herself. Let the kinship be exact, for it matters to everything that follows: Ranni was Marika's stepdaughter, and Godwyn, whom we shall come to, was her stepbrother. No blood ran between the killer's line and the victim's, said the age; only law, and a shared table. What the age did not know, this history has already laid bare where it weighed the red-haired consort against his queen: if Radagon was Marika, the stepdaughter was the queen's blood daughter and the stepbrother her half-brother, and the knives of this chapter stayed, after all, within the family. I set the kinship down both ways because both mattered — the false reckoning governed how the age judged the crime; the true one governs what the crime was.

It has been told already how the Two Fingers named their three Empyreans — candidates fit to succeed Marika as god of a coming age — and how Ranni's election came sealed with the shadowbound wolf-man **Blaidd**, companion and fail-safe in one skin, the Fingers insuring their candidate against straying. What this chapter's crime requires of that story is only the office, and what the office implied.

They had reason. Ranni looked at the prize — at what being the vessel of the Greater Will had made of Marika — and declined it with her whole soul. This is no inference of mine; it is her own account, given in her own person within living memory, and it is the star testimony of this entire chapter. Godhood, as she understood it, meant being owned: body and soul surrendered to the thing behind the Fingers. So she resolved to spoil the goods. The Fingers' claim, she reasoned, lay upon her Empyrean *flesh*; very well — the flesh would die, and the soul would slip out of the contract through the gap. She would need a death that could kill a demigod, in a world where such deaths had been abolished.

She has confessed the rest flatly: it was Ranni who stole a fragment of Destined Death from Maliketh's keeping.

How the theft was done — by what stealth, what bribery, what treachery of the vault — the record does not say, and I confess the magistrate in me grinds his teeth at the omission. What we hold instead is the aftermath, reconstructed by a witness this library remembers fondly: **Sir Rogier**, a knight-sorcerer who lodged in this very hold in the last days before the burning, and who made the Night his obsession. It was Rogier who traced the assassins' blades to their source and established what the stolen fragment became: knives — black knives, worked through a rite he calls fearsome, a forging that warped the blades themselves as if the metal objected to what was being poured into it. Each knife carried a sliver of Destined Death. Each was, in a deathless world, a key to the one locked door.

And the robbed keeper? Maliketh's response, when it came, tells us how deep the wound went: he bound the remainder of the rune into his own flesh, so that the vault and the guardian could never again be parted, and gave the rest of his long life to hunting down every escaped scrap of Death and swallowing it back. The beast that prowled the eastern sanctum in the last age, devouring death-tainted roots with a penitent's hunger, was the same guardian — and his hunger, I judge, was one part duty and three parts shame.

## The night

Of the night itself, the capital's records give us almost nothing and the killers' own equipment gives us almost everything. So let the equipment testify, and let it testify as a scene.

The gear argues its wearers counted on no light at all. **Leyndell** at the height of the golden age's peace: a capital so long past fearing anything that its gold slept easy in the dark, indigo where the braziers failed, soot-black past the last lamp. Through it walked women. Veiled, in number unrecorded, and dressed in what survives to instruct us — armor supple and night-blue, worked to swallow sound, so that we know to the very seam how quiet the age's ending was. No footfall. No ring of mail. Cloth that ate its own rustle. The one hard surface those women carried was the one that mattered: the black knives, warped in the forging, each with its sliver of stolen Death sitting in the metal like a splinter of the old law. A glint where a blade turned, the hem of a veil against darker dark, and then the night closing over both, because the armor was made so that it could. Every plank of that scene is a relic — veils, blue, silence, knives; I have furnished the murder from its own evidence and nothing else.

Now the magistrate resumes. The armor was the gear of people who expected to walk past guards rather than through them, and its inscription carries the detail I must handle with tongs. The armor texts record of these women that they were rumored to be "Numen, with close ties to Queen Marika," as the inscription itself has it. The Numen: that rare, long-lived people said to hail from beyond this world, whose women attended Marika's court in the manner of kin, and whose stock rumor makes Marika's own — it was this very knife-print's whisper, the reader may remember, that we first weighed beside a braid of golden hair in a meadow with a chapel in it.

I give the rumor as a rumor, for that is what the relic calls it, and a chronicler who launders rumor into fact has stopped being useful. But I will not pretend the rumor is idle, and I ask the reader to stand where I stand and feel the weight of it. If it is true, then the women who carried stolen Death into the god's own capital were the god's own kinswomen — the closed, foreign, long-lived circle that stood nearest the queen of anyone alive. Such women do not assemble as bandits assemble. Someone gathered them; someone armed them from Ranni's forging; someone told them which doors, which halls, which sleeping prince. The record preserves the blades, the armor, and the rumor of the blood — and of the chain of obedience that moved those veiled figures through the night, not one link. That silence sits at the exact center of this crime, and the reader should keep an eye on it, as I do.

The victim was **Godwyn the Golden**.

Of all the demigods the Order had to lose, it lost the one it could least afford: Marika's celebrated son of the golden lineage — his father, by the reasoning this book set out when it treated the first Elden Lord's house, was **Godfrey**, for the relics define that lineage as Godfrey and his offspring, though no single line troubles to name the obvious — the prince who had turned the ancient dragons from enemies into comrades, whose sworn companion was the dragon **Fortissax**, and whose name was the nearest thing the golden age had to a synonym for its own future. Why the knives sought Godwyn, of all the god's children — whether he was the target of the rite's design or merely the demigod whose death would be felt farthest — no relic says. The record gives us the choice of victim as a bare fact with no reasoning attached, and I set it down the same way.

The knives found him, and then the night did something stranger than murder.

## One death, cut lengthwise

Here is the fact the whole remainder of this history grows from, and it is stated in the record as plainly as anything in this book: on the Night of the Black Knives, Godwyn the Golden died *in soul only*, his body living on without him — and Ranni died *in body only*, her soul surviving the destruction of her flesh. One Death, the first true Death dealt in the Lands Between since the rune was sealed, divided between two demigods — and a cursemark of death split between them to prove it. Half that mark can be seen to this day, graven on the abandoned corpse of Ranni's Empyrean body where it sits enthroned atop the Divine Tower of Liurnia; the sword forged for Godwyn's funeral names him, without rival, the first of the demigods to die.

Set the halves side by side, reader, for the mirror is the point. The prince: soul slain, body warm, breathing, *alive*, and empty — a golden shell the Order would inter among the Erdtree's roots because its doctrine had no other drawer to file him in. The witch: body slain, soul loose, at liberty — a spirit that would in time take up residence in a small jointed doll and continue her war against the Fingers from inside it. He kept everything but himself; she kept nothing *but* herself. Her half was the plan — she has said so; the body's death was the whole object of her conspiracy. His half is the question. Whether the rite *required* a second, opposite half-death — whether one cannot cheat death of a soul without paying it a body, or cheat it of a body without paying a soul — or whether Godwyn's ruin was simply the price of aiming stolen Death with mortal hands, no relic explains. The record shows us the arithmetic's answer and burns the working.

*On the sources, and on the question every reader is now asking.* Whose design was the Night? The confession we hold is Ranni's, and it is generous by the standards of this land's testimony: the theft was hers, the forging was hers, and the orchestration of the night she takes upon herself without flinching. I accept it; a witness confessing to the crime of the age has little reason to invent her guilt. But observe, as a magistrate must, what the confession does *not* contain. It does not name the assassins. It does not explain how a Carian stepdaughter, however brilliant, came to command veiled women who — if the armor's rumor holds — belonged to the closed kin-circle of the queen herself. It does not say who let them into Leyndell. Perhaps Ranni's reach was simply that long, and the whole night hers from vault to bedchamber; nothing in the record forbids it. Yet some scholars venture further, and the reader deserves to hear the theory stated honestly before I put it back in its drawer: that the hand on the knife was, at whatever remove, Marika's own — that only the queen could have opened her capital, loosed her kinswomen, and aimed the blow; that the mother's later deeds, which this history has still to face, were not answer to the crime but continuation of it. It is a theory with excellent taste in evidence and no evidence: no relic names Marika its organizer, and the whole edifice rests on the rumor of the assassins' blood plus a reading of her silence. I do not build on silences, though I have learned — the reader has learned with me — to weigh this queen's silences like ingots. The confession stands; the veils stay on; and the question of whether a god grieved that night or merely watched her own work completed is one this historian records, and cannot close.

## What grew from the grave

The empire buried its prince in the only way it knew: downward, into the roots, into the cycle that had always taken the dead and given back golden order. It was the worst thing they could have done, and they could not have known it.

For the body was still alive, and Death was in it — and what Death does with a body it has been let into but not allowed to finish, witnesses of the last age saw beneath the capital, and their account I set down as they carried it out.

The prince did not decay. Decay was the old law's mercy, and the old law was locked in a sword. He *enlarged*. Down among the roots that were meant to carry him home, the golden flesh whitened and spread and put out what no body should own: a face where no skull was, vast and slack against the root-vault; fin and frill where a man keeps his shoulders; a pallid eye budding blind from the mass the way a mushroom-cap buds from wet timber. Mildew-white. Root-threaded. Breathing. And from that flesh, along those same roots, Death travelled like black sap drawn the wrong way — down instead of up, outward instead of home — surfacing across the Lands Between as the black growths men learned to call Deathroot.

The first casualty was the faithful **Fortissax**, who went down into his companion's grave to fight the thing rooting in him — a duel with a principle, the most hopeless engagement in this entire history of hopeless engagements — and was corrupted for his loyalty into a lichdragon, red lightning gone grave-cold. The second casualty was the order of the dead. Where the Deathroot surfaced, the dead stopped keeping to the Order's schedule. They rose. They wandered. Those Who Live in Death, the coming ages named them — skeletons that reassembled, corpse-mariners, carrion birds of the death-rite — an entire counter-order seeded from one warm grave, the murdered prince becoming in his ruin the **Prince of Death**, unwilling patriarch of everything his mother's Order had abolished. The doctrinal wars this ignited — the hunters of the dead on one side, those who pitied the deathless dead on the other — will come in their own season; the seed is planted here, on the Night, in the roots.

Above ground, the empire grieved as empires do: in gold and liturgy. A sword was forged for the prince's funeral rites, holy, bright, and inscribed with a prayer that stops the breath if read plainly — a prayer that Godwyn the Golden be granted, at last, a *true death*. The Order of deathlessness, praying for death; no relic in this collection states the age's bankruptcy more economically. The prayer is written as a child's, for a beloved lord brother, and by every sign that survives the child was Miquella — the same brother whose faith at Castle Sol in the far north later prayed an eclipse might call a spirit back into a soulless demigod. The sun, the record notes, never darkened. Grief built its weapons and its rites, and none of them worked, and the fish-eyed thing beneath the capital went on breathing.

And the mother?

I must first enter into evidence a witness this book has not called until now: the historian. Working through this night's relics I have caught myself, more than once, handling them kindly — reading the queen's stillness as shock, hearing a mother in every silence, wanting the veiled women to be no kin of hers and the opened doors to be treachery rather than permission. I want her innocent of this. There — the wanting is on the page, where it can do no quiet work in the margins. A wish holds no tier in my method and will be given none; I record it only so the reader may subtract it from every sentence that follows.

Here the record does what the reader now expects of it. Queen Marika the Eternal, who raised ten thousand statues and left no sentences, left none here either. No decree of mourning survives, no inquest, no purge of the guard that failed her son, no hunt proclaimed for veiled women — nothing. Her grief is asserted by every later teller and attested by no relic; perhaps it consumed her, as the common account would have it, and perhaps the common account mistakes a queen's silence for a mother's, and the truth of that night's watcher is stranger than grief. I have promised not to build on silences. I note only that we have met this queen's quiet before, in the matter of a son's erased father and a homeland's erased sky, and that it has never yet meant nothing.

What we know is what she did next. The record holds no interval, no measure of days or years between the Night and her answer — only the order of the two events, and the shape of the answer itself, which was not a decree, and was not a war.

She went to the Elden Ring — the law of everything, the deathless age's charter, her own godhood's seat — and she took up a hammer.

That blow is still falling; the rest of this history is the noise it made. This chapter ends where the golden age ended: in the dark, in Leyndell, with a warm corpse under the roots, an empty body enthroned above Liurnia, and a god standing very, very still.
