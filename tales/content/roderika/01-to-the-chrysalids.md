## Part One: To the Chrysalids

White flower that blooms in catacombs. Each a soundless bell used to summon spirits. — Grave Glovewort

### 1.
I've been meaning to tell you.

I've been meaning to for days now, or weeks. The rain makes it hard to count. And every time I open my mouth to do it, something else comes out instead. A laugh, usually. You'll have heard it, if you can hear anything, where you are. A small dry thing. Heh. Like a cough that's ashamed of itself. I don't know where it comes from. It isn't mine. Very little I say here is mine any more, which is why I'm saying this to you and not to anyone who might answer. You were mine. All of you. I think that's the last true thing I've got, so I'm going to hold it with both hands and talk.

I'm in the hut. You know the one. At the fork, where the road can't decide whether it wants to go up to the castle or off along the cliff, and so it does both, and leaves this little house in the crook of its elbow like something it forgot. The roof is more hole than roof. There's a ladder to the loft that nobody has climbed since we came, and in the loft a key, a stone one, shaped like a little sword, which the captain looked at and put back because it wasn't ours. That's the sort of man he was. You know that. You were there.

I sit on the floor. I sit the way I always sat at home when nobody was looking, legs off to one side under me and my weight on one hand, which is not how a lady sits, I was told, and I have made it how I sit anyway. Sideways. The rain comes through the roof and finds me wherever I put myself, so I've stopped moving. It's easier. It finds you either way.

From the door I can see the castle. Not all of it. A tooth of it, up on the rock, with the birds going round and round. In the mornings the fog comes down and takes the tooth away and I can almost believe the hill just ends up there, in nothing. Then the fog lifts. It always lifts.

I can't leave. I want to say that plainly, once, and then not say it again, because it's the sort of thing that gets smaller the more you say it, and I'd like it to keep its true size. I can go as far as the road. I can put my foot on the road. Then I can't. It isn't a wall. It's more that the road is for going somewhere, and I've forgotten how to be someone who does that. So I come back in and sit down sideways and the rain finds me.

And nothing comes in. I've noticed that. Not the wolves at night. Not the men from the gate in their red and gold. Not the birds, even, though they'd be welcome. They come to the door and they stand there, and something in them decides against it. Perhaps a person can be so small that nothing bothers. I don't know. I've never been small before. I'm learning the rules as I go.

### 2.
I should start with the cloak, because that's where you'd start, if you were the one telling it. And because it's the one part of all this that I understood at the time.

You know the custom. Everyone at home knows it, the way everyone knows which stair creaks. When someone is to leave and not come back, they are given a cloak. Crimson. Good wool, lined, with a hood deep enough to hide a face in. It's given in the long hall, with the family standing, and it's said to be an honour. It's said in the way things are said when everyone in the room knows better.

There's a saying about the cloaks. I won't repeat it, because you know it, and because it's true, and because I've had enough of true things lately. I'll only say that it's a very old custom and a very cheap one. A cloak costs less than a dowry. It costs less than a wall to put someone behind. And it has this to recommend it over both, that the one who wears it goes off believing she's been asked to do something, and by the time she works out that nobody said what, she's a long way from home.

I'll tell you what nobody said. Nobody said, go and take the castle. Nobody said, go and find our cousins, or carry this letter, or bring back that. There was no errand. I was given a journey without one, which is the whole trick of it. Go where it comes from, they said, and it will find you there. That's all. That's the errand. Go where it comes from.

Where what comes from. You know what. The gold.

My brother saw it first. He was standing at the tall window in the east passage, the one that looks out over the harbour, and he said, there, very quietly, and pointed at nothing. I was beside him. I'd come to fetch him for supper. I looked where he was pointing and there was the harbour, and the boats, and the grey water going out to the grey sky. There, he said again, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do. By the end of the week every one of them had seen it. Every one with our blood. My brothers, my sister, my aunt who never left her chair, all of them standing at windows and doors with their faces turned west, saying there, there, do you see it, and I stood where they stood and looked where they looked and I saw the sea.

Threads, they said. Gold threads in the air. Going out over the water, all of them going the same way, to where the fog stands on the horizon like a wall with no gate. Guidance, they said. It had come back. It had been gone so long that it was only a story, the sort you tell children so they'll feel they're from somewhere, and now here it was in the east passage, and my sister was crying, and my brother had his hand flat on the window as if he could touch it.

I tried. I'd like you to know that. I stood at that window every evening for a month. I stood there in the cold until my hands went white. I put my face where his face had been. I'd have given anything. I'd have given the seeing of everything else for the seeing of that. And there was the harbour, and the boats, and the grey water going out.

So. The cloak.

They were kind about it. That's the thing I keep turning over. Nobody said the word that everyone was thinking, the one that means wrong in the blood. They said I was Tarnished, the same as all of us, that it would come, that it was only slow with me. They said, go where it comes from. They gave me the cloak in the long hall with the family standing, and my brother put it on my shoulders himself and did the clasp, and his hands shook, and I understood that he was ashamed, and that he loved me, and that he was glad I was going, and that these three things were all one thing in him and he couldn't have picked them apart if he'd tried.

He gave me a seed. Afterward, in the passage, when it was just us. A gold one, small as a fingernail, warm in the hand, and it hummed. I don't know how else to say it. It hummed the way a bee hums with its wings shut. For when you see it, he said. Plant it where you see it, and something of us will grow there. I put it in the pocket inside the cloak, over my heart, where the lining is, and I've kept it there since. It hums still. I've never once known where to plant it.

That's the cloak. I'm not wearing it now. It's folded under me, on the floor, because the floor is wet and wool is wool. I've thought about putting it on and walking up the road in it and letting the castle see what I am. That's a thought I have. I've had it a great many times. It's a very good thought and it's never once got me to my feet.

### 3.
Now you.

You'll forgive me if I don't say your names. Not even here, where there's nobody to hear them but the rain. I've given away everything else, the house, the country, the way I used to sit, the sound of my own voice not laughing, and your names are the one thing left that's only mine. I'm keeping them in my mouth like stones. If I said them they'd be out in the air and the air here is full of other people's business. So you'll be who you were, and not what you were called.

The captain, then. Grey, and slow to speak, and the only one of you who'd known my mother. He put the key back. He did up my boots on the ship when I couldn't stand to bend. He'd been sent, I think. Told to go. He never said so and I never asked, because I was afraid of the answer, and now I'd give a great deal to have it either way.

The one who sang. Younger than me, a lad with a voice too big for him, who sang the whole crossing, every song he knew and then the same ones over again, and when the captain told him to give it a rest he sang under his breath instead, and the sound came up through the boards of the deck like something in the hull was happy. I never learned where he'd come from. I never asked him that either. I'm seeing a pattern, telling this. I'm seeing that I never asked any of you anything.

The two who quarrelled. Brothers, or as good as. Over an oar, over a blanket, over which of them had seen a fish. You'd have thought they hated each other. Then the fog came down thick on the fourth day and one of them was sick, properly sick, and the other sat with his back to the rail all night and didn't sleep and didn't speak and didn't once let go of his hand. And in the morning they were quarrelling again about who'd been more frightened.

The one with the split lip, who taught me knots. Who said, Milady's hands are too soft for this, and then sat with me for an hour anyway, and by the end of it I could tie a bowline, and I still can. I do it with a bit of string, here, when I need my hands to be somebody's.

The cook, who wasn't really a cook. The tall one who never said a word and was always the first up the mast. The one who prayed at dawn, facing the wrong way, because he didn't know which way was right any more and said it didn't matter, the sky was all one sky.

Nine of you. I count on my fingers. Nine, and me.

Some of you were told to come. I know that. You had families and a lord and it was put to you as a thing that would be done, and so you did it. And some of you weren't told anything, and came anyway, and I don't know which of you were which and I've decided I don't want to. Because there's a point on any crossing when the land is gone behind you and the fog is ahead and there's no more choosing to be done, and after that point it doesn't matter why anyone got on the boat. After that point you were mine. All nine. You couldn't have got off if you'd wanted to. I like to think you didn't want to.

I was sick the whole way. You know that. The captain told me to watch the horizon and there wasn't one, there was only fog, so I watched the place where it should have been. The one who sang said the sea was only a hill that kept moving and I should think of it as a hill. I thought of it as a hill. It didn't help.

I'll tell you what I thought about, being sick. I thought, any moment now. I thought, the fog will part and there will be the gold, the threads, going the way we're going, and I'll see it, and I'll turn to the captain and say there, the way my brother said it, and he'll look at me differently, and all of you will. I thought that every day of the crossing. Every day I was ready to be somebody else by evening.

The fog parted. There was a coast, low and green, with a white beach, and some ruins on the headland, and rain. There were the threads too, I suppose. I've decided there must have been. Because you all went very quiet, coming in, and stood at the rail looking at the same piece of sky.

I don't know what you saw. You never said. Not one of you, not once, in all the days after. I've thought about that more than anything. I think it was the kindest thing you ever did for me, and I'm not sure that it was kind.

### 4.
There was a man on the beach in a white mask.

You'll remember him. You'd have to. He stood at the top of the sand where the path started, in a red robe, with his hands folded, as if he'd been waiting for us in particular, and I suppose he had, in the way that a fisherman waits for fish in particular. He was very courteous. He called us all Tarnished, and he said the word as though he were tasting it, and then he looked at me, and he kept looking, and he said, maidenless, and smiled.

I didn't know what it meant. I know now. It's been said, on the road and at the gate, that everyone who comes over the water is met by him, and that he says the same to all of them, that there's a castle to the north where a lord sits who was born of the golden blood, and that a Tarnished with any spine will go and take it from him. It's been said that he sends everyone that way. It's been said, too, that nobody has ever seen his face, and I believe that, because he looked at me the whole time he talked and I never once felt that anyone was looking.

He talked about a table. A round one, somewhere, where the champions sit. He said it as if we'd know where somewhere was.

The captain listened to all of it and said nothing, and when the man in the mask had finished, the captain thanked him, the way you'd thank a man for directions to a place you'd already decided to go. Then we went up the path.

I've wondered since whether it was the mask that decided it. Whether if he'd said east we'd have gone east. I don't think so. I think the castle was already in you, all nine. I think you'd been told before we ever left home that a castle was what you did with a princess in a crimson cloak and no errand, and the man in the mask only said aloud what you'd been carrying. I think that's the errand nobody gave me. I think you gave it to yourselves, so that there'd be one.

It rained the whole way up. The country here is beautiful and I'd like that written down somewhere, because I don't think anyone ever bothers. The grass is very green. There are trees with gold leaves that hum, a little, the way my seed hums, and the first time I heard one I stopped in the road and the one who sang walked into the back of me. There were ruins by the gate, a great gate of grey stone across the road with two giants at it, real ones, with clubs, and soldiers in red and gold camped in the ruins with a fire going, and the captain took us round by the cliff so we didn't have to pass them. There was a hut at the fork past the gate. This hut. The captain said we'd rest.

And in the morning he said I'd wait.

### 5.
I want to be honest about the morning, because it's the part I've been least honest about, and I've only been telling it to myself.

He said I'd wait. That's true. He said the way up was steep and the castle was a castle and that they'd go and see how things stood, and send for me, and he said it kindly, with his hand on my shoulder where the clasp of the cloak was. He said milady the way he always did, as if it were my name. And the one who sang looked back from the road, twice. Nobody else did. I don't hold that against you. You had a castle in front of you. I'd have looked at the castle.

That's the kind part.

The true part is that I'd have waited anyway. If he hadn't said it. If he'd said come. I'd have stood up and put on the cloak and gone as far as the road and put my foot on it, and then I'd have come back in and sat down sideways, and you'd have gone on without me, and I'd have told myself you'd told me to. I know this. I know it the way I know the roof leaks. I've tested it since, every day, going as far as the road, and every day the road says the same thing.

So I let him say it. I let it be his kindness instead of my failing. And then I did the one thing I could think of to do, which was to take off my brooch.

You'll remember the brooch. Everyone at home would. It's the one with the ship on it, that was my mother's, and her mother's, and that says, to anyone who knows how to read a brooch, this is a person of the house, and these are her people. I'd wrapped it, on the boat, in a bit of red velvet cut from the lining of the cloak, because the sea air was bad for the silver. And I gave it to the captain and I said, so they know whose men you are. So the castle would know. So that if there was any treating to be done, you'd have the thing that gets a hearing. I thought I was giving you a door. I thought, I can't go up, but the brooch can, and the brooch is more me than I am.

He put it inside his coat, against his chest, and did the buttons over it. And he went.

I sat in the door and watched you go up the road until the road turned. Nine backs. The one who sang looked back twice. Then the road turned, and the birds went round the tooth of the castle, and it began to rain.

### 6.
I don't know how long I waited before I knew.

I've tried to work it out. I count the rains, but the rains are all one rain here. I count the nights, but I don't sleep in the ordinary way any more, I go somewhere else for a while and come back, and it doesn't feel like a night. Long enough that the food you'd left me was gone and I'd found the fruit that grows by the door, the little red ones, and the flowers with gold in the stems that taste of nothing and fill you up. Long enough that I'd stopped going to the road at every sound. Not long enough to stop going.

Nobody came down. I want that plain too. No one came down that road from the castle in all that time, not one of you, not a message, not a bird with a ribbon on it, nothing, and I sat and told myself the castle was large and things took time and the captain was thorough, and I believed it a little less each rain.

Then the soldier came.

He was one of the ones from the ruins, in the red and gold, with the helmet with the flaps, and he came down the road from the castle side with a sack over his shoulder that dripped. I heard him before I saw him. He was whistling. He saw the hut and turned in at the door, the way you'd turn in at any hut, to see what was in it, and he saw me, and he stopped.

He didn't come in. I told you. They stop at the door. He stood there with the rain running off him and the sack dripping and he looked at me in my cloak, on the floor, and he laughed, and he said, Well, milady. And I understood, from the way he said it, that he knew the word from somewhere. That someone had said it to him recently. That someone had said, milady, milady's men, milady sent us, and it had been funny.

I asked him. I don't know how I found the voice. I asked him where you were.

And he told me.

I'll tell it the way he told it, because I've kept it the way he told it, every word, the way you keep a splinter by not touching it. He said his lordship had been very pleased. He said his lordship was always very pleased to have new material, that was his word, material, and that nine at once was a gift. He said they'd been taken up to the hall where his lordship works. He said, You know what he does, don't you, milady? Up there? You've heard? And I hadn't. So he told me that too.

He said his lordship takes arms. Arms, and legs, and where the arm won't do, the head. He said his lordship puts them on himself, that he's more arms than man now, that he sits in his hall with the hands all over him and the hands all move, and it's like, he said, and he thought about it, and he said, it's like a spider, milady, if a spider were a king. He said that when his lordship takes an arm, what's left is wrapped up. Bound. Kept, in case any more of it should be wanted. He said the wrapped-up ones lie in a courtyard in a heap and they move a little, some of them, for a while. He said his lordship has a word for them.

He said, Chrysalids, milady. That's what he calls them. Little bundles that might turn into something.

Then he laughed again, and hitched up the sack, and went off down the road, whistling, and I never saw him after, and I hope, and I don't often hope things about people, I hope that the giants at the gate had him.

I've never said the lord's name. I won't. Everyone here says it, the soldiers say it, the mask said it, it's on the wind like the smell of the rain, and I've decided that's enough people saying it. I call him the spider. It's what the soldier gave me, and it's the only thing that soldier ever gave anyone that was worth having. The spider. Up in his hall, with his hands.

### 7.
Here is what I know, and I'll say it to you once, straight, the way I've never managed to say it to anyone else.

Everyone's been grafted. Everyone who came with me. You crossed the sea for me, you fought, for me, I know you fought, you'd have fought, and the spider took your arms. Your legs. Your heads, some of you, when the arms wouldn't do. He took them and stuck them to himself, and what was left of you he wrapped up and put in a heap in a courtyard in the rain, and gave a name to, as if you were something that might turn into something.

That's what I know.

And here's what I do with it, and I'd like you to hear this part, because I can't make anyone else hear it and I've tried. I laugh. I laugh, and I say things like it's quite the lark, when you think about it. I've said that aloud. I've said it to the rain and I've said it to a stranger and every time it comes out as a joke and every time I mean it as a prayer. I don't know why my mouth does that. I think something in me looked at what the soldier said and decided that it couldn't be carried whole, and so it broke it into a shape that could be carried, and the shape it chose was a joke, and now I can't get it to choose anything else. Did you know? If you're grafted by the spider, you become a chrysalid. Heh. Isn't that something. Isn't that a strange thing.

I'll tell you what I think about, in the nights that aren't nights.

I think about the courtyard. I think about the heap, and the rain on it, and which of you is on top. I think about whether the one who sang still does, under the wrappings, under his breath, the way he did in the hull. I think about whether the two who quarrelled are near each other, and whether they know it. I think about the brooch, inside the captain's coat, under the buttons, and whether the spider found it, and what a spider would want with a ship made of silver.

And I think, and this is the thing I've never told anyone, that I want to be there.

Not to rescue you. I'm not so foolish as that. To be with you. I want to be a bundle in the rain beside you. I want to be like everyone else. I want the spider to take my arms, and my legs, and if the arms won't do then the rest, and wrap what's left of me and put it on the heap, and give me the same name he gave you, and then we'd all be one thing again, the nine and me, the way we were on the boat after the point where the land was gone.

I want it so badly that I've gone as far as the road for it.

And there the road says what it always says. And I come back in. Because it's scary, you know. Having your arms cut off. Or your legs. Or your head. I want to be like everyone else and I'm just too scared, and I sit here on the floor wanting it and not doing it, which is worse than not wanting it, which is the worst thing I know about myself, and I know a great deal now.

Craven. That's the word. It's a good old word, my mother used it, and I've taken it for my own because I've nothing else that fits. I'm nothing but a craven. I say that aloud too. I say it to the rain.

There's one more thing in the hut with me, and I should tell you about it, because it's the only thing here that isn't mine and isn't the rain.

It was in the pack you left. At the bottom, in a leather bag with a drawstring, and I didn't find it for days, because I didn't look. A little bag of ash. Grey, fine, the kind that doesn't blow. I don't know which of you it belonged to. I don't know if any of you knew you had it. It's been said, in this country, that the dead don't always go where they're meant to, that some of them stay in their ash the way a tune stays in a room, and that there are people, somewhere, who can call them out of it. I don't know what to make of that. But I'll tell you what I do know, and you can make of it what you like.

It weeps.

Not so you'd hear it. Not so anyone would. But I sit with the bag in my lap, at night, and I know it the way you know there's someone in the next room who's crying and trying not to be. Something in there is small, and far from home, and very frightened, and it's holding itself together the way a child does, with both hands, and every so often it can't. I call it the little one. I don't know what else to call it. I can't get it out. I've tried. I've opened the bag and said things into it, kind things, and it doesn't come, because I haven't got whatever it is you need to have, the thing that calls. So I sit with it. That's all I can do for it. I sit with it, and it weeps, and I know, and I think that must be worth something to it, to be known, though it's not worth much.

Two of us in the hut, then, that can't leave and can't stop crying. You'd laugh. I do.

### 8.
Then the stranger came.

I heard them on the road first, the way I hear everything, and I thought, soldier, and put the little one's bag under my cloak, and then I thought, no, because the step was wrong. Soldiers walk as if the road owes them. This was someone walking as if they'd never walked this road before and wanted to be sure of it. They came to the door, and they stopped, the way everything stops, and I looked up.

I don't know how to tell you what they were. Someone in armour that had been through things. Someone tired. Someone alone, which was the first thing I saw, because I've learned to see it. They looked at me on the floor in my cloak and they didn't laugh and they didn't say milady and they didn't come in, and then, after a while, they did. They came in. First thing that has.

I'd like to say I greeted them properly. I didn't. What came out was the joke. It's always the joke first. I told them about you, all of it, the arms and the legs and the heads, the spider, the chrysalids, and I laughed in the middle of it, the little cough, and I said that thing about the lark. And they stood there and listened to the whole of it and their face didn't do what faces do. It just listened. And I thought, well. There's a person who's heard worse.

They didn't say anything. I think they don't. Or can't. Or won't, with me. It didn't matter. I've had enough of being talked at. I talked, and they let me.

So I said the other things. I said, You're all on your own, are you? And heading to Stormveil Castle? Because where else. Enticed by the one in the white mask, I suppose. And they didn't nod and they didn't shake their head and I knew anyway, I knew the mask had had them, the way it has everyone. Oh, you've come to be one with the spider? Well, that makes us two peas in a pod. I heard myself say it. Two peas. I nearly laughed again. But I don't have your courage. And I told them. I told a stranger the thing I've only ever told you. That it's scary, having your arms cut off, or your legs, or your head, that I want to be like everyone else and I'm just too scared, that I'm nothing but a craven. I said it to their face. Their face went on listening.

I think that was when I decided about the little one.

Because here's what I saw, sitting there with them standing over me. The bag under my cloak went quiet. Not quiet the way it goes when it's exhausted itself. Quiet the way a child goes when someone it trusts walks into the room. It turned toward them. I don't know how ash turns, but it did, it leaned, the whole small frightened weight of it leaned at the stranger like a plant at a window, and I understood, the way I'd understood the soldier's milady, that this was somebody the little one could go with. That they had the thing that calls. I don't know how I knew. I knew the way I know things, lately, in the next room.

So I took the bag out and I held it up and I said, Oh, I know. Can you take this little one along with you?

And I told them the truth, which is that the poor thing deserves someone braver than myself. And I said the other thing, the thing I'd seen, that the spirits look rather fondly upon them. I said it'll be glad of your company, I think, the little'un. I don't know where little'un came from. It's a word from the kitchens at home, from when I was small, and it came up out of me like a thing from a well.

They took it. They took the bag in both hands the way you'd take something that might break, and they put it away somewhere inside the armour, and I felt the little one go with them, and go quiet, properly quiet, the quiet of being carried. I'd never felt it quiet before. I nearly asked for it back.

They stayed a while after that. They didn't say anything and I'd run out of things to say, and the rain came in, and then they did something I want you to know about. They sat down. On the floor, across from me, in all that armour, and they sat sideways. Legs off to one side, weight on one hand. The way I sit. I don't think they knew they were doing it. I think they looked at me and their body decided that this was how one sat, in this hut, and did it. And we sat there, the two of us, sideways, like two people waiting for the same person to come back.

When they got up to go I asked them the thing. I'd been holding it the whole time and I got it out just as they reached the door, in a rush, so it came out wrong, the way everything does. I said, It was a pleasure to see you. Oh, can you pass on a message for me, if you see the little chrysalids in Stormveil Castle?

The little chrysalids. Listen to me. As if you were kittens.

Tell them I love them, I said. And that despite my craven heart, I'm sure I'll be joining their club soon enough. I'm finally getting the hang of this whole "pain" thing, you know. And I laughed. Heh heh.

That's what I sent you. That's the message. A joke about a club, and a lie about pain, and the word love in the middle of them like the brooch in the velvet. I heard it go out of me and I thought, that isn't it, that isn't what I meant, stop them, say it again properly, and the stranger was already on the road. And I sat there and I thought: well. It's in the message. The true thing's in there. Wrapped up in the wrong words, the way everything I own is wrapped up in something. Anyone who unwrapped it would find it.

I don't know if you can be found. I don't know if the stranger will find you, in the courtyard, in the heap, or if there's enough of any of you left to be told a thing. I don't know if being told a thing is any use to a bundle in the rain. I sent it anyway. It was what I had.

### 9.
They came back.

I hadn't thought they would. People go up that road and they don't come down it, that's the road's whole character, and I'd made my peace with the stranger being one more back going up until the road turned. But I heard the step again, the careful one, and it was coming down, and I was at the door before I knew I'd stood.

I asked them straight off. A pleasure to see you. Did you give the little chrysalids the message? That I love them? And the rest. I said the rest again. It's easier the second time.
